## The Orangery
Kind: site
About: orangery
The Orangery stands on a terrace west of the French Parterre, on the site of an earlier greenhouse that Batty Langley designed for the Duke of Kent. Thomas Philip, 2nd Earl de Grey designed it himself, with James Clephan as clerk of works. He took the idea from a plate in Jacques-François Blondel's Maisons de Plaisance (also the source for his new house) and added classical ornament and a tall French dome at each end. Historic England lists it Grade II* and calls an orangery in this French style unique in England. Dates differ: the listing says about 1835, but a Historic England research report on the Bath House (2018) says the Orangery was built in 1838 or 1839, and the 2015 earthwork survey says about 1840. The Earl's new house went up in 1834–1839, and the Historic England landscape report says the Orangery followed soon after it.

It is yellow brick, with the front and sides in cement render. The main roof is glass on a shallow cast-iron frame. Cast iron is strong in compression, so slim glazing bars block less winter light than timber would (general reasoning, not stated in the listing). It faces east over the garden on grassed terraces, with broad central steps, smaller steps to the south and a ramped path round the north side. The rear is plain brick with five arched doors into a vaulted undercroft of thirteen bays and thirteen semicircular windows above.

Heat and water: the listing says the floor "contained heating grilles", replaced by ceramic tiles after 1948, and that a cast-iron pump near the north wall was "formerly used for pumping water up from the tanks in the undercroft". English Heritage's head gardener told Albion Archaeology in 2007 that rainwater from the roof was originally held in tanks in the undercroft, and that the hydraulic ram pump from the garden's water system, taken out in the 1980s, is now stored there. No source found says how the grilles were fed (boiler, hot water or warm air), so do not guess.

The orange trees were bought from King Louis-Philippe of France. In summer they stood in tubs on the terraced parterre below, and by about 1900 they were among the largest in England. The front doors were too low for grown trees, so the north end has a full-height double door of render over timber, disguised as wall.

Inside, a stone fireplace with the de Grey arms comes from the old house. The listing dates it about 1600; Historic England's 2013 report, following the Earl's own 1831 caption, says about 1570, with the arms of Edmund Grey, 1st Earl of Kent. Bedfordshire Archives says it was only installed in the Orangery in the twentieth century. On the steps are four early-1700s stone cherubs, probably by an unknown Flemish sculptor, holding a book, a puppy, a bird's nest and a torch. The Orangery was conserved and repainted in 2011.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1158549; Bedfordshire Archives, Buildings at Wrest Park; Historic England Research Report 6/2013 (landscape investigations); Historic England Research Report 74/2018 (Bath House); Historic England Research Report 84/2015; Albion Archaeology, Old Park Weir (2007); English Heritage, Revitalising Wrest Park.

## Louis-Philippe and Queen Maria Amalia
Kind: reference
About: louis-philippe, orangery
Louis-Philippe I was King of the French from 1830 to 1848. He came to the throne after the July Revolution of 1830 and styled himself a "citizen king". He was overthrown in the revolution of February 1848, fled to England, landing at Newhaven, and Queen Victoria let the family live at Claremont House in Surrey, where he died in 1850.

His queen, Maria Amalia (married 1809 in Palermo), was the daughter of Ferdinand I of the Two Sicilies and Maria Carolina of Austria. Maria Carolina was a Habsburg archduchess and a sister of Marie Antoinette, so Marie Antoinette was Maria Amalia's aunt. Both sisters were daughters of the Habsburg Empress Maria Theresa.
Sources: Wikipedia, Maria Amalia of Naples and Sicily; general knowledge.

## Thomas Philip, 2nd Earl de Grey
Kind: person
About: earl-de-grey, house, admiralty, riba, ireland
Thomas Philip de Grey, 2nd Earl de Grey (born 8 December 1781, died 14 November 1859), built the present house, the Orangery and the French gardens at Wrest. He was born Thomas Philip Robinson, was known as Lord Grantham from 1786, took the surname Weddell in 1803 when he inherited Newby Hall in Yorkshire from a cousin, and changed it to de Grey in 1833.

In 1833 he inherited Wrest Park, the earldom de Grey and the barony of Lucas from his aunt Amabel, the daughter of Jemima, Marchioness Grey. He found a large, crumbling house of medieval origins and, rather than repair it, pulled it down and built a new one about 200 metres to the north.

He was a Tory politician: First Lord of the Admiralty under Robert Peel from December 1834 to April 1835, Lord Lieutenant of Ireland from 1841 to 1844, and Lord Lieutenant of Bedfordshire from 1818 until his death. He was a keen amateur architect and became the first president of the Institute of British Architects when it was founded in 1834, holding the post until he died; it received its royal charter in 1837 and is today's RIBA. He was also a Fellow of the Royal Society (1841) and commanded the Yorkshire Hussars, a yeomanry cavalry regiment, for over forty years. Of the house he wrote: "I was strictly and in every sense of the word my own architect." Wrest passed to his elder daughter, Anne Florence, Countess Cowper.
Sources: Wikipedia, Thomas de Grey, 2nd Earl de Grey; English Heritage, History of Wrest Park; Historic England list entry 1311484.

## The house
Kind: site
About: house
The house was built in 1834–1839 to the Earl's own design, with James Clephan as clerk of works. It is a near-unique example in England of a whole country house designed and decorated, inside and out, as an 18th-century French château in the Louis XV style. The garden front copies a design in Blondel's Maisons de Plaisance (1737); the entrance front is modelled on the Hôtel de Matignon in Paris. It is built of Bath stone with a mansard roof, the steep double-pitched French roof that hides an attic storey.

The Earl moved the house about 200 metres north of the old one but kept it exactly in line with the Long Water and the Archer Pavilion, so the main axis of the garden runs from the front door to the Pavilion, about 800 metres due south. In 1968 Pevsner wrongly credited the house to an "unknown French architect" called Cléphane; Clephan was in fact the Earl's clerk of works.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1311484; English Heritage, History of Wrest Park; Wikipedia, Wrest Park.

## The Archer Pavilion
Kind: site
About: pavilion, pavilion-paint, pavilion-roof-repair
The Archer Pavilion was designed by Thomas Archer and built in 1709–1711 for Henry Grey, then Marquess of Kent, who became Duke of Kent in 1710 while it was going up. It cost £1,809. Historic England lists it Grade I and calls it "a miniature masterpiece in the English Baroque style". It stands at the south end of the Long Water on the house's axis, closing the view and giving a view back.

Plan: borrowed from Borromini's Sant'Ivo in Rome, two interlocking triangles making a six-pointed star. Archer made three points semicircular and three rectangular. A central circular block carries a lead-covered dome with an Ionic lantern, an iron balustrade and a ball finial. The building is red and yellow brick on a base of channelled red brick, with tiny semi-domes where the curved wings meet the drum, and round "bull's-eye" windows. Brick walls in the audited area were about 50 centimetres thick. The exterior was originally rendered; it was later stripped back to the brick.

The Great Room is circular and open to the dome. Hauduroy painted it in 1712, probably in oil on plaster, and signed "Hauduroy pinxit 1712". It shows a coffered dome, Corinthian columns, urns, statues and Grey badges. The listing calls him Louis Hauduroy; English Heritage says Mark Anthony. He is one of two known surviving English schemes by him. The painting hides doors to spiral stairs: up to servants' bedrooms and down to a brick kitchen and a double privy. The two ground-floor closets served as bedrooms.

Roof: the heavy lead slowly slipped down the dome, letting rain into the roof void and damaging the lath-and-plaster ceiling beneath the painting. English Heritage repaired the dome in 2016–2017, with the lead due for replacement, and a bat roost in the dome needed a licence. A crack in the dome was treated in 1952. The 1990s audit found the lantern's timber and canvas in poor state.

Setting: it stands at the south end of an earth causeway running from a dam. A 1716 account mentions matting and "double deals for closets" for a "new building below the gardens", probably this. The "goose foot" of paths round it was restored in 2011.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1113807; Ancient Monuments Laboratory Report 28/97; Historic England Research Report 6/2013; FPCR Environment, Archer Pavilion at Wrest Park; English Heritage, Revitalising Wrest Park; English Heritage, History of Wrest Park.

## Thomas Archer, architect
Kind: person
About: thomas-archer
Thomas Archer (about 1668–1743) was the youngest son of a Warwickshire gentry family. He went to Trinity College, Oxford, in 1686, then spent four years abroad in the early 1690s, including Italy, where he saw the Roman Baroque of Bernini and Borromini at first hand. Historic England calls him "the only architect to bring a direct experience of European Baroque to England".

He was an amateur: from 1705 he held the court post of Groom Porter, which licensed gambling, and from 1715 he was comptroller of customs at Newcastle. Most of his commissions came from the aristocracy. He helped the 1st Duke of Devonshire at Chatsworth, giving the house its curved north front and probably building the domed cascade-house, and worked on Heythrop in Oxfordshire. Other works are St Philip's in Birmingham (1708–1715), St Paul's Deptford (1712–1730) and St John's, Smith Square, London (1713–1728).

At Wrest he also designed Cain Hill House (about 1712–1715, now demolished) for the Duke of Kent, but the Pavilion is the part that survives.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1113807 (education, travels, Chatsworth, Heythrop, Cain Hill House); Wikipedia, Thomas Archer (Groom Porter, comptroller, church dates).

## Henry Grey, Duke of Kent
Kind: person
About: duke-of-kent, great-garden
Henry Grey (1671–1740) made Wrest's great formal garden. His parents, Anthony, 11th Earl of Kent, and Mary, Baroness Lucas, had made the Long Water in the 1680s, and he inherited in 1702. He became Marquess of Kent in 1706, and in 1710 was made Duke of Kent in exchange for giving up his post as Lord Chamberlain, which he had held since 1704. He was made a Knight of the Garter in 1712, and later served as Lord Steward (1716–1718), Lord Privy Seal (1719–1720) and Lord Lieutenant of Bedfordshire (1711–1740).

Contemporaries thought him lacking in talent, and he was unpopular: he was nicknamed "Bug", reputedly because of his body odour. He was a loyal servant of the Protestant succession and the new Hanoverian kings. Between 1706 and the 1720s he laid out the Great Garden, a rare English example of a French-style formal woodland garden, employing Nicholas Hawksmoor, Thomas Archer, Batty Langley and William Kent.

None of his sons outlived him. Shortly before he died he was made Marquess Grey, with the title passing to his granddaughter Jemima Campbell, who inherited Wrest. Another grandson, through his daughter Anne and Lord Charles Cavendish, was the scientist Henry Cavendish (1731–1810), who discovered hydrogen and in 1797–1798 "weighed the Earth" by measuring the tiny gravitational pull between lead spheres.
Sources: Wikipedia, Henry Grey, 1st Duke of Kent; English Heritage, History of Wrest Park; Historic England register entry 1000113.

## The statue of William III
Kind: site
About: william-statue
The lead statue of King William III stands about 30 metres north of the Archer Pavilion, on the garden's main axis. It is thought to be by John Cheere (1709–1787), a leading maker of lead garden statues. It is believed to date from the 1730s, probably after 1737, since it does not appear in John Rocque's 1737 views of the garden.

William is shown as a Roman emperor: in armour, crowned with a victor's laurel wreath, holding a baton, with a cloak. The straps of his armour alternate thistles and roses, for Scotland and England. The pedestal is of Ketton stone on a Portland stone plinth, with a white marble panel reading: "KING WILLIAM the 3 of GLORIOUS and Immortell Memory". The wording echoes the toast to William's "glorious and immortal memory" drunk by his supporters, a way of declaring loyalty to the Protestant settlement. It is listed Grade II*.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1158878; general knowledge for the toast.

## William III and the Glorious Revolution
Kind: reference
About: william-iii, glorious-revolution, william-statue
William of Orange (born 1650) was the Dutch stadtholder and husband of Mary, the Protestant daughter of the Catholic King James II. When James's son was born in June 1688, raising the prospect of a Catholic dynasty, leading English politicians invited William to intervene. He landed at Torbay on 5 November 1688 with a large army and James fled to France. In 1689 Parliament offered the crown to William and Mary jointly, and the Bill of Rights limited royal power and barred Catholics from the throne. This largely bloodless change in England became known as the Glorious Revolution, though it was fiercely fought in Ireland, where William won the Battle of the Boyne in 1690.

Mary died in 1694 and William reigned alone until he died in March 1702, after his horse stumbled on a molehill at Hampton Court; his opponents, the Jacobites, toasted "the little gentleman in the black velvet waistcoat". Abroad, he led the alliance against Louis XIV of France, including the Grand Alliance of 1689 with the Habsburg Emperor Leopold I.

The Act of Settlement of 1701 passed the crown, after Anne, to the Protestant House of Hanover, and George I succeeded in 1714. Jacobites, supporters of James II's descendants, rose in 1715 and again in 1745. So in the 1730s a statue of William was a statement of loyalty to the Protestant succession and the Hanoverian kings.
Sources: general knowledge.

## The boundary canals ("the river")
Kind: site
About: boundary-canals, brown-river
The water round the west, south and east edges of the old garden, which visitors often call the river, is a chain of canals: Old Park Water on the west, the Serpentine to the south-west and the Broad Water on the south and east. It began as straight, formal canals dug for Henry Grey, Duke of Kent, as part of his Great Garden, and Albion Archaeology says most took their shape between 1702 and 1720. The canals were altered in the 1720s, and a serpentine canal was made in 1729. To the south, the garden ended at the Old Brook, still the boundary between the parishes of Silsoe and Gravenhurst.

In 1758–1760 Jemima, Marchioness Grey, and her husband Philip Yorke brought in Lancelot "Capability" Brown. Jemima valued her grandfather's garden, so his changes were limited. Jemima's letters describe the job as laying "the waters together around the garden", so the canals read as one stream winding among planting. Where levels differed more than digging could cure, a bank of earth was needed, which planting was to conceal. That is why the "river" curves while the Long Water stays straight.

A column about 6 metres tall records the work: see the section on the Capability Brown column for its inscription, finial and the dispute over its date and moves. The Bath House (about 1769–1771, by Stevens, for Jemima) stands on the west bank of the Bath House Water, an arm of the system fed by a spring, with the Cascade at its north end.

Everything holds water because of the clay: the banks are puddled clay, and the levels depend on weirs, sluices and culverts (see "How Wrest's water works"). Old Park Water is the highest of the chain, and the others are supplied from it.
Sources: Historic England register entry 1000113; list entries 1158972 and 1113790; Albion Archaeology, Old Park Weir (2007), quoting Bedfordshire Archives CRT 190/45; English Heritage, History of Wrest Park.

## Lancelot "Capability" Brown
Kind: person
About: capability-brown, brown-river
Lancelot Brown (1716–1783) was the most famous English landscape designer of the 18th century. Born at Kirkharle in Northumberland, he became head gardener at Stowe in 1741, then worked all over England, reshaping more than 170 parks. His nickname came from telling clients their land had "capability" for improvement. His style swept away straight lines for rolling grass, clumps and belts of trees, and serpentine lakes made by damming streams, as at Blenheim. In 1764 he became the King's Master Gardener at Hampton Court.

At Wrest he was held back, which is why the formal garden survives. He worked there in 1758–1760, brought in by Jemima or her husband Philip, who also employed him at Wimpole. His brief was to curve and join the boundary canals, open views and plant. He visited briefly again in 1778 and 1779. No drawings survive, so no one knows exactly what is his. Some of his drainage schemes elsewhere did not work, and Historic England wonders if the drains at Wrest were the same. Historic England does not think he designed the Bath House.

Earth-moving in his day was done by hand: gangs of labourers with spades and wheelbarrows, with horses and carts for the soil.
Sources: Historic England Research Report 74/2018; Historic England list entry 1158972; Research Report 6/2013; general knowledge (Brown's career).

## Jemima, Marchioness Grey
Kind: person
About: jemima, bath-house
Jemima Campbell (1722–1797; some sources give 1723 for her birth) was the Duke of Kent's granddaughter and heir, the daughter of his daughter Amabel and John Campbell, later Earl of Breadalbane. She inherited Wrest when the Duke died in 1740, and that year married Philip Yorke, son of the Lord Chancellor, Lord Hardwicke; Philip became the 2nd Earl of Hardwicke in 1764. Both were keen on the gardens. Jemima kept her grandfather's formal garden but softened its edges with Capability Brown and added garden buildings: the Bath House, a Chinese temple and bridge, and an altar. Wrest later passed to her daughter Amabel, and then to Amabel's nephew, the 2nd Earl de Grey.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1113790; English Heritage, History of Wrest Park; general knowledge.

## The de Grey family and Wrest
Kind: site
About: grey-family, grey-first-record, kent-earldom, wrest-sale, edmund-grey, amabel-de-grey, lord-lucas
The Greys and their heirs held Wrest for more than 600 years, from the 1280s until the estate was sold in 1917. The Victoria County History of 1908 says the manor "has been in the possession of the De Greys, earls of Kent, and their descendants for over six hundred years". The first record is of Reginald de Grey, who held a fee in Flitton and Silsoe in 1284–1286. Edward IV made Edmund Grey Earl of Kent in 1465.

The family had a hard time in the early 1500s. Richard, 3rd Earl, gambled and borrowed, and Wrest was sold to Sir Henry Wyatt in 1507. His half-brother Henry bought it back in 1512 (Bedfordshire Archives also gives a deed of 1514) but was too poor to use the earldom. His grandson Reginald was recognised as Earl again in 1572.

In the early 1680s Anthony, 11th Earl, his wife Mary, Baroness Lucas, and his mother Amabel made the Long Water. Their son Henry became Duke of Kent in 1710 and made the great formal garden. When he died in 1740 his Kent titles died with him, and Wrest passed to his granddaughter Jemima, Marchioness Grey. Her elder daughter Amabel, made Countess de Grey in 1816, left it in 1833 to her nephew Thomas, 2nd Earl de Grey, who pulled down the old house and built the present one in 1834–1839.

After Thomas, Wrest went to his daughter Anne Florence, Countess Cowper (1859), then to her son Francis, 7th Earl Cowper (1880), then to Francis's nephew Auberon Herbert, 9th Baron Lucas (1905), who let it to the American ambassador Whitelaw Reid. Lucas, a Royal Flying Corps pilot, was killed in November 1916. His sister Nan, who had run the wartime hospital in the house, sold the estate in 1917 to John George Murray of Thirsk, a brewery and coal company chairman. Bedfordshire Archives says it was a private sale after an auction had been advertised.

Fuller sections follow the story in order: the medieval Greys, losing and regaining Wrest, the Earls of Kent, the heiresses, the Cowpers and Lucases, the hospital and fire, the 1917 sale, and after the family.
Sources: Victoria County History, Bedford vol. 2 (1908), Flitton cum Silsoe; Bedfordshire Archives, The Manor of Wrest, The Earls of Kent, Wrest Park in the 20th Century; Cambridge Core abstract of Inventories of Bedfordshire Country Houses 1714–1830, Wrest Park chapter; English Heritage, History of Wrest Park and Wrest Park at War.

## The Conservatory
Kind: site
About: conservatory
The conservatory is a glass-roofed room at the west end of Wrest Park house, built with the house in 1834–1839 for Thomas Philip, 2nd Earl de Grey. Historic England's listing calls it an "elegant conservatory which has a glazed roof supported by ornate cast-iron trusses". It sits in one of the single-storey end pavilions of the house, and it closes the west enfilade, the line of state rooms that runs through the house. The room just before it is the Countess's sitting room, "more feminine" than the rest, with delicate plasterwork and putti hanging from the ceiling, and the conservatory opens straight off it.

From the conservatory a door leads out to the Italian Garden to the west. English Heritage says that visitors can look through the conservatory to the Italian Garden and the walled garden beyond, just as Henrietta, Countess de Grey did when the house was new in the 1830s. That view, from sitting room through glass to a formal garden, was part of the design.

How it works as a structure. Cast iron is strong when it is squeezed (compression) but brittle when it is stretched (tension). Builders of the 1830s therefore shaped cast-iron roof members as arches or triangulated trusses, so that most of the load goes into compression, and often used wrought iron for the stretched parts such as tie rods. The result is a roof of slim iron ribs with large panes of glass between them, far lighter than stone or timber. The listing says the trusses are cast iron and ornate; no source found names the iron founder or describes the glazing in detail.

Where it fits in history. Iron-and-glass building was new and fashionable: Joseph Paxton's Great Conservatory at Chatsworth was built in 1836–1840, and the Crystal Palace followed in 1851, about twelve to seventeen years after Wrest's conservatory. Glass was still taxed by weight until the duty was abolished in 1845, which kept large glass roofs expensive.

Heating. No source opened for this guide says how the conservatory was heated. Plant houses of this date were usually warmed by hot-water pipes or flues fed from a boiler outside, but that is general practice, not evidence about Wrest. The Earl wrote his own account of building the house, printed in the Bedfordshire Historical Record Society's Miscellanea (2024), which may answer it.

Recent history. English Heritage planned in 2008 to restore the conservatory and the Countess's sitting room, and says the conservatory was fully repaired and planted up as part of the 2011 restoration. Recent photographs show it planted with palms and other tender plants. The visitor entrance and lift are at this end of the house, and pushchairs stay outside.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1311484 (house and service block); Historic England list entry 1321790 (Italian Garden); Heritage Fund, "Grand garden revival at Grade I registered Wrest Park"; English Heritage, "Revitalising Wrest Park"; Cambridge University Press listing for "Earl de Grey's account of the building of Wrest House" (Miscellanea, 2024; text not read); general knowledge for cast iron, Chatsworth, the Crystal Palace and the glass duty (verify).

## The Italian Garden
Kind: site
About: italian-garden
The Italian Garden is a formal, square garden on the west side of the house, beside the conservatory. Historic England's listing of its structures dates it to about 1839, in the first years after the Earl built the new house, and says the statue of Ceres was probably introduced then. It is a Grade II listing; the garden itself lies within the Grade I registered park.

The layout. Gravel paths divide the garden into four quarters. Moulded stone curbs outline geometric beds around a circular central bed. There are three entrances: from the conservatory on the east side, and from the garden through gates on the west and south. The west gate is wrought iron, hung on two stone piers topped with vases; the south entrance is flanked by similar vases on plinths with carved panels. "Italian" here means formal and symmetrical. English Heritage describes the gardens' bedding as "fancy French curves and bold Italian geometry".

The statue. At the north end of the path, opposite the south entrance, stands Ceres, the Roman goddess of harvests and the earth's abundance. She holds flowers in one hand and rests the other on an overflowing cornucopia, on a moulded stone pedestal decorated with a scale pattern. Some photo captions call her Pomona, the goddess of fruit; the listing says Ceres. Bedfordshire Archives describes the same figure only as a mid-nineteenth-century marble woman with a large cornucopia of fruit and flowers.

What has been lost. The listing says four figure groups once stood in the quarters and that vases flanked the entrances. The groups have been removed and the vases replaced with plainer ones.

When was it laid out? Sources disagree. The listing says the garden was laid out or modified about 1839. A Heritage Fund news page says its layout "dates from 1882", while English Heritage's own conservation page says the beds were restored to their early-nineteenth-century design. I would give 1839 as the listing date and treat 1882 as a possible later alteration, unconfirmed.

Restoration. English Heritage restored the garden in 2011, after detailed research and archaeological survey. Broken stone edging was conserved and re-laid, and low-maintenance foliage planting was replaced by seasonal bedding such as tulips, wallflowers, hyacinths, dahlias and pelargoniums. Because the garden lies beside the conservatory and the walled garden, the view through the glass from the house is the best way to understand it.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1321790 (Italian Garden structures); Bedfordshire Archives, Statues at Wrest Park; Heritage Fund, "Grand garden revival at Grade I registered Wrest Park"; English Heritage, "Revitalising Wrest Park"; English Heritage, seasonal garden highlights page.

## The French Parterre
Kind: site
About: parterre, parterre-restored, mermaid-basin
The French Parterre is the formal garden of low box-edged beds below the terrace on the south front of the house. English Heritage says the upper gardens laid out in the 1830s between the new house and the earlier gardens were created in the French style, and Parks & Gardens UK describes a "formal 1830s parterre" on the south garden front. It was laid out for Thomas Philip, 2nd Earl de Grey to go with his French-style house (built 1834–1839).

A parterre is meant to be read as a pattern from above, so the best view is from the terrace or the house windows. The central gravel walk runs south from the house along the main axis toward the Long Water and the Archer Pavilion. Wooden Versailles planters, with Portuguese laurels alternating with pencil junipers, stand along it, and four lead statue groups flank the walk (see the section on the lead groups). English Heritage has also planted an avenue of 26 disease-resistant elms to replace the English elms lost to disease that once framed the parterre.

Restoration. Much of the intricate detail was lost in the early twentieth century, when the parterre was simplified to make it easier to maintain. English Heritage carried out a full-area archaeological excavation, which revealed the original beds and gravel layout, and restored the parterre in 2012 to reflect the Earl's design. Investigation took place in autumn 2011 (Heritage Fund).

The Mermaid Basin. On the west side of the parterre stands the Mermaid Basin, Grade II listed. It is Italian veined Carrara marble on a stone base, about 1.5 metres high. Four seated mermaids (or nereids) with double tails curl either side of a central pier decorated with rockwork, and each raises a hand to support a large fountain bowl, which is now missing; Bedfordshire Archives says the original broad basin has been replaced by a small one. Sources disagree on its date. Historic England thinks it is late nineteenth century, probably put up by Francis, 7th Earl Cowper (1834–1905), who inherited in 1880: the only evidence is a Country Life article of 9 July 1904 that mentions it, so it was acquired between 1880 and 1904. Bedfordshire Archives (2011) says "mid 19th century", probably by the 2nd Earl de Grey. Historic England's is the later, better-argued dating, but neither is certain.

French style at Wrest. The Earl's taste for the French style began before the house: his entrance lodges at Silsoe of about 1826 were his first Louis XV buildings. The parterre completes the idea: a French château with a French garden.
Sources: English Heritage, History of Wrest Park; English Heritage, "Revitalising Wrest Park"; Heritage Fund, "Grand garden revival at Grade I registered Wrest Park"; Parks & Gardens UK, Wrest Park; Historic England list entry 1158506 (Mermaid Basin, read via British Listed Buildings); Bedfordshire Archives, Monuments and Ornaments at Wrest Park; Bedfordshire Archives, Buildings at Wrest Park.

## The four lead statue groups on the parterre
Kind: site
About: parterre-statues, parterre-statues-restored, john-cheere
Four large lead statue groups flank the central walk of the French Parterre, two on each side. Historic England lists them together at Grade II* as "Four Statuary Groups in the South Parterre", dated about 1730 and attributed to John Cheere (1709–1787), the prolific London statue maker with a yard at Hyde Park Corner. Each group is about 3 metres high, in painted lead on splayed Portland stone bases that are probably nineteenth century.

The four groups. Venus and Adonis stands about 25 metres south-east of the house, with a seated Venus, Cupid releasing a dove (a symbol of departing love) and Adonis ready to leave with his hunting dog. Meleager and Atalanta stands about 25 metres south-west, with a standing Meleager holding a shepherd's crook and a seated Atalanta, a dog and a boar's head under the dog's paw. Aeneas and Anchises stands about 50 metres south-east: Aeneas strides out carrying his old father Anchises on his shoulder, with his son Ascanius beside him and a fallen woman at his feet, the escape from Troy in Virgil's Aeneid. The Abduction of Helen of Troy stands about 50 metres south-west: a striding soldier carries off a struggling woman, with a crouched man and a child, loosely derived from a work by the French sculptor Philippe Bertrand.

Naming muddle. Bedfordshire Archives calls the Meleager group "Diana and Endymion" and the Abduction of Helen "the Rape of the Sabine Women". Historic England's 2012 entry says the Meleager group was previously misidentified as Diana and Endymion. Some photo captions on Wikimedia Commons follow the older names, and a couple of them appear to be swapped between statues.

Where they came from. The listing says they are believed to come from Cheere's surviving stock, dispersed at the end of the eighteenth century, and passed through dealers or collectors before the Earl bought them. A view of 1839 shows them in their present positions. English Heritage's collection page says Venus and Adonis is "late 18th century" and was brought to Wrest about 1839, which differs from the listing's about 1730. English Heritage says many of Wrest's lead statues were melted down in 1809 by Amabel, Countess de Grey, so these groups are later arrivals.

Why white? Cheere worked in lead painted white to look like marble from a distance, because imported marble weathered badly in Britain.

Restoration. In April 2021 English Heritage announced the first phase of a wider project to restore the parterre statues. Conservators from Skillington Workshop removed the peeling paint with high-temperature steam, which does not damage the surface beneath, then repainted the figures to recreate the marble effect. Venus and Adonis and Aeneas and Anchises, both over 3 metres tall, were done first. Wrest has more than 70 statues and monuments across its 90 acres.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1113789 (Four Statuary Groups, South Parterre); English Heritage press release, 22 April 2021 (statue restoration); English Heritage, Wrest Park collection highlights; Bedfordshire Archives, Statues at Wrest Park.

## John Cheere, statue maker
Kind: person
About: john-cheere
John Cheere (1709–1787) was a London sculptor whose workshop at Hyde Park Corner made garden and interior statues in lead and plaster, mostly copies of classical and Renaissance works, for country houses across England. English Heritage describes him as a major manufacturer of lead statues in eighteenth-century London. His figures were usually painted white to imitate marble, though his sculpture yard also made coloured rustic pieces: English Heritage's collection includes a Cheere lead "Columbine" painted in bright, lifelike colours, with traces of paint left in the nostrils and mouth.

At Wrest, the four lead groups on the French Parterre are attributed to him by Historic England, which dates them about 1730 and says they probably came from his stock after it was dispersed at the end of the eighteenth century. The statue of William III north of the Archer Pavilion is also thought to be by Cheere. English Heritage's collection page says a Venus and Adonis may have been part of the stock auctioned after his death in 1787 and brought to Wrest about 1839. The Earl de Grey, who built the new house in 1834–1839, bought the groups for his parterre.

His yard worked like a garden-sculpture shop: an owner could pick a classical figure from stock, which made statues affordable (general knowledge, verify). Painted lead needs repainting from time to time: in 2021 conservators steamed off flaking paint from two of the Wrest groups and repainted them.

## The Dairy Sculpture Gallery
Kind: site
About: dairy, dairy-gallery
The ornamental dairy is a small building at the south side of the service range, to the east of the house, projecting at right angles from a garden wall. Historic England's listing of the house and service block (built 1834–1839 for the 2nd Earl de Grey) describes it as an "ornamental dairy, unfortunately much altered", with a verandah, stained glass windows and a cupola. The listing says its internal fittings have not survived. In 1982 an extension was built that incorporated the dairy by knocking through its north wall.

The dairy once supplied butter and cream to the de Grey family. Ornamental dairies were a fashion of country houses, places where the work of the dairy was done in a pretty building, cool in summer, and where the family could show visitors the process; the one at Wrest was designed to match the French-style house. (The fashion is general knowledge, not a Wrest-specific source.) English Heritage's conservation page says the ornamental dairy was restored and used as a sculpture gallery, listed under its 2012 work.

Today it is the Dairy Sculpture Gallery, where garden statues too fragile to stay outdoors are displayed. English Heritage names a Portland stone Neptune and a bronze sundial dated 1682 among the highlights. The listing for the sundial in the gardens also dates it to 1682, so the two may be related, but no source found says so.

Other sculpture at Wrest. English Heritage says the gardens and the dairy together hold a remarkable collection of eighteenth- and nineteenth-century statues. Highlights on its collection page include Atlas, a Cheere lead Columbine, a lead Diana of the 1720s (bought by English Heritage at auction in 2011 with Art Fund help, and put back in the garden at the head of Ladies Lake), a Hawking Party of 1839–1857 whose horse was carved in 1839 for the stable block entrance, and "A Nymph Going to the Bath" of 1837, bought by the Earl, who commissioned a companion piece from Richard James Wyatt. The pair flanked the entrance to the Ante-Library. The page does not say which of these is in the dairy.

The dairy stands close to the Petit Trianon playhouse and the old stable block, east of the house.

## Le Petit Trianon playhouse
Kind: site
About: petit-trianon
Le Petit Trianon, also called the Wendy House, is a small log playhouse near the house and the old stable block, built in 1856. Historic England listed it at Grade II in 2012 (list entry 1402866). It is a single-storey structure of split logs on a timber frame, standing on a low brick wall, with a tiled roof and a brick chimney. The gable ends have decorative split-log work and plain carved bargeboards. Inside are three rooms, which were described in 1885 as a kitchen, a scullery and a sitting room, with tongue-and-groove panelling, tiled floors, a brick-surrounded fireplace with a basket grate, and a panelled wall cupboard. Old records mention a sundial and a workshop, but the listing found no evidence of them. The roof and much of the split-log work were replaced in the late twentieth century after a tree fell on the building. Parks & Gardens UK records it as a three-roomed log cabin dated 1856.

Who was it for? The listing says it was built "for his children as a playhouse or summerhouse", following the fashion for rustic wooden buildings in gardens. The "he" is Thomas Philip, 2nd Earl de Grey (born 1781). In 1856 he was 74, and his two daughters were long grown up: his elder daughter, Anne Florence, was the mother of Francis Cowper, born in 1834. So the listing's "his children" is puzzling: grandchildren would fit better. No source found settles it, and the grandchildren idea is an inference, not a fact.

The name. It is named after the Petit Trianon at Versailles, the small château Louis XVI gave to Marie Antoinette, whose grounds held a make-believe rustic hamlet (see the Versailles section). No source found says why the Earl chose the name, or that the log house copies the hamlet: the name is the link. It fits his French taste: the house copies 18th-century French designs, and his Silsoe entrance lodges of about 1826 were his first Louis XV-style buildings.

Nearby, an "American Garden" was laid out in 1857, according to the listing.

## The old house of Wrest
Kind: site
About: old-house, north-front, old-house-demolished, carew-poem
The house the Greys lived in for about 550 years stood in the southern part of the South Lawns, close to the fountain and more than 200 metres south of today's mansion. Nothing stands above ground, but its walls lie just under the turf. They show as brown parchmarks in dry summers: archaeologists noticed them in 1988 and photographed them from the air in 1994.

It began as a manor house. When Reginald de Grey died in 1307–1308 the papers listed a "capital messuage with a dovecote" at Silsoe, meaning a manor house with its outbuildings, and by 1512 it was moated. Each generation then added rooms, so that the 1573 inventory lists a chapel, a "clock chamber" and many named bedchambers. Bedfordshire Archives says it grew "piecemeal with no attempt at symmetry".

In spring 1639 the poet Thomas Carew stayed here with Henry, 8th Earl of Kent, and his wife Elizabeth Talbot, and wrote "To my friend G.N. from Wrest". He praises the plain buildings and the generous welcome, and describes streams that "twice surround this island Mansion". The 2013 English Heritage survey calls it the first reference to a designed garden at Wrest, though it says the meaning is far from clear.

The classical north front came next. Anthony, 11th Earl, married Mary Lucas in 1663. Her father died in July 1671, leaving her a large estate, and the money paid for much of the new front, built "to disguise its irregularities". Sources date it differently: 1671 (Historic England listing), 1672 (Bedfordshire Archives), 1672–1676 (History of Parliament) and "the 1670s" (the 2013 survey). An undated agreement describes a building 212 feet long with a cupola. Amabel, the Earl's mother, kept the accounts: £3,227, over a quarter above the estimate. The architect is unknown.

Henry, Duke of Kent, considered rebuilding the whole house with plans from Giacomo Leoni, but nothing was built, so the old house survived another hundred years. Jemima added a dining room in 1760, and in 1791 both fronts were refaced with sash windows; John Woolfe redesigned the north front about 1790.

Thomas, 2nd Earl de Grey, inherited in 1833 and demolished the house, building the present one 200 metres north in 1834–1839. Demolition ran alongside the building: watercolours show parts standing in 1838, and Bedfordshire Archives gives 1834 to 1840. The survivors are the clock tower, moved to the new service yard, and a chimneypiece of about 1570 to 1600 now in the Orangery. It carries the arms of Edmund Grey, 1st Earl, and the motto "Foy est tout". The Earl's 1831 sketchbook records the old house just before it fell.

## The fountain and its eight statues
Kind: site
About: wrest-fountain
The round pond with its marble fountain stands about 200 metres south of the present house, where the path down the Long Water crosses the path from the Orangery. It is Grade II listed. A wide, shallow bowl of white Carrara marble sits on a large square base. Beneath it four seated young women each hold something to do with water: a fishing net with a basket of fish (north), a dolphin (south), a goose (east) and an oar (west). The pond wall and the eight pedestals round it are Ketton stone.

The eight marble statues stand on pedestals of the early 1700s, but the figures are early to mid nineteenth century. Their names have changed. Bedfordshire Archives names them Venus, Cato, Iris, the Tragic Muse, the Comic Muse, Flora, Pandora and Ceres. The Historic England listing, revised in 2012, reads them as Psyche and Venus (both after Canova), Ceres (a copy of an antique), two figures of Spring, Clytemnestra, Aeschines, and a figure it calls Tragic Comedy.

The date is disputed. Bedfordshire Archives says "probably built about 1840 for the 2nd Earl de Grey". The listing says the fountain was one of the pieces acquired by Anne Florence, Countess Cowper, after she inherited in 1859, and quotes a 1904 Country Life article that the statues "formerly stood at more distant points". The 2013 English Heritage survey says only that the circular fountain was installed "at some time", possibly by Lady Cowper. It was not the first fountain here. From 1686 Amabel and Anthony's formal garden had circular pools with fountains in the panels south of the house, and by about 1720 these had been filled in and replaced with statues.

The listing says the fountain "was fed by cisterns at the top of the house". That is gravity feed: water in a tank high in the house pushes down the pipe, and every 10 metres of height gives about 1 bar of pressure, so a jet of a few metres needs only a modest head. The present house, built in 1834–1839, stands about 200 metres away. The service yard also has a horse-driven pump house beside a well and a fire-engine house, both of 1834–1839; the listing does not say they filled the cisterns. In 2006 the water pipe to the fountain was replaced, and English Heritage's restoration plan was to run the fountain from an electric pump.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1113791; Bedfordshire Archives, Monuments and Ornaments at Wrest Park and Statues at Wrest Park; Historic England list entry 1403022; English Heritage Research Report 6-2013, Wrest Park Landscape Investigations.

## The sundial and the six statues
Kind: site
About: sundial, sundial-statues, henry-wynne
The sundial stands about 270 metres south of the house, in a D-shaped lawn south of the Broadwalk. It is a large brass dial plate on an octagonal marble pedestal carved with acanthus leaves and flower swags tied with ribbons. The pedestal is listed Grade II as late seventeenth century, and Bedfordshire Archives measures it at about three feet high.

The dial plate is dated 1682 and signed Henricus Wynne. Henry Wynne was a London instrument maker (the listing gives his death as 1709) and one of the best makers of "double horizontal" dials of the Restoration. The listing calls him "the architect Henry Wynne", which looks like a mistake. He made a similar dial for Windsor Castle in 1680, carrying the monogram of Charles II. The Wrest dial is about 777 millimetres across and is marked in one-minute steps from 4 a.m. to 8 p.m. It also has sixteen rings giving the latitude and longitude of places, the names and positions of twelve stars, and tables for the Moon's age.

Today's plate is a replica: the listing calls it fibreglass, the Sundial Society calls it an electrotype in bronze resin. The original was taken to Coles Park by John Murray, who bought Wrest in 1917; the Ministry bought it back after 1954. It then spent years at English Heritage's London office and returned in 2024, and is now kept at the Dairy. The original blade bore the Grey arms and the motto "Foy est tout" ("faith is all"). A 2024 register entry says the blade is missing, and Bedfordshire Archives found it missing in 2011, though a 2017 photograph shows it.

Where the dial first stood is not settled. English Heritage's 2013 survey says the Kip and Knyff view of about 1704 shows a sundial at the centre of the Great Court in front of the old house, and that by the 1720s a sundial stood at the middle of the south terrace. A square brick base found in the lawn here may be an original base. Two dials, bought in 1682 and 1690–1694, are mentioned.

Six marble statues stand round it on early-1700s Ketton stone pedestals. Clockwise from the north-east: Hebe (a copy of Thorvaldsen), a dancing bacchante with a bunch of grapes, a bacchante with a tambourine, a girl feeding a bird on her shoulder, a bacchante with a ewer, and Minerva with a Medusa breastplate (a copy of the Vatican statue). Bedfordshire Archives says about 1840 for Earl de Grey. The listing says Anne Florence, Countess Cowper, added them after 1859, and quotes William Treacher (1899) that they were copied from the Vatican and bought from the 1862 Exhibition.
Sources: Historic England list entries 1113797 and 1321796; British Sundial Society register S2125, S5169 and S6338; Bedfordshire Archives, Monuments and Ornaments at Wrest Park and Statues at Wrest Park; English Heritage Research Report 6-2013; Wikipedia, London dial.

## Sundial time and clock time
Kind: reference
About: sundial
A sundial reads local solar time, which differs from the clock for three reasons.

First, the blade (gnomon) must point at the celestial pole. It is tilted up from the horizontal by the latitude, which at Wrest is about 52 degrees north, so its shadow swings round at an even 15 degrees an hour as the Earth turns. A dial made for one latitude reads wrongly at another, which is why Wynne's dial lists the latitudes and longitudes of sixteen places.

Second, longitude. Clocks in Britain keep Greenwich time, and the Sun crosses the meridian four minutes later for every degree you go west. Wrest is about 0.41 degrees west, so local noon comes about 1 minute 40 seconds after 12.00 Greenwich time.

Third, the equation of time. The Earth's orbit is an ellipse and its axis is tilted, so the Sun's true position drifts against an even clock. Over the year the sundial runs up to about 16 minutes ahead of clock time in early November and about 14 minutes behind in mid-February. The two agree on about four days: around 15 April, 13 June, 1 September and 25 December. Even then the Wrest dial still runs 1 minute 40 seconds behind Greenwich time because of its longitude, so a perfect match with a watch is never expected. Once John Flamsteed's equation-of-time tables became available, makers such as Wynne experimented with putting them on dials. The Sundial Society register lists calendrical tables on the Wrest dial but does not say whether they include one.

Add British Summer Time, which runs from late March to late October. Then a clock is an hour ahead of Greenwich, and the dial reads about an hour behind it, plus or minus the equation of time and the minute and 40 seconds. A reading is also taken from the edge of the shadow, and a thick blade throws two edges; London dials have a gap at noon for that reason.
Sources: general knowledge (astronomy of the sundial and the equation of time); Wikipedia, London dial; British Sundial Society register S2125 and S5169.

## The Long Water
Kind: site
About: long-water, mercury-statue
The Long Water is the straight canal running south from the lawns to the Archer Pavilion, and it has been the axis of the garden for more than 300 years. English Heritage dates it to the early 1680s and credits Amabel Benn, her son Anthony, 11th Earl, and his wife Mary. The registered garden's history dates the Earl and Countess's phase of work 1671–1702. The date is uncertain. The 2013 English Heritage survey says only that it was probably dug before 1685, because the 1685–1701 garden accounts do not mention digging it. Later accounts show it cleaned, with two rows of hedges and iron gates added in 1701–1706.

It has changed since. The Duke of Kent added the Pavilion at its southern end in 1709–1711, and by 1719 the northern end, where you stand, had a spade-shaped head with two arms of water. Capability Brown softened the outer canals in 1758–1760 but left the Long Water alone. The north end was cut back in the later 1700s to extend it and later backfilled, as the 1882 map shows, so today's head is not exactly the Greys' original. OpenStreetMap shows the water about 330 metres long, though the whole axis, house to Pavilion, is about 800 metres.

It is formal for a reason. The straight canal was French taste, as at Versailles, and Charles II dug one at Hampton Court about 1670. After the Glorious Revolution of 1688 the Dutch style of William III followed, more modest. The future Duke toured the Netherlands, and his letters home mention tree-lined canals.

The water is fed by a stream that enters the garden's canals at the north-west corner, from Silsoe village, with conduits and cross-canals carrying it to the head of the Long Water. Archaeologists found brick land drains on its west bank flowing west to east, and the earliest water pipes, of terracotta and planked channels, are mentioned in 1703. The south end is held by a dam, with the Pavilion's causeway on it, and sluices control the levels. The banks are clay, which is puddled to stop leaks. The garden's original spring, behind the Bath House, no longer flows, so a circulating pump has been used since 1972. Calm water is a mirror, doubling the Pavilion.

On the east bank is a statue of Mercury, 2.5 metres high, with a winged Cupid at his feet. The listing calls it "Mercury and Cupid", probably late seventeenth century and perhaps by Caius Gabriel Cibber, based on Giambologna's Mercury of 1588, and moved here in the late nineteenth century. Bedfordshire Archives dates it mid-nineteenth century. The listing puts it 70 metres from the north end, but photographs place it nearer 200 metres down. Neptune (west), Juno (north-west) and Leda (north-east) stand along the canal.
Sources: English Heritage, History of Wrest Park; English Heritage Research Report 6-2013, Wrest Park Landscape Investigations; Dawson, Bedfordshire Archaeology 24; Albion Archaeology, Old Park Weir evaluation 2007; Bedfordshire Archives, Wrest Park 1700 to 1770 and Statues at Wrest Park; Historic England list entry 1158734; Parks & Gardens UK, Wrest Park; OpenStreetMap, way 89187555.

## Amabel, Anthony and Mary: the makers of the Long Water
Kind: person
About: anthony-11th-earl, amabel-countess-of-kent, mary-baroness-lucas
Amabel Benn (about 1606–1698) was the daughter of Anthony Benn, Recorder of London, and the widow of Anthony Fane when, in 1644, she became the second wife of Henry Grey, 10th Earl of Kent. He died in 1651, leaving her a boy not yet six, Anthony, to bring up. Bedfordshire Archives calls her "the Good Countess". She stayed a dominant force at Wrest until she died in 1698: she bought land in the 1650s, leased the Cainhoe warren in 1656, and kept the accounts for the north front.

Anthony Grey (born 11 June 1645) succeeded as 11th Earl on 28 May 1651. He married Mary Lucas on 2 March 1663. Mary was the only surviving child of John, Baron Lucas of Shenfield, and was made Baroness Lucas of Crudwell in her own right in 1663. When her father died on 2 July 1671 she inherited a large estate in Wiltshire and Essex, and Henry, later Duke of Kent, was born that year. Bedfordshire Archives and the History of Parliament both link the wealth and the birth to the classical north front, which cost £3,227; the History of Parliament says about half came from the Lucas inheritance.

From 1686 Anthony and Amabel began a new walled formal garden south of the house, with fountains, two wildernesses and a terrace walk, costing over £3,000 until 1701. The Long Water, probably dug before 1685, is their best-known work. English Heritage credits the three of them together. The 2013 survey says most of the central garden shown on the Kip and Knyff views, the house, Great Court, walled garden and Long Canal, was their creation.

Anthony died on 19 August 1702 playing bowls at Tunbridge Wells, apparently from a stroke. Mary died on 1 November 1702, and both were buried at Flitton. Their son Henry inherited and became Duke of Kent in 1710. Anthony's marriage is dated 1662 in some sources because the year then began in March; it is 1663 by modern reckoning.

## Bowling Green House
Kind: site
About: bowling-green-house, bowling-urns, amphitheatre
Bowling Green House is a single-storey garden pavilion on the west side of the old garden, just south of the American Garden, beside the Duke of Kent's bowling green. Historic England (list entry 1158672, Grade II*) dates it to about 1735 and credits Batty Langley, working for Henry Grey, Duke of Kent. The dating is not settled. Parks & Gardens UK and the Heritage Fund say Langley remodelled an earlier building in 1735, and Parks & Gardens adds a latest date of 1734. Bedfordshire Archives says it "incorporates an earlier building". English Heritage's 2015 phased plan colours the main walls 1720–1721 and only the six front columns 1735 (read from the plan's colour key). The listing says the building "probably incorporates an early-eighteenth-century deer house"; no source found says more about it.

What you see: the east front, facing the green, is colour-washed render with a portico of six painted timber Tuscan columns. The west side is an arcade of arches on pale yellow brick piers (Flemish bond). There are small square wings north and south, and a hipped tiled roof. Inside are plaster panels and an ornate marble chimney-piece. The balustrade above the columns once carried six life-size statues; by 1831 they had been replaced by urns, and those were replaced again in the late nineteenth or early twentieth century by replica urns, probably by Barwell and Co. of the Eagle Foundry.

The two lead urns on stone pedestals, about 2 metres tall, stand about 5 metres south-east and north-east of the house. The listing dates them to about 1720 and says they are probably by John Van Nost the Younger, copies of carved urns by Edward Pearce and Cibber. One shows Venus in a sea chariot; the other shows a procession of nymphs, fauns and Bacchus. They appear in John Rocque's 1735 drawing and have stayed in place since. The listing's reasons for Grade II* include the rarity of early-eighteenth-century bowling pavilions.

Bowls and the family: Bedfordshire Archives says Anthony, 11th Earl of Kent, the Duke's father, died on a bowling green in Tonbridge. Wikipedia dates his death 19 August 1702 (single source); only Bedfordshire Archives mentions the bowling green.

The lake and the amphitheatre: the Leg o' Mutton Lake lies next to the house. A Geograph caption says the lake once had a semicircular extension on its south side, which gave it its name, and that the old shape can just be made out in the grass. That is one photographer's caption, so treat it as unconfirmed. The Historic England listing for the ha-ha in the Old Park (1402869) says that in 1729 work resumed on the garden, adding an amphitheatre north of the bowling green and a serpentine canal. No source found describes the amphitheatre's form. English Heritage's leaflet marks "Leg O'Mutton Lake and Amphitheatre" as one stop, number 15, next to Bowling Green House (14).

The listing calls the Duke "12th Duke of Kent"; he was the 1st Duke and 12th Earl.

## The West Half House
Kind: site
About: west-half-house, great-garden
The West Half House is a small garden shelter in a clearing in the woodland west of the Long Water. Historic England (list entry 1158741, Grade II) dates it to about 1726 and says it was built for Henry Grey, Duke of Kent. Bedfordshire Archives also says about 1726, and calls the pair of Half Houses "essentially, glorified shelters".

It has a half-round (exedral) plan under a half-conical roof covered in lead. The south-facing front is white brick, with a central segmental arched opening between pilaster strips. The back is red brick and blind, pierced only by small round ventilation holes. One step leads in through the arch to a rendered interior with a semicircular wooden seat and a stone floor with decorative black tiles. The listing says it is little altered, though its wooden pediment was replaced in the twentieth century. No architect is recorded.

Where it fits: 1726 is when the avenues and "squares" beside the Long Water were largely complete (Historic England, list entry 1158972, on the Brown monument). English Heritage calls the whole thing "a rare example in England of a formal woodland garden in the French style", with woodland avenues decorated with statues and garden buildings. English Heritage's leaflet marks "Duchess's Square and West Half House" as one place (number 17) and "Duke's Square" (22) elsewhere in the wood. A garden blog describes the woodland as bosquets and geometric clearings, including the Duke's and Duchess's squares.

Purpose: shelters and seats. A garden blog describes the Half Houses as "concealed in the shrubbery and containing benches to rest on". The plan is an exedra, a half-round recess.

Just south is the statue of the lady reading, covered in its own section ("The statue of Jemima"). Further south is the Hutton Monument, a brick wall with an inset plaque about 1730 for the Duke's friend Thomas Hutton, stated by Bedfordshire Archives to be 175 yards south of this half house.

The East Half House is its twin, about 350 metres away across the Long Water (distance measured from OpenStreetMap positions). The two differ: the East has a semicircular arch under a triangular pediment, and niches that once held statues; the West has a segmental arch and pilaster strips.

## The statue of Jemima (the lady reading)
Kind: site
About: jemima-statue, john-cheere, jemima
South of the West Half House, in the clearing the leaflet calls Duchess's Square, sits a lead statue of a woman reading. Historic England (list entry 1113805, Grade II*) says she stands about 2.5 metres high on a Portland stone pedestal, and calls her "a rare example of a mid-eighteenth-century lead sculpture depicting a seated female figure". It dates the statue to about 1748 and says it is probably by John Cheere (1709–1787). Bedfordshire Archives gives the same pose: a woman in classical dress, seated, resting on an urn, with an open book in her left hand, standing 25 yards south of the West Half House.

Who is she? The sources disagree. Historic England says she is believed to be Jemima, Marchioness Grey, and links the statue to her influence on the garden. Bedfordshire Archives says she is thought to be Jemima, first wife of the Duke of Kent, who died in 1728. The Duke's first wife was Jemima Crew (died 2 July 1728), and his granddaughter was also Jemima, which makes the confusion easy. The statue is not documented as a portrait of either. If asked, give both. Do not assert one.

Jemima, Marchioness Grey (born 9 October 1722 at Copenhagen according to Bedfordshire Archives; The Peerage says 1723) was the Duke's granddaughter. She married Philip Yorke, the Lord Chancellor's son, on 22 May 1740, when she was 17 by the Bedfordshire Archives date. The Duke died on 5 June 1740, having outlived all his sons, and she inherited Wrest and the Marquess Grey title. Some summaries say she inherited on condition that she marry Philip; Bedfordshire Archives does not mention a condition, so treat that as unconfirmed. Philip became 2nd Earl of Hardwicke on 6 March 1764. A History Today piece describes the circle at Wrest: relatives and friends, days in the gardens and evenings trading poems.

John Cheere (1709–1787) was the younger brother of the sculptor Sir Henry Cheere and ran a business making statues, many of them in lead. Wikipedia credits him with the Medici lions at Stowe and, from 1751 to 1766, figures for the grottoes and gardens at Stourhead. Historic England's listing says he lived 1709–1789; Wikipedia says 1787. At Wrest he is thought to have made King William III by the Long Water and probably this statue. Bedfordshire Archives does not name a sculptor for the lady reading, and attributes the William III statue to John Nost.

## The East Half House
Kind: site
About: east-half-house, great-garden
The East Half House is the twin of the West Half House, in the woodland east of the Long Water, near the Pavilion end. Historic England (list entry 1138342, Grade II) describes it as "a garden house or alcove seat" for Henry Grey, Duke of Kent, dating from 1726, and says the architect is unknown. Bedfordshire Archives says about 1726 and groups it with the West Half House as "glorified shelters" in the wooded areas east and west of the central canal.

It is brick with a lead-covered half-conical roof. The plan is square at the front and apsidal (rounded) at the rear. The front is three bays wide, with a central semicircular archway under a triangular pediment, and flanking niches which once held statues. The interior is plain apart from a semicircular wooden seat. The pair were built with small differences, and Wikipedia says they sit in small clearings on either side of the Long Water.

Who designed things for the Duke: English Heritage says the Duke employed Hawksmoor, Archer, Langley and William Kent. Historic England's entry on the Brown monument also names Juvarra, Leoni and Gibbs among the architects from whom garden buildings were commissioned in the 1720s, and the Duke's garden buildings were not all built (general knowledge; unconfirmed). The Half Houses are not credited to any of them.

The Duke. Henry Grey (1671–1740) succeeded his father as 12th Earl of Kent in 1702 and was Lord Chamberlain from 1704 to 1710. He became Marquess of Kent in 1706, the year English Heritage gives for the start of his Great Garden, and Duke of Kent in 1710, in exchange for giving up the Chamberlain's post. He was made a Knight of the Garter in 1712. He outlived all his sons. English Heritage thinks the Pavilion's wall paintings celebrate the dukedom.

Walking: the Archer Pavilion is about 200 metres west of here (measured from OpenStreetMap positions), and the canal that curves round the garden's east side is close by. The 2011 restoration reinstated about three miles of woodland paths, which had been grassed over during the Second World War (Heritage Fund).

In 1726 the avenues and squares were largely complete. The other garden buildings the Duke left are the Pavilion (1709–1711), the Bowling Green House (about 1735), the Hutton Monument (about 1730) and the statue of William III (1730s).

## Oldpark Water and the western canal
Kind: site
About: boundary-canals, serpentine-canal
Oldpark Water (also written Old Park Water) is the western stretch of the chain of canals that encircle the old garden, usually called "the river". It is part of the boundary canals: an Albion Archaeology report for English Heritage (2007) says "the encircling canals including Old Park Water and the Serpentine achieved their present form during the landscaping works carried out by Brown 1758–1760". It adds that Brown's designs were mostly "modifications in shape to earlier water bodies constructed from the period 1702–1720". English Heritage says the Duke's canals enclose the woodland garden on three sides.

It is presumably named for the Old Park, which lies to its west. Historic England (list entry 1402869) calls the Old Park the oldest parkland at Wrest, parts probably dating from the fourteenth century. An 18th-century sandstone ha-ha about 400 metres long (Grade II, listed 2012) runs across the Old Park from the garden boundary to a bridleway on the west.

Not all the curves are Brown's. The same listing says that in 1729 the Duke's garden works included a serpentine canal. Brown (1758–1760) joined the waters together and made the straight perimeter canals meander. Jemima valued her grandfather's garden, so his changes were limited.

How it works (Albion 2007, quoting earlier surveys): the canals are man-made and rely on weirs and retaining walls. The banks are natural clay, prone to erosion if levels fluctuate, and repairs still involve "puddling" the clay to stop leaks. Old Park Weir links Old Park Water with the Serpentine on the western edge of the park. The two waters were completed about 1760, and the difference in their levels needed a bank of earth "which planting and shrubs must conceal" (a Bedfordshire archives document, crt 190/45, quoted by Albion). A sluice was added between 1901 and 1924. The original spring in the Bath Ground no longer flows, so the present pumped circulation, adapted in 1972, keeps the water moving. A farm ditch at the north-west corner brings silt, so the head of Old Park Water silts up. A Historic England photograph records a dredger clearing Old Park Water, probably in 2009 (found by search only).

Views: the quiet tree-lined water makes a good still picture. The Bowling Green House stands on its east bank to the north, and the Serpentine carries on southward.

Sources disagree on when the garden began: Albion says "from the 1650s onwards"; Historic England's register gives the earlier formal landscape as 1671–1702, with the Long Water in the early 1680s.

## The Duke of Kent's Great Garden (1706–1740)
Kind: site
About: great-garden, boundary-canals
Between 1706 and the 1720s Henry Grey, Duke of Kent, laid out what English Heritage calls "a massive formal woodland garden, enclosed on three sides by canals". It is "a rare example in England of a formal woodland garden in the French style". It was built round his family's earlier work: English Heritage says the Long Water, made in the early 1680s, was the work of his grandmother Amabel Benn, his father Anthony and his mother Mary.

What he made: woodland flanking the Long Water, cut by straight avenues and open "squares", decorated with statues and garden buildings; formal canals enclosing it, with more at right angles to the Long Water; and, at the far end, Thomas Archer's Pavilion (1709–1711). The avenues and squares were largely complete by 1726, and the canals were altered in the 1720s. In 1729 more work added a serpentine canal and an amphitheatre north of the bowling green. John Rocque recorded the garden and its buildings in plans of 1735 and 1737.

The designers: English Heritage names Hawksmoor, Archer, Langley and William Kent. Historic England adds Juvarra, Leoni and Gibbs among the architects from whom garden buildings were commissioned in the 1720s; not all of the designs were built (unconfirmed). Surviving buildings: the Pavilion (1709–1711), the Half Houses (about 1726), the Hutton Monument (about 1730), the Bowling Green House (about 1735) and the statue of William III (1730s).

After him: Jemima, Marchioness Grey, inherited in 1740 and "hardly altered the core of the garden". Lancelot Brown worked on the periphery from about 1758 to 1760 to soften the perimeter canals. The register lists the Great Garden among Wrest's main phases (early eighteenth century and early nineteenth century, on a late-seventeenth-century base). Most park and avenue trees were felled between the 1920s and 1940s. The woodland paths were grassed over in the Second World War and about three miles were reinstated in the 2011 restoration.

Walking it: paths run each side of the Long Water to the Pavilion. The Half Houses give places to rest. The canals can be followed round the west (Oldpark Water) and east sides.

The register, as quoted by Parks & Gardens UK, says the garden's iconography relates to the Duke's Whiggish beliefs and his regard for William III. Albion Archaeology's date for the first formal garden here is "from the 1650s", earlier than the register's 1671.
Sources: English Heritage, History of Wrest Park; Parks & Gardens UK, Wrest Park (quoting Historic England's register entry 1000113); Historic England list entry 1158972; Historic England list entry 1402869; Heritage Fund (2011).

## The Duke of Kent and the War of the Spanish Succession
Kind: person
About: duke-of-kent, spanish-succession, pavilion
Henry Grey (1671–1740) lived through the War of the Spanish Succession (March 1701 to September 1714) as a member of the royal household. He succeeded as Earl of Kent in 1702, was Lord Chamberlain and a Privy Councillor from 1704 to 1710, became Marquess of Kent in 1706 and Duke of Kent in 1710. Wikipedia says he got the dukedom for giving up the Chamberlain's post. It calls him "the politically expedient candidate", seen as lacking talent and ambition. He was made a Knight of the Garter in 1712 and was Lord Lieutenant of Bedfordshire from 1711 to 1740.

The war: when Charles II of Spain, the last Spanish Habsburg, died childless in November 1700, Philip of Anjou, grandson of Louis XIV, became Philip V. In September 1701 the Habsburg Emperor Leopold I, the Dutch Republic and Britain renewed the Grand Alliance. The Habsburg claimant was Leopold's son, Archduke Charles. In 1711 Charles became Holy Roman Emperor, which led the British government to seek peace. The Treaty of Utrecht followed in 1713 and the Treaties of Rastatt and Baden in 1714. Britain kept Gibraltar and Menorca.

Genuine links, and their limits. First, timing: the Pavilion was begun in 1709 and finished in 1711, and the dukedom came in 1710, during the war, in the year before Charles became Emperor. English Heritage thinks the Pavilion's wall paintings celebrate the dukedom. Second, politics: the register says the garden's iconography reflects the Duke's Whiggish beliefs and regard for William III, whose 1689 Grand Alliance against France was renewed in 1701. I found no source showing the Duke fought, held a command, or built anything in direct response to a battle. His posts were in the royal household. Any story linking the Pavilion to a particular victory is unsupported.

The Duke's family: he married Jemima Crew in 1694 (she died on 2 July 1728), and Lady Sophia Bentinck on 24 March 1729. Of his sons, Henry died in 1717, Anthony, Earl of Harold, in 1723 and George in 1733; he died on 5 June 1740. Shortly before he died he became Marquess Grey, with the title passing to his granddaughter Jemima.
Sources: Wikipedia, Henry Grey, 1st Duke of Kent; Wikipedia, War of the Spanish Succession; English Heritage, History of Wrest Park; Historic England register entry 1000113 (via Parks & Gardens UK); Bedfordshire Archives, Buildings at Wrest Park.

## Batty Langley
Kind: person
About: batty-langley, bowling-green-house
Batty Langley (baptised 14 September 1696, died 3 March 1751) was a garden designer and writer of pattern books. He was born at Twickenham, the son of a gardener, Daniel Langley, and was named after David Batty, one of his father's patrons. His one confirmed commission at Wrest is Bowling Green House, one of his few known commissions overall (Wikipedia); Bedfordshire Archives also credits him with a building on the site of the later Orangery.

Pattern books were illustrated guides that let builders and craftsmen copy correct designs and proportions without an architect. Langley's included Practical Geometry (1726), New Principles of Gardening (1728), A Sure Guide to Builders (1729), Ancient Masonry (1733–1736), The Builder's Complete Assistant (1738) and The Builder's Jewel (1741). Their geometric rules and details spread across Georgian Britain and America: George Washington drew on them for Mount Vernon.

He is best known for trying to "improve" Gothic architecture by giving it classical rules and proportions (Ancient Architecture, Restored, and Improved, 1742, reissued in 1747 as Gothic Architecture, improved by Rules and Proportions). Horace Walpole, whose Strawberry Hill was a major Gothic Revival work, criticised this harshly. Langley named four sons Hiram, Euclid, Vitruvius and Archimedes, wrote a column for the Grub Street Journal (1734–1735) as "Hiram", is thought to have been a Freemason, and was imprisoned for debt in Newgate.

At Wrest he worked for the Duke of Kent in the 1730s. Parks & Gardens UK and the Heritage Fund say he remodelled the Bowling Green House in 1735 (Parks & Gardens gives a latest date of 1734); the Historic England listing says about 1735. A garden blog says he and Thomas Wright worked on the grounds in a more informal style between the Duke's early work and Rocque's 1737 survey; this is not confirmed elsewhere.

The Duke's other designers: English Heritage names Hawksmoor, Archer, Langley and William Kent.
Sources: Wikipedia, Batty Langley; Historic England list entry 1158672; Bedfordshire Archives, Buildings at Wrest Park; Parks & Gardens UK, Wrest Park; Heritage Fund (2011); The Garden History blog (2014); English Heritage, History of Wrest Park.

## The Mithraic Altar and the Hutton Monument
Kind: site
About: mithraic-altar, hutton-monument, athenian-letters
The Mithraic Altar stands in woodland west of the Archer Pavilion. It was put up for Jemima, Marchioness Grey, and her husband Philip Yorke, and Historic England says it was complete by September 1748. It is a block of flint and stone: a rough base of Ketton stone on uncut flint, a die faced with knapped flint, a frieze encrusted with pebbles, carved lion's paws at the corners, and acanthus-leaf scrolls under a cap carved with drapery. Two stone tablets, on the west and east faces, carry inscriptions, one in cuneiform-style characters and one in Greek, with traces of black paint. It is Grade II*, and still stands where it was put. Bedfordshire Archives says it is about ten feet high; the Historic England listing gives the pedestal's plan as roughly 2.3 by 2.55 metres.

The name is misleading. Mithras was a god of Persian origin, worshipped in a Roman mystery cult popular with soldiers, but this is not an ancient altar. Bedfordshire Archives calls it "a fanciful creation of 1748"; Historic England describes a "Persian" style design. English Heritage's account of the women of Wrest calls it "an intellectual joke": Jemima, her husband and friends built it with a cryptic inscription inspired by the Athenian Letters.

The Athenian Letters were written by a circle around Philip Yorke and his brother Charles, begun in 1739 and printed privately in two volumes in 1741 and 1743. They are framed as letters from an agent of the King of Persia living in Athens during the Peloponnesian War. Historic England records an 1831 account that the altar commemorated the book, and that the Greek inscription was written by Daniel Wray, one of the contributors. That a Persian agent is the narrator fits the altar's Persian look, though no source states that link outright.

The design is credited to Thomas Wright, who was Jemima's tutor: see the section on Thomas Wright of Durham. (The Historic England listing gives his dates as "1711–1776"; every other source gives his death as 1786.)

The Hutton Monument stands about 40 metres west of the altar, about 175 yards south of the West Half House. Bedfordshire Archives dates it to about 1730 and says it was put up for Henry, Duke of Kent: a brick wall with an inset ashlar plaque commemorating Thomas Hutton, Esquire, "of Somerset House", who enjoyed the place and was agreeable company to his friend the Duke. The archive's author finds the inscription "rather condescending". The wording could not be checked against the stone, so quote it only as a paraphrase.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1158808 (Mithraic Altar); Bedfordshire Archives, Monuments and Ornaments at Wrest Park; English Heritage, Women's Designs at Wrest Park (Google Arts & Culture); Wikipedia, Athenian Letters.

## Thomas Wright of Durham
Kind: person
About: thomas-wright, original-theory, kant-theory, mithraic-altar
Thomas Wright (born 22 September 1711 at Byers Green, County Durham; died 25 February 1786) was a clockmaker, navigation teacher, astronomer and garden designer. He was apprenticed to a clockmaker at Bishop Auckland in 1725, studied mathematics and navigation, and in 1730 opened a school in Sunderland, selling instruments he made himself. He published a large engraved diagram of the heavens as a teaching aid (Clavis coelestis, 1742). From about 1746 he taught mathematics and astronomy to aristocratic women and surveyed estates, planning at least 15 gardens and grottoes. In 1755 he bought the house where he was born, rebuilt it and lived there until his death.

His link to Wrest is genuine. English Heritage says Jemima was tutored in garden design, surveying, geometry and astronomy by Wright, and the Wrest Circle research project at Oxford says he tutored all the women in her coterie. Historic England credits him with the design of the Mithraic Altar. A Durham University catalogue says he taught Lady Anne Sophia Grey (later Egerton) in 1739, 1741, 1742 and 1745, and adds that Capability Brown later destroyed some of Wright's landscapes at Wrest. That last remark is not confirmed by any Wrest source found, so treat it with caution.

His famous book is An Original Theory or New Hypothesis of the Universe (1750), self-published, with fine engravings, in a very small printing (one account says 118 copies). Wright explained the Milky Way as an effect of our position: we are inside a thin layer of stars, so looking along it we see a dense band of light and looking out of it we see few. His own picture was more theological than the modern one. Stars moved round a divine centre, in a flat ring (his Plate XXV) or in nested spherical shells (Plate XXVII); he treated both as possible and later preferred the shells. Parks & Gardens UK describes it as a grindstone-shaped disc and a forerunner of William Herschel's work (1785); Linda Hall Library points out that Wright himself did not conclude the Milky Way is a disc.

The disc idea was taken up by Immanuel Kant. Kant learned of Wright's book from a review in a German periodical, not from the book itself, and in 1755 published his Universal Natural History and Theory of the Heavens. Wright also suggested that faint nebulae might be other galaxies, which Kant called "island universes". Most astronomers rejected the idea until Edwin Hubble used Cepheid variable stars to show in 1924 that spiral nebulae are distant galaxies.

## The Chinese Temple
Kind: site
About: chinese-temple, chinese-bridge
The Chinese Temple, listed Grade II as the Chinese Summerhouse, stands on the south side of the north Broad Water. It is a timber building with a lead-covered roof on a stone plinth, open on its sides and front. A two-stage pagoda roof with upturned corners is topped by a crouching wyvern, the Grey family emblem (a wyvern is a two-legged dragon), and ornamental bells hang from the corners. Fretwork decorates the painted panelling and eaves. Inside are a stone floor and a simple timber bench along the back.

Date and designer: Historic England and Bedfordshire Archives date it to about 1760, for Jemima, Marchioness Grey, and say it was possibly designed by Sir William Chambers, who also designed the Pagoda at Kew. English Heritage's Google Arts story puts Jemima's Chinese-inspired "set piece" in the late 1750s: a wooden Chinese bridge, a temple, a conch-shell water feature, a willow and a tulip tree. Wikipedia's list of Chambers's work gives "about 1766" and "attributed". No document linking Chambers to Wrest has been found, so describe the attribution as possible. Bedfordshire Archives records that Edward Stevens, who also built the Bath House, repaired the temple in 1769.

It was rebuilt in 1876 for Lady Anne Cowper (Bedfordshire Archives says "rebuilt"; Historic England and Wikipedia say "remodelled") and heavily restored in the twentieth century.

It stands in the setting made when Jemima had Capability Brown rework the canals into the Broad Water between 1758 and 1760. Historic England places the Chinese Bridge about 60 metres from the temple.

What to look for: the wyvern on the very top of the roof; the little bells hanging at the corners of both tiers; the lattice-style fretwork in the panels and under the eaves; the bench inside; and the upturned, curling ends of the roof, which are the feature that most says "Chinese" to a European eye. Because it is timber, it needs repainting and repair far more often than stone, which is why it has been restored more than once. Jemima wrote of her garden in 1749 that "nothing can surpass it" (English Heritage's Google Arts story), a few years before this corner was made.

Chinese buildings like this were fashionable in English gardens in the middle of the eighteenth century. The taste is called chinoiserie, a European fantasy based on imported goods and travellers' reports; see the section on William Chambers.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1321760; Bedfordshire Archives, Buildings at Wrest Park; English Heritage, Women's Designs at Wrest Park; Wikipedia, William Chambers (architect); Wikipedia, Wrest Park; Historic England list entry 1158909; general knowledge for the upkeep of timber buildings.

## The Chinese Bridge
Kind: site
About: chinese-bridge
The Chinese Bridge crosses the north end of the Broad Water, east of the temple. The present bridge dates from 1876. Historic England says it was designed by Anne Florence, Countess Cowper, and built by estate workmen. It is of yellow brick with limestone dressings and a single elliptical arch, with rusticated voussoirs along its lower edge. The parapet has a dentil course of brick at its base, recessed panels, and a corbelled brick pier at the centre; the central and end piers carry ball finials. Stones on either side bear the date 1876 and her monogram under a coronet. Bedfordshire Archives reads the initials as those of Anne Florence, Countess Cowper, also 7th Baroness Lucas of Crudwell.

It replaced a timber bridge of the 1760s, which had itself succeeded an earlier bridge of the 1750s, from the years when Lancelot Brown reshaped the canals. Bedfordshire Archives says its style copies the bridge on Willow Pattern china. The Willow Pattern is a design made in England in the late 1700s, not an old Chinese one (general knowledge).

Engineering: an elliptical arch is a flattened curve, wider than it is high. Compared with a semicircular arch it gives a lower roadway for the same span, so less climbing, but it pushes outwards more strongly at its feet. Those sideways thrusts must be resisted by the abutments, which here are part of the banks. The wedge-shaped stones (voussoirs) pass the load round the curve in compression, which brick and stone handle well. The rusticated voussoirs are the roughened, boldly outlined stones at the arch's edge. This is general engineering knowledge, not stated by any Wrest source. The bridge has steps.

The Countess's family, the Cowpers and Lucases, had inherited Wrest in 1859; see the section on Anne Florence, Countess Cowper. The meadow just north of the bridge is one of English Heritage's recommended spots for autumn colour.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1158909; Bedfordshire Archives, Buildings at Wrest Park; English Heritage, Seasonal garden highlights; general knowledge for the arch, the Willow Pattern and voussoirs.

## William Chambers and chinoiserie
Kind: person
About: william-chambers, kew-pagoda, chinese-designs-book
Sir William Chambers (1723–1796) was an architect. Between 1740 and 1749 he worked for the Swedish East India Company and made three voyages to China, where he studied Chinese buildings. In 1757 he published Designs of Chinese Buildings, Furniture, Dresses, Machines, and Utensils in London. In 1761–1762 he built the ten-storey Great Pagoda at Kew for Princess Augusta, which helped to spread the fashion for Chinese-style garden buildings in England.

Historic England says the Chinese Temple at Wrest was "possibly" to his design. No other source confirms it, so say "possibly". Some accounts of the Kew Pagoda give its inspiration as the Porcelain Tower at Nanjing and others a timber tower at Whampoa; sources differ.

Chinoiserie is the European fashion for Chinese-style objects and buildings, about 1700 to 1780. It drew on imported porcelain, lacquer and silk and on travellers' accounts. It gave light, playful, painted and fretworked buildings: pagodas, bridges, tea houses with bells and upturned eaves. Wrest's temple has all these: upturned roof corners, bells and fretwork. Jemima's Chinese set piece was in the late 1750s, so quite early in the fashion.
Sources: Wikipedia, William Chambers (architect); Historic England list entry 1321760; English Heritage, Women's Designs at Wrest Park; History Today, The Great Pagoda at Kew (search summary only, not opened); general knowledge for the dates and character of chinoiserie.

## Diana and the greyhound
Kind: site
About: diana-statue
By the Ladies' Lake, a short way west of the Chinese Temple, stands a lead statue of Diana, the Roman goddess of hunting. She is naked except for drapery about her waist, with a greyhound at her side. The statue is 2.34 metres tall and stands on a Portland stone plinth. The geograph caption and OpenStreetMap place it by the Ladies' Lake.

Art Fund records it as made about 1729–1730 and attributed to Andrew Carpenter, and says it was commissioned specifically for the Wrest gardens. Art UK says it is thought to be by Carpenter (about 1677–1737), a leading maker of lead garden statues with a yard near Hyde Park, and that Henry, Duke of Kent, bought it in the late 1720s. English Heritage calls it an eighteenth-century statue.

Why it was ever away: the Art Fund's record says most of the estate's lead statuary was melted down for financial reasons by the Greys in 1809, but this one survived. Its recorded provenance runs: John George Murray, 1917 (the buyer of Wrest, when the estate was sold); moved to Coles Park about 1939; moved to Idlicote House about 1970. It was bought back with a £30,000 Art Fund grant towards a cost of £143,200, recorded in 2012; the vendor was the auction house Summers Place.

Lead was a popular garden material in the eighteenth century: it could be cast in a mould, was far cheaper than carved marble, and weathers to a soft grey unless painted or gilded. That is also why it was vulnerable: lead was valuable to melt down, which is how most of Wrest's lead figures were lost. What to look for: the goddess leaning forward with one foot raised on a small block, the dog sitting up beside her with its head lifted to her hand, and the collar round the greyhound's neck.

Do not confuse it with the painted lead group of Diana and Endymion in the French Parterre, which Bedfordshire Archives dates to about 1730. OpenStreetMap labels this statue simply "Diana".

## The five ancient altars and the dogs' cemetery
Kind: site
About: ancient-altars, ancient-altars-carved
Five white marble altars stand on a circular lawn among trees south of the Ladies' Lake: four round the edge and one in the middle. Historic England dates them to the late Hellenistic period, the late second to first century BC, making them by far the oldest objects at Wrest. Each is cylindrical, about a metre tall (Bedfordshire Archives says about four feet), with a thin projecting cornice, a plain marble cap and a square stone base. Each is carved with four ox heads (bucrania) joined by festoons of flowers. The pattern recalls the ox sacrifices made at altars.

Provenance: in 1817 Amabel, Countess de Grey, bought them from T. Beauclere Esq. of Muswell Hill and placed them where they are. Where Mr Beauclere obtained them is not known. They have stood in this setting ever since. Historic England lists the group Grade II*; the lawn is also on OpenStreetMap as the "Graeco-Roman Altars".

The central altar carries a Greek inscription: "Hail Diogenes, son of Enon, honest citizen of Askalon", Askalon being the ancient city of Ashkelon, on the coast near Gaza. Bedfordshire Archives notes that this inscription is much less worn than the stone around it and thinks it probably an early nineteenth-century addition.

Amabel (1751–1833) inherited Wrest from her mother, Jemima, in 1797. English Heritage says she made few changes to the garden, bought the altars in 1817 and had many of the lead statues melted for roof repairs, which she later regretted. The Art Fund puts the melting at 1809 and gives "financial reasons". The two sources agree on the act, differ slightly on the reason.

The dogs' cemetery lies in woodland, about 90 metres south-east of the altar lawn on OpenStreetMap's positions. English Heritage says the Lucas family, who owned Wrest into the twentieth century, buried their favourite dogs there, with headstones arranged round a stone dog on a plinth. No source found gives the dates or the dogs' names; treat any detail beyond this as unconfirmed.

## The statue of Atlas
Kind: site
About: atlas
The Atlas statue stands in rough grass about 250 metres south-east of the site of the old house. Historic England lists it Grade II as a Portland stone figure of Atlas, stooping with the globe on his shoulders and holding it with both hands. Drapery hangs over his left shoulder and falls to a tree stump behind his leg. He is muscular, with a strong face, a large nose and thick beard and hair. A small hole on the body is thought to be left from a nineteenth-century fig leaf. The figure stands on a naturalistic base with a later sandstone base, and the pedestal is thought to be nineteenth century. The hands and feet have lost much of their detail.

Date and place: the statue is understood to date from the early eighteenth century, when Henry Grey, later Duke of Kent, was developing the gardens, and is probably one of his additions. Its original position is unknown; the earliest record of it here, east of the old house, is from 1900. Earthworks show it once stood in a pond, now silted up; Bedfordshire Archives noted in 2011 that the pond was dry, and that although it is called the Atlas Fountain, "it is not a fountain, just a statue".

Space link: in Greek myth the Titan Atlas was condemned to hold up the sky (the heavens), not the Earth. Classical art shows him holding the celestial spheres. The "Atlas carrying the world" picture is a later muddle, helped by Mercator's sixteenth-century dedication of his map collection to the Titan, which made "atlas" mean a book of maps.

A celestial globe shows the sky as if seen from outside, so constellations look the opposite way round to the view from Earth. The best-known ancient example is the Farnese Atlas in the National Archaeological Museum in Naples: a Roman marble of about 150 AD, probably copied from an earlier Greek work. Atlas carries a globe 65 centimetres across, carved with 41 or 42 of the 48 classical constellations. It is the oldest surviving celestial globe. In 2005 Bradley Schaefer argued that its constellations fit the lost star catalogue of Hipparchus, about 129 BC; this is contested. Nothing suggests the Wrest Atlas was modelled on the Farnese one.

## The Bath House and Cascade
Kind: site
About: bath-house, bath-house-cascade, bath-house-bath-filled, bath-house-restoration, bath-house-pipes-lead, recirculating-pump
The Bath House is a cold plunge bath for Jemima, Marchioness Grey, and her husband Philip Yorke, built about 1769–1771 by Edward Stevens. It is a single-storey figure-of-eight: a circular bath room to the south and an octagonal dressing room to the north. Contemporaries called it a Roman bath, a hermitage and a cold bath. In June 1771 Horace Walpole praised "a hermitage and cold bath" at Wrest in "bold good taste". It was really used: in 1784 the vicar asked for a key to the gardens to bathe for his health.

Construction: local ironstone, dressed inside and rough-hewn outside, with big round stones as lintels. Historic England says the rough ironstone shows "considerable engineering skill". The outer rusticated skin may have been applied after the shell was built, and early drawings show buttresses (now gone), perhaps to suggest a ruin propped up. The bath room has a stone dome with holes in it, three large ragged openings according to the listing, built to look ruined. The dressing room has a conical thatched roof, tall niches for clothes, and a floor of pebbles and deer bones in a central circle and eight radiating sections. It is uncertain how much is original, as the floor was redone in the 1960s.

The bath is a round tank in two stages with a stone path round the lower one. Narrow steps run down on either side of the doorway, rebuilt in the 1960s from slight remains. The lion's-head spout above is also from that restoration; the original inlet is unknown, though the listing calls it a lead lion's head.

Water: the bath was fed by pipes from a spring. They were wood, mended in 1783 and replaced in 1785, until 1801–1802, when boggy ground wrecking the wooden pipes led the agent to favour lead; lead pipes were laid in 1802. The Cascade, a mound with a rough arch, was built over the spring head. The listing says its chamber holds a lead-lined tank, but the 2015 survey found a concrete-lined tank, probably replacing an earlier one. The spring was flowing in 1952, then dried up as boreholes drew down the water table. Since about 1972 a pump on the Broad Water recirculates water to the Cascade. Earl de Grey thought the "chalybeate" (iron-rich) claim was wrong; later accounts disagree.

Later history: the Earl filled the bath in 1834, after hot and cold baths were fitted in the old house in 1827–1828. The ministry underpinned the building in 1954 and rebuilt it in the 1960s, re-thatching the dressing room and digging out the bath. English Heritage restored the grounds in 2019 and the Cascade and bridge in 2022.

Steps: the bath room's steps lead down into the bath; three steps rise to the dressing room; and the 2015 survey found a stepped path down the east side of the Cascade mound. Which of these the leaflet marks is unconfirmed.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1113790; Historic England Research Report 74/2018 (Kewley); Research Report 84/2015 (Alexander, Newsome and Chamberlin); Albion Archaeology 2007 (Old Park Weir) and 2016/177; English Heritage, Revitalising Wrest Park.

## The Capability Brown column (the Rustic Column)
Kind: site
About: brown-column, brown-river, capability-brown
The monument credited to Capability Brown is a tall stone column on a plinth, about 6 metres high, with two rough rusticated blocks breaking the shaft. Its inscription reads: "These gardens originally laid out by Henry Duke of Kent, were altered by Philip Earl of Hardwicke and Jemima Marchioness Grey, with the professional assistance of Lancelot Brown Esq. in the years 1758, 1759, 1760." Earl de Grey called it the Rustic Column. The listing (Grade II) says it was erected in 1760 and is attributed to Edward Stevens. Historic England's 2018 report reads the inscription as marking the end of Brown's works, so the column was probably put up later in the 1760s, perhaps in 1769–1771 when Stevens was active at Wrest; the 2013 report gives 1770–1772. (Stevens was born about 1744, so he was about sixteen in 1760.)

What tops it is disputed. Bedfordshire Archives says a pineapple, the Historic England listing says a finial ornamented with acanthus leaves, and the 2018 report calls it an urn-topped Tuscan column (the listing says Doric). A close look on the day would settle it.

Where it stands: Earl de Grey wrote in the 1830s that it was first put up "as an object to look at" from the Bath House and was moved in 1828 by Countess de Grey. The listing repeats 1828, but the 2015 survey puts the move at about 1830 and certainly by 1831. The 2015 excavations found its original site near the Great Yew Hedge, now marked by a plaque, visible from the Cascade's top. The listing suspects the rustication was added later to echo the rustic Bath House.

Brown at Wrest: Jemima (or Philip, who also employed Brown at Wimpole) hired him in 1758. English Heritage says he realised that to do more "might unravel the Mystery of the Gardens". The listing says he laid the waters together and made the straight canals meander. He returned briefly in 1778 and 1779. No Brown drawings for Wrest survive. Walpole attributed the Bath House to him, but Historic England thinks Stevens designed it.

## How Wrest's water works: supply, levels and drainage
Kind: site
About: boundary-canals, brown-river, bath-house, orangery, recirculating-pump, old-park-weir-raised
Wrest Park's canals and lakes are man-made on gault clay, held in by clay banks, stone weirs and sluices. Albion Archaeology's 2007 report, built on a talk with English Heritage's head gardener, gives the plumbing. Apart from the circulating pump for the Bath House Cascade, the flow appears to be by gravity.

Supply and flow: a stream from the north-west (a farm ditch, which also carries run-off from the Silsoe bypass) feeds Old Park Water, the highest of the chain. The stream enters above the Bath House Pond (the Lily Pond), which spills into Old Park Water over a stepped sluice. Water passes Old Park Weir into the Serpentine, which falls 4–5 feet at its own weir, towards the Broad Water. The River Hit enters the south Broad Water through a stop-cock sluice. An underground drain with an adjustable pipe feeds Leg o' Mutton Lake from Old Park Water. Its exits carry excess water into the Long Canal, which passes it by pipe to Ladies Lake. Ladies Lake's outflow joins the north Broad Water near the Chinese Bridge. The south-east corner of the Long Canal has a wooden sluice gate, with an underground pipe running past the Archer Pavilion to a big brick culvert (5 feet square) that carries water out under the Broad Water to the River Hit.

Making the Long Canal: Albion's chronology says it was made about 1686–1688 by building a dam across a shallow valley (English Heritage says the early 1680s). Historic England's 2013 survey records an east-west dam and a causeway running from it to the Archer Pavilion.

Leaks and floods: the weirs are sandstone and ironstone blocks in mortar, and leaks are stopped by puddling the clay. About 1971 Old Park Weir was raised 6–10 inches in concrete to hold more water for the research institute's irrigation. By the 2000s this was blamed for flooding between the Orangery and Bath House; blocked clay pipes were dug out in 2003.

House supply: a hydraulic ram in a brick chamber in the woodland near the Bowling Green took water from Old Park Water and pushed it up to two 500-gallon lead tanks in the roof of the mansion, supplying house, stables and garden, with overflow feeding the marble and Atlas fountains. The sources do not date the ram. It was removed in the 1980s and is stored in the Orangery undercroft. A hydraulic ram uses the force of water suddenly stopping in a pipe to lift a part of it uphill, with no fuel (general knowledge).

Drying and refilling: the Bath House spring dried up (see the Bath House section), and a pump installed about 1972 circulates water from the north Broad Water. The canals were drained in the Second World War, and dredged in 1952, about 1965, 1973 and 1988–1989.
Sources: Albion Archaeology, Hydrology Restoration: Old Park Weir (2007/47) and Evaluation 2016/177; Historic England Research Report 84/2015; Research Report 74/2018; Historic England list entry 1158549; English Heritage, History of Wrest Park; general knowledge (hydraulic ram principle).

## The service yard: brewhouse, horse pump and fire engine
Kind: site
About: service-range
The service range at the east end of the new house was designed by Earl de Grey in 1834–1839, with James Clephan as clerk of works. Historic England lists the former brewhouse, link block, pump house and fire-engine house together as Grade II (2012). They sit beside the stable block because two of them needed horses.

The pump house was open-ended, aligned east to west, and driven by a horse; it adjoined a well in the courtyard. The listing does not say where the water went, and no equipment survives. A horse engine (or gin) is a beam or capstan turned by a horse walking in a circle, which drove a pump (general knowledge, not from the listing). The sources found do not say where this pumped water went, or how the pump house relates to the ram and the roof tanks.

The fire-engine house is a big double-height shed at right angles to the range, built for a horse-drawn fire engine. A fire engine of the 1830s was a hand- or horse-worked pump on a cart, and a country house kept one because the nearest brigade was far away (general knowledge).

The brewhouse brewed beer for the household and was linked to the main house by a beer pipe. By 1934 the building held water-softening equipment. Most of it is now offices and storage.

## Edward Stevens, architect
Kind: person
About: edward-stevens
Edward Stevens (about 1744–1775) was an architect who died young and is little known. He was William Chambers's pupil from 1760 to 1766, when he set up on his own. His principal work seems to have been Doveridge Hall, Derbyshire, designed in 1769, built 1770–1777 and demolished in 1938. He went to study in Italy in 1774 and died there soon after.

At Wrest he is the documented architect of the Bath House: in September 1769 he wrote to Lord Hardwicke that he had nearly finished the front of the arch over the Spring Head, and Lord Hardwicke paid him £1,898 between August 1770 and July 1773. He also designed the Chinese Bridge of 1770, drew up a "Theatre or Colonnade" (not built), made alterations to the house, and is believed to have designed the column commemorating Brown. Historic England's 2018 report says the Bath House is one of several designs that owe something to Jemima and Philip's own taste and to their tutor Thomas Wright.
Sources: Historic England Research Report 74/2018 (Kewley); Historic England list entry 1158972.

## The science years (east lawn)
Kind: site
About: niae, sun-insurance, ministry-purchase, niae-closure
From the east lawn you look north-east towards the modern buildings that make up the former research campus. For nearly sixty years, from 1948 until 2006, this side of Wrest Park was home to an agricultural engineering research institute: first the National Institute of Agricultural Engineering (NIAE), renamed Silsoe Research Institute in 1991. English Heritage's history says the estate "housed agricultural engineering research institutes from 1948 until 2006". The Historic England register entry adds that industrial farm buildings and offices were added to the east and north-east of the house in the later twentieth century, which is the part of the estate you are looking at. The old stable block (built 1834–1839 for the 2nd Earl de Grey) lies in front of them and is listed as Grade II; a search summary of its listing says it was used as offices.

Three uses of Wrest follow one another after the de Greys sold in 1917. In the Second World War the Sun Insurance Office moved its head office here from London. After the war the state bought the house and gardens. The institute then took the buildings on a lease. The institute closed in 2006 and English Heritage took over the house and began restoring the gardens.

The institute is best known for four things. It tested farm machinery for the government and manufacturers. It developed a driverless tractor steered by a buried wire. It built one of the first camera-guided field robots, demonstrated in 1996, whose guidance later went into commercial hoes. And it did applied physics: drying crops, spraying, and measuring wind on buildings. Each has its own note.

What is left of the institute? Geograph photographs taken in 2018 show buildings in this part of the park captioned "Industrial buildings in Wrest Park" and "Enterprise House", and a Geograph description says the site now has rentable office space. A search summary also says some research still goes on at Wrest Park behind the scenes, with former institute staff still working there (IAgrE). Former staff keep in touch through the Wrest Park Group, and the Institution of Agricultural Engineers has held celebration days at Wrest Park (25 November 2018 and 14 May 2022). Dates and sources disagree in small ways: see the notes on the institute's arrival (1947 or 1948), the Ministry purchase (1946 or 1949) and the closure (2006 or 2007).

## The institute's history, 1924–2006
Kind: reference
About: niae, niae-origins, niae-closure
The institute began in Oxford. In 1924, as the Institute of Agricultural Engineering at the University of Oxford, it ran farm machinery trials and research into subsoiling, haymaking and crop drying. The Ministry of Agriculture set up an Agricultural Machinery Testing Committee the same year and asked it for testing. It was renamed the Institute for Research in Agricultural Engineering in 1932 and moved in 1934 from St Giles to larger premises in Parks Road.

The Second World War made it central to food production. From 1939 its staff advised the Ministry on tractor numbers, power needs, servicing, training and mechanising crops. In 1942 it became a branch of the Ministry and moved to temporary quarters at Askham Bryan, 4 miles west of York. It was renamed the National Institute of Agricultural Engineering, and twelve staff who made the move formed its core. In the early 1940s testing and training took priority over research, with training aimed at machinery instructors and members of the War Agricultural Executive Committees. The MERL archive keeps a staff magazine, "Furrow Press", from October 1944.

By 1947 the staff numbered 150 and a new home was found at Wrest Park. The institute's own history says the move took place between June 1947 and July 1948; English Heritage gives 1948 as the start of its use, and one timeline gives 1947. The simplest reading is that staff began to arrive in 1947 and the move was complete in 1948.

In 1949 the institute was transferred to the Agricultural Research Council, which meant more long-term research and less routine testing, though tractor testing continued. It became the AFRC (Agriculture and Food Research Council) Institute of Engineering Research in 1986, and was renamed Silsoe Research Institute in 1991 as a more manageable name. In 1994 the new Biotechnology and Biological Sciences Research Council (BBSRC) took over and the institute became one of its eight grant-supported institutes. Work widened to physical, engineering and mathematical methods applied to farming and biological processes.

In 2004 the BBSRC decided to stop supporting it because its own research priorities had changed. The institute ceased operations at the end of March 2006, after 80 years. English Heritage's own history also gives 2006. Wikipedia's article on Silsoe says "until 2007", and Cranfield University's separate Silsoe College (founded as the National College of Agricultural Engineering, at Silsoe from 1963) finished moving to Cranfield by December 2007, which may be the source of that second date. This note takes 2006 for the institute.

The MERL archive of the institute covers 1918–2005 and runs to 49 linear metres.

Sources: Silsoe Research Institute history (silsoeresearch.org.uk/sri-history); Silsoe Research Institute home page (closure); Museum of English Rural Life, SR SRI archive catalogue; English Heritage, History of Wrest Park; Wikipedia, Silsoe; Wikipedia, National College of Agricultural Engineering.

## The driverless tractor and the buried wire
Kind: site
About: driverless-tractor, niae
The family's top interests meet here. The idea was to steer a tractor with no driver by following a signal in the ground, and the story starts at Reading University, not Silsoe.

According to Farmers Weekly's "Machinery milestones", the Reading project began in the mid-1950s on an International Harvester B-250 tractor. Steering followed "an electric current carried in a wire buried in the ground below cultivation depth". Development was at the university's Sonning farm, and the tractor was first shown about 1958 to "an audience of highly sceptical agricultural students". Sensors on the front followed the wire and steered the tractor. A front-mounted emergency stop was triggered by touching an obstruction. Programmed signals sent along the wire could raise or lower the implement linkage at the headland turn, operate the power take-off, or stop the tractor.

How does that work? An alternating current in a long wire produces a magnetic field around it. Pick-up coils on the front of the tractor measure how strong the field is on each side, and the steering moves to keep the two readings balanced, so the wire acts as an invisible rail. That is general physics (magnetic induction), not something the article spells out. The brief calls it a "leader cable"; Farmers Weekly says "guide wire".

Reading then handed the project to the NIAE. Silsoe carried on with the buried-wire system on a Fordson Major tractor that had already been used in a hydrostatic transmission project, and demonstrated the emergency stop with a straw bale. Farmers Weekly gives no dates for the Silsoe phase, so the earlier tour text ("1950s: Silsoe took over") should not be taken as a firm date. The date used in the dates file is that of the Reading start, about 1955.

It was not alone. Auto-Track, a company in Hampshire, developed a buried-wire tractor and showed a prototype at the 1969 Royal Show.

Sources: Farmers Weekly, "Machinery milestones: driverless tractors"; general knowledge (magnetic induction, flagged verify).

## Machine vision and the robot hoe
Kind: site
About: robot-hoe, robocrop-hoe, niae
By the early 1990s the institute had moved from buried wires to cameras. Farmers Weekly says it began a new driverless project then, and demonstrated it in 1996. The vehicle was a small tool-carrier tractor with a 10 horsepower engine. Instead of a wire, steering came from computer analysis of images from a CCTV camera. It was driven by hand to and from the field. In the field it ran up and down crop rows and made 180-degree turns at the headland on its own. It could pick out weeds between the rows and spot-treat them with herbicide, and put fertiliser close to the plants. Its guidance was accurate enough to run hoe blades within 2 centimetres of the crop plants.

A Farmers Weekly report of 28 February 1997 says the experimental spray vehicle had first been demonstrated in summer 1996 and that the Ministry of Agriculture would fund a down-the-row hoe from 1 April 1997. In plot tests on brassica crops, targeted treatment cut herbicide use by 90 per cent compared with blanket spraying. Nick Tillett was the project co-ordinator and expected the first commercial product to be a steerage hoe, a tractor attachment with a driver, with the guidance added. The fully automatic vehicle would come later.

That is what happened. Farmers Weekly (12 May 2000) reports the camera system fitted to a standard steerage hoe: a mass-produced black-and-white video camera, scanned 25 times a second by a Pentium-based computer, finds the crop rows ahead, and the steering keeps the blades between them. In winter wheat trials it cut herbicide use by 80 per cent without reducing yields at about 6 kilometres per hour, a speed limited by the hoe blades rather than the guidance. Professor John Marchant of the institute said a hoe developed with Garford Engineering and Robydome Electronics would be on sale in 2001. A search summary of an IAgrE profile says the Garford Robocrop hoe was first sold in 2001, and that Nick Tillett and Tony Hague set up Tillett and Hague Technology after the closure was announced in 2005, with over 600 machines sold using their computer-vision technology. Farmers Weekly's 'milestones' article adds that the hoped-for commercial driverless row-crop tractor never appeared.

The 1996 demonstration is the one dated in the tour timeline. The research papers by Hague, Marchant and Tillett were not located, so figures here come from press reports.

Sources: Farmers Weekly, "Machinery milestones: driverless tractors"; Farmers Weekly, "MAFF to finance robot weeder development" (28 February 1997); Farmers Weekly, "Auto-guidance hoe cuts down pesticides use" (12 May 2000); Farmers Weekly, "Robot hoe to reduce pesticide use" (11 September 2000); IAgrE profile of Dr Nick Tillett (via search summary, unverified); Tillett and Hague Technology listing.

## Tractor testing and safety cabs
Kind: site
About: tractor-testing, safety-cab-tests, tractor-cab-regulations, niae
Testing was the institute's oldest job. The Ministry of Agriculture set up the Agricultural Machinery Testing Committee in 1924, the year the institute began, and testing was done at first solely for the Ministry. Manufacturers soon wanted independent results to help them develop new machines. The institute's history says tractor testing "always continued in some capacity", from the World Agricultural Tractor Trials of 1930 through the Royal Agricultural Society trials of the 1930s to later NIAE, British Standards Institution and OECD tests. When the institute moved to the Agricultural Research Council in 1949, research grew and testing shrank, but did not stop.

The Museum of English Rural Life holds the institute's archive, with printed test reports on hundreds of machines: grass and grain dryers, potato planters and harvesters, slurry pumps, silage equipment and tractors. The latest tractor report seen in the catalogue is a 1991 Massey-Ferguson 399.

Safety cabs are a good example. The catalogue includes NIAE reports on the Bonser Safety Weather Cab Mk II on a David Brown 990 (1963), the Sta-Dri Mark III cab on a Fordson Super Major (1964), the Duncan Safety Frame and Cab (1964) and a County 1164 tractor fitted with the County Safety Cab (1971). New Zealand safety-frame reports of 1965–1967 say their strength tests used apparatus similar to the NIAE's at Silsoe, and a Spanish official bulletin of 1980 approved a cab on the strength of an OECD test carried out by NIAE (both via search summaries, so verify).

Why did it matter? A tractor can overturn, and a rigid cab or frame keeps the driver from being crushed. The Agriculture (Tractor Cabs) Regulations 1974 require safety cabs, which include safety frames, on tractors driven by agricultural workers, with certificates of approval given by the Minister, and a mark on each approved cab. A "Q" mark means the noise inside is 90 dBA or less. A farming-industry article reports about 45 deaths a year from overturning tractors before 1974, falling sharply after (search summary, verify). Whether NIAE's tests were the basis of the statutory approvals is not confirmed in the sources read: the catalogue shows only that it tested cabs and frames.

Sources: Silsoe Research Institute history; Museum of English Rural Life, SR SRI catalogue (items ET2/1/502, 533, 545, 766, 885); The Agriculture (Tractor Cabs) Regulations 1974, SI 1974/2034, explanatory note (legislation.gov.uk); AGRIS records of Lincoln University (NZ) frame reports; Spanish BOE bulletin of 17 January 1980; FarmingUK, "Wise-up on new tractor law say HSE".

## Crop drying, spray drift and the Silsoe cube
Kind: site
About: crop-drying, spray-drift, silsoe-cube, niae
Three strands of the institute's physics and engineering are worth knowing.

Crop drying goes back to Oxford. The 1924 institute was already researching it, alongside subsoiling and haymaking. Hay and grain stored damp will heat and rot, so warm air is blown through them. The MERL catalogue lists a booklet "Crop Drier Engineering Development" (undated, 1940–1949), "The Testing of Grass Driers" (1950) and test reports on many grain, grass and pyrethrum dryers from the 1950s onwards. The institute also tested dryers made by firms such as Gascoigne, Blanch-Bettison and Penney and Porter.

Spray drift is the sideways carry of fine droplets from a boom sprayer onto neighbouring land. Paul Miller of Silsoe Research Institute presented a 1999 paper on field-boundary drift to the British Crop Protection Council. Its finding, as summarised in the search result, was that drift can be related to the share of the spray volume in small droplets, and that for all nozzle types the height of the boom is critical. A mechanistic "Silsoe Drift Model" was developed by Miller and Hadfield, and a later ground-boom model by Clare Butler-Ellis and Miller. The ways to cut drift that Miller lists are low-drift nozzles, such as air-induction nozzles, and keeping the boom low and steady. Miller later led research at Wrest Park and spoke at the IAgrE's 2022 celebration.

The Silsoe cube is the most famous piece of physics. Researchers at the institute, chiefly Richards and Hoxey (as authors of the papers), built a cube 6 metres on each side in open country on the Silsoe site and measured the wind pressure on its faces and the airflow around it. A cube is simple, symmetrical and can be turned to face the wind at different angles. A 2002 paper by Hoxey, Richards and Short compared full-scale measurements with wind-tunnel results, and found that suction on the roof and side walls with the wind square-on was stronger in the real wind than in most tunnel tests. The cube is now a standard check for wind-tunnel and computer-fluid-dynamics work. A search summary says Birmingham University's Silsoe-Birmingham venture still offers a full-scale test site at Wrest Park. The year it was built and its exact position on the estate were not found, so do not tell visitors where to look.

Miller; Miller, "Pesticide Outlook" 2003 (via search); OECD spray-drift entry (via search); Hoxey, Richards and Short (2002) and Richards and Hoxey (via search summaries, not read); IAgrE event page SRI2020.

## The medieval Greys (1280s–1490)
Kind: site
About: grey-first-record, kent-earldom, edmund-grey, reginald-de-grey, old-house, grey-family
Wrest does not appear under its own name in Domesday Book. The Victoria County History takes the manor to be the larger of two in Silsoe in 1086. It was held by a man called Hugh from Walter, brother of Saher, and was assessed at four hides. A thegn called Leofnoth had held it in Edward the Confessor's time. Bedfordshire Archives adds a mill worth 26 shillings, woodland for 100 pigs, six villagers, eight smallholders and four slaves. That identification is the historians' and not Domesday's own. Between 1086 and 1284 no holder of Wrest is recorded. The VCH says the manor was held of the barony of Wahull, and J. H. Round suggested that the Greys got their Bedfordshire lands by marrying an heiress of the Wahull tenants. John de Grey was a sheriff of the county under Henry III.

The first record of the Greys here is the Feudal Aids of 1284–1286: Reginald de Grey held one fee in Flitton and Silsoe of John de Wahull. When he died in 1307–1308 the papers listed a "capital messuage with a dovecote", 100 acres of arable, meadow, pasture and a wood. The manor was worth £4. The capital messuage is probably the start of the old house. Reginald was summoned to Parliament in 1295 as Lord Grey de Wilton.

His son John, 2nd Baron, was a Lord Ordainer in 1310. In 1311 Wrest was settled on Roger, John's son by his second wife, and the rest of the Bedfordshire property came to Roger when John died in 1323. Roger was summoned as Lord Grey de Ruthyn in 1324 and in about 1344 claimed free warren and a park at Wrest, the first mention of the park. After Roger came Reginald (died 1388, when the manor was worth £13 a year) and a second Reginald, captured in 1402, who died in 1440–1441.

His grandson Edmund Grey, born in 1416, was at first a Lancastrian. At Northampton on 10 July 1460 he went over to the Yorkists. Edward IV made him Lord High Treasurer in 1463 and Earl of Kent on 30 May 1465. Sources disagree about his reward. The VCH says he received Ampthill. Bedfordshire Archives says he bought it in 1446, and the 2nd Earl de Grey doubted the story. The Inventories chapter says he bought Ampthill Castle in 1454, after which Wrest was not the main residence until 1508. He died on 22 May 1490 (the VCH says 1489).

His son George, 2nd Earl, fought at Stoke Field in 1487. The VCH says he was sent to France in 1491 to help "the Emperor Maximilian", the Habsburg ruler. The old house was moated by 1512.
Sources: Victoria County History, Bedford vol. 2, Flitton cum Silsoe; Bedfordshire Archives, Silsoe in 1086, The Manor of Wrest, Wrest Park Before the 17th Century, The Earls of Kent; Cambridge Core abstract of Inventories of Bedfordshire Country Houses, Wrest Park.

## Losing and regaining Wrest (1507–1572)
Kind: site
About: grey-family, kent-earldom
Richard Grey, 3rd Earl of Kent, succeeded in 1503 (the Victoria County History and the Complete Peerage say his father George died on 21 December 1503; Bedfordshire Archives and Wikipedia say 1505). He was a courtier, at the Field of the Cloth of Gold in 1520, and by every account a spendthrift. Bedfordshire Archives quotes the 2nd Earl de Grey's family history: he "wasted the Family property" and mortgaged or sold most of it, including Ruthin, Ampthill and Ashill in Norfolk. Gambling is the usual explanation. Bedfordshire Archives adds that his father George predicted on his deathbed that Richard "will not thrive and will be a waster".

The Inventories of Bedfordshire Country Houses says that in 1507 he was forced to sell Wrest to Sir Henry Wyatt. Wyatt was a Tudor royal councillor and (general knowledge, verify) the father of the poet Sir Thomas Wyatt. Richard died in London in 1524 with no children.

His half-brother Henry (born in 1490 by Bedfordshire Archives) could not afford to take the earldom, so he lived as Sir Henry Grey. The chapter on Wrest says he bought Wrest back in 1512, to stop it being pulled down for its materials. Bedfordshire Archives quotes a document of about 1524, in which Henry says he heard that Wyatt meant to pull down the manor place and sell the timber and tile. Having no house of his own and pitying the loss of the house of his ancestors, he bought "the manor off Wraste" and nearby lands for 450 marks (£300). Bedfordshire Archives gives two dates: 1512 in one page, and in another 28 April 1514, a deed that lists Wrest, Flitton, Pulloxhill, Upper and Lower Gravenhurst, Brobury and Cainhoe, and describes the manors as mortgaged and not sold. So Wrest and its neighbouring manors were recovered. The rest of Richard's lost property mostly was not, and Henry spent years in lawsuits. The VCH skips the Wyatt episode and says only that the manor passed to Henry on Richard's death in 1524.

Henry died in 1562 (Bedfordshire Archives gives 24 September; the Complete Peerage 21 September) and Wrest passed to his grandson Reginald. In 1571 Reginald petitioned Elizabeth I, and in 1572 she recognised him as Earl of Kent. William Camden says the title had "lain asleep" for fifty years. He failed to recover Richard's other lands. He died in 1573 with no children. The 1573 inventory of the house lists rooms from the Dining Chamber to the Clock Chamber.
Sources: Bedfordshire Archives, The Earls of Kent and Wrest Park Before the 17th Century; Cambridge Core abstract of Inventories of Bedfordshire Country Houses 1714–1830, Wrest Park; Wikisource, Complete Peerage vol. 4 p. 352; Wikipedia, Richard Grey, 3rd Earl of Kent and Reginald Grey, 5th Earl of Kent (quoting Camden); VCH Bedford vol. 2.

## The Earls of Kent, 1572–1702
Kind: site
About: grey-family, kent-earldom, anthony-11th-earl
After Reginald (died 1573) the earldom went to his brothers. Henry, 6th Earl (born in 1541, died 1615), was one of the commissioners who sentenced Mary, Queen of Scots, in 1587, and was present at her execution. He built the de Grey Mausoleum at Flitton church, where the family were buried until 1859. Charles, 7th Earl, followed in 1615 and died in 1623.

Henry, 8th Earl (born about 1583), was a friend of the lawyer John Selden. He married Elizabeth Talbot in 1601 and died on 21 November 1639 with no children. The VCH gives 1631, which is wrong. The title passed to a distant cousin, Anthony, 9th Earl, a country clergyman, rector of Aston Flamville, who was 82 when he succeeded and died in 1643.

His son Henry, 10th Earl (born 1594), backed Parliament in the Civil War. He was a Speaker of the House of Lords until it was abolished in February 1649. He died on 28 May 1651. His widow Amabel Benn, the "Good Countess", born in 1607 and died on 29 August 1698, ran the estate for her young son and helped pay for the old house's classical north front. A sculpture on the Flitton tomb shows her effigy higher than her husband's.

Anthony, 11th Earl (born 11 June 1645), became Earl at six. He married Mary Lucas on 2 March 1663; on 6 or 7 May 1663 she was made Baroness Lucas of Crudwell. Her father had bought the title with an unusual rule, so that it could descend through women. When her father died on 2 July 1671 she inherited a large estate in Wiltshire and Essex. Their son Henry was baptised on 28 September 1671. Their money paid for the north front, the Long Water and the first formal gardens. Anthony died in August 1702 (Bedfordshire Archives gives both 9 and 19 August) and Mary a few months later.

Numbering differs. This guide follows Bedfordshire Archives and Wikipedia, where Anthony is the 11th Earl and his son Henry the 12th. The VCH counts one fewer.
Sources: Bedfordshire Archives, The Earls of Kent, Wrest Park in the 17th Century and The Barons and Baronesses Lucas of Crudwell; Wikipedia, Henry Grey, 8th Earl of Kent; VCH Bedford vol. 2.

## Heiresses: Jemima, Amabel and the de Greys
Kind: site
About: jemima, amabel-de-grey, earl-de-grey, grey-family
Henry, Duke of Kent, died on 5 June 1740 with no surviving sons: nine of his eleven children died before him. Wrest went to his granddaughter Jemima Campbell. Her mother Amabel was the Duke's eldest daughter; her father John Campbell, later Earl of Breadalbane, was British envoy at Copenhagen, where Bedfordshire Archives says she was born on 9 October 1722. Wikipedia, The Peerage and the Early Modern Letters database say 1723. Sources disagree, and I could not settle it.

In 1740 the Duke was made Marquess de Grey with a special remainder to Jemima, so a woman could hold the title. She married Philip Yorke on 22 May 1740, two weeks before her grandfather died. One source suggests she inherited on condition of marrying him. I found nothing in the sources I read to support that, so treat it as unconfirmed. Yorke became 2nd Earl of Hardwicke in 1764 and died in 1790. They lived at Wimpole in Cambridgeshire and at Wrest. Jemima died on 10 or 11 January 1797 (sources differ).

The marquessate then died out, but the Lucas barony, which can pass through women, went to her elder daughter Amabel (born January 1751). She was widowed in 1781 and had no children. Created Countess de Grey on 25 October 1816, with a special remainder to her sister's male heirs, she left Wrest to her nephew in 1833. She died on 4 March 1833 (Wikipedia; Bedfordshire Archives says 4 May).

Thomas Philip, 2nd Earl de Grey, was the son of her younger sister Mary Jemima and Lord Grantham. He took the name Weddell in 1803 and de Grey in 1833. The foundation stone of his new house was laid on 12 February 1834 and the family moved in autumn 1839. The earldom de Grey itself could pass only in the male line, so it went to another nephew, George Robinson, later Marquess of Ripon. He did not inherit Wrest. The barony of Lucas, which can pass to a daughter, and Wrest went to the Earl's daughter Anne Florence.
Sources: Bedfordshire Archives, Jemima Marchioness Grey, The Earls and Countesses de Grey of Wrest, The Duke of Kent; VCH Bedford vol. 2; Wikipedia, Jemima Yorke, 2nd Marchioness Grey and Amabel Hume-Campbell; Early Modern Letters Online (Swansea), Jemima Campbell Yorke.

## The Cowpers and Lucases
Kind: site
About: anne-florence-cowper, francis-cowper, lord-lucas, whitelaw-reid, grey-family
Anne Florence (born 8 June 1806) inherited Wrest and the Lucas barony in 1859, aged 53. She had married George Cowper in 1833 and became Countess Cowper when he succeeded as 6th Earl in 1837; he died in 1856. Wrest was no longer the family's main home from then: the Cowpers lived at Panshanger in Hertfordshire. She died on 23 July 1880.

Her son Francis Thomas de Grey Cowper, 7th Earl Cowper (born 11 June 1834), was Lord Lieutenant of Bedfordshire from 1861 and of Ireland from 1880 to 1882. He died at Panshanger on 22 July 1905 with no children. He held 37,869 acres in ten counties, 9,105 of them in Bedfordshire, and his will was valued at £1,079,000.

The barony and Wrest went to his nephew Auberon Herbert, "Bron" (born 25 May 1876), 9th Baron Lucas. Whitelaw Reid had been appointed American ambassador in June 1905, and Lucas let him Wrest that year at £1,500 a year. Reid stayed for about eight years. Bedfordshire Archives says he died at Wrest; Wikipedia, citing the New York Times, says he died at Dorchester House in London on 15 December 1912.

King Edward VII spent the weekend of 24–25 July 1909 at Wrest as Reid's guest. The Bedfordshire Times called it the first visit by a sovereign. Among the guests invited to meet him was the Austrian ambassador. Crowds lined the road, and the King went to church at Silsoe and looked round Flitton church and mausoleum.

Lucas lost a foot as a Boer War correspondent. He was Under-Secretary for War from 1908 to 1911 and President of the Board of Agriculture from August 1914 to May 1915. He then joined the Royal Flying Corps and was killed on 3 November 1916. He was unmarried. His sister Nan inherited.

Numbering: Bedfordshire Archives and Wikipedia call Bron the 9th Baron and Nan the 10th. English Heritage often calls them 8th and 9th. The difference is whether Anthony, Earl of Harold (died 1723), who held the barony by writ in his father's lifetime, is counted.
Sources: Bedfordshire Archives, The Barons and Baronesses Lucas of Crudwell, Wrest Park in the 20th Century, King Edward VII at Wrest Park; Wikipedia, Whitelaw Reid; Parks & Gardens UK, Wrest Park (Historic England register 1000113); English Heritage, Auberon Herbert (members' magazine).

## Hospital, fire and sale (1914–1917)
Kind: site
About: wrest-sale, lord-lucas, nan-herbert, wrest-hospital, wrest-fire
Bron Lucas offered Wrest as a hospital in August 1914, just after he joined the Cabinet. It was ready for its first patients on 7 September 1914 (English Heritage). It began as a convalescent home and became a base hospital in November 1914. Unlike most auxiliary hospitals it was not run by the Red Cross or the Order of St John but privately, and Lucas paid for it. His sister Nan Herbert set it up, trained at the Metropolitan Hospital in London, and became matron in February 1915. English Heritage calls it "one of the best-run country house hospitals".

About 1,600 men passed through it. It could hold up to 200 patients, and had a medical officer who could call on three London surgeons and about 20–25 nurses (over 100 in all served there). A-Ward, for the worst cases, was in the ground-floor reception rooms, B-Ward in the first-floor bedrooms and C-Ward in the service wing. The operating theatre was in a first-floor bedroom over the north side of the entrance hall, with an X-ray room. Soldiers came by train to Ampthill and were bathed in the "louse house" before the wards.

The fire was on Thursday 14 September 1916. English Heritage says it badly damaged the mansion and that all 156 patients were got out safely, with beds wheeled onto the terrace and into the garden. A local-history account says it started in the east wing (the service wing), ten fire brigades attended and Royal Engineers came from Haynes Park. No-one was hurt, and Ampthill recruits helped save furniture and paintings. About 50 patients were moved at around midnight to Woburn Abbey hospital and huts at Ampthill Camp. The damage from fire, smoke and water was estimated at £20,000. The 1917 sale catalogue says the house needed a new roof because of "the recent fire". I found no source for the cause. The damage was too serious to reopen the hospital. Nurses stayed to help clean up.

Six weeks later, on 3 November 1916, Bron was killed. Nan succeeded him and married Lt-Col Howard Lister Cooper on 30 April 1917. Why the family sold is not stated outright. The sources give three reasons together: Bron's death, the fire damage and the burden of such a large house.
Sources: English Heritage, Wrest Park at War, First World War nurse identified, and the nurses page; Tommy's Footprints, Wrest Park Military Hospital (a local-history site, quoting the Bedfordshire Standard of 22 September 1916; not independently checked); Bedfordshire Archives, Wrest Park in the 19th Century (1917 sale catalogue) and Wrest Park in the 20th Century.

## The 1917 sale and what happened to the contents and statues
Kind: site
About: wrest-sale, contents-auction, lord-lucas
Who bought Wrest? Bedfordshire Archives says a sale by auction was put in hand in 1917 (the Silsoe local history says it was to be at the Mart, Tokenhouse Yard, London, on 17 July 1917), but in the event the house was sold privately to John George Murray of Thirsk. The Bedfordshire Archives catalogue says Wrest was sold in July 1917. Another Bedfordshire Archives page says the estate was sold off in 1918 and 1919, with Murray taking the bulk. Whether those were separate parcels is unclear.

Murray was born in 1864 at Consett, County Durham. By Bedfordshire Archives he was chairman of Associated Breweries and North Eastern Breweries, and of several north-eastern coal companies including Seaton Burn, North Walbottle and Redheugh Colliery. So the usual description, a brewing and coal businessman, is right. He was High Sheriff of Bedfordshire in 1923.

The contents were sold separately. English Heritage's "Lost and Found" says they went in a five-day auction in September 1917. The catalogue of "the valuable remaining contents of the mansion" by Messrs Foster, on the premises, began on 10 September 1917 and ran four more days. "Remaining" matters: Bron had sold some furnishings before the war, and the manuscripts had been dispersed. Nan gave the British Museum a collection of over 3,000 prints in memory of Bron, and later gave fossils to the Natural History Museum. Most furniture and porcelain was sold, so most rooms are now empty. The Huysmans portrait of the three Crew sisters left at the sale and was bought by English Heritage in 2015. The hall's eleven portraits were excluded from the sale and offered separately, and in 1939 the Sun Insurance bought them.

The statues mostly did not go in 1917. The sources date their loss to Murray's time in the 1930s. In 1933 he moved to Coles Park in Hertfordshire, taking many statues and ornaments, including the 1682 sundial. The Four Seasons and Four Elements went to Ditchley Park, and are still missing. The Duke of Kent's obelisk and the Duchess's column went to Trent Park in about 1934 (from a search summary of the Historic England entry; not opened). The Ministry of Works bought back Italian Garden edging in 1950 and the sundial in 1954 when Coles Park was pulled down. The Diana statue stayed with Murray's descendants and came back in 2011–2012 with Art Fund help. Murray also removed the Rose Garden, and many old trees were felled in the 1920s to 1940s. Earlier still, lead statues were melted down in 1809 or around then (Art Fund) or by Countess Amabel for roof repairs (English Heritage).
Sources: Bedfordshire Archives, Wrest Park in the 20th Century, The Manor of Wrest, catalogue entry for the Wrest Park [Lucas] Archive, Wrest Park in the 19th Century; Wikishire, Silsoe; English Heritage on Google Arts & Culture, Wrest Park: Lost and Found; Wikipedia, Nan Ino Cooper, 10th Baroness Lucas (citing the 1917 catalogue and The Times, 20 December 1917) and Wrest Park; Art Fund, Figure of Diana.

## After the family (1917–today)
Kind: site
About: grey-family, sun-insurance, ministry-purchase, niae, eh-takeover, gardens-reopened, archaeology-store
John George Murray lived at Wrest until the 1930s. A 1927 valuer found the house modernised, but the grounds "too big and not kept up well". In 1932 he advertised it as a school for at least 250 pupils, an institution or a country club, and failed to sell it. He had left for Coles Park by 1933 (English Heritage) though Kelly's Directory had him at Wrest in 1936.

In June 1939 the Sun Insurance Office bought the mansion and about 260 acres of the roughly 500-acre estate for £25,000, expecting war. Murray kept the rest. Sun built twelve huts and converted the stables for its staff, and finished the alterations on 4 September 1940. Bedfordshire Archives says Sun moved back to London in late 1945 and early 1946.

Then the state bought it. The date is disputed. Geograph descriptions echoing the Historic England entry say 1946; Bedfordshire Archives says October 1949, and about 1946 in its timeline. The National Institute of Agricultural Engineering moved in between June 1947 and July 1948, and a 1960 Commons answer says the Ministry of Works began restoring the garden buildings in September 1949. The buyer was the Ministry of Works (the register calls it the Ministry of Public Building and Works, a later name). In 1960 the house and gardens were leased to the British Society for Research in Agricultural Engineering and occupied by the institute. From 1954 the historic gardens opened to the public on summer weekends. The institute (renamed Silsoe Research Institute in 1991) closed at the end of March 2006.

English Heritage took over and began a 20-year plan to restore the gardens to how they were before 1917. The Wolfson Foundation pledged up to £400,000 in autumn 2007 and the Heritage Lottery Fund gave over £1 million in 2010. The restored gardens reopened on 4 August 2011. In 2014 a £2 million store for English Heritage's archaeological collections opened in the grounds, holding over 150,000 objects from sites such as Kirby Hall, Audley End and Hill Hall. English Heritage looks after the house and gardens for the state.
Sources: Bedfordshire Archives, Wrest Park in the 20th Century and Timeline of Events in Silsoe; Silsoe Research Institute history; Hansard, written answer "Wrest Park, Bedfordshire", 16 February 1960; Geograph, shared description 7382; Wikipedia, Wrest Park (citing BBC News, The Guardian and the Heritage Fund); English Heritage, History of Wrest Park; Parks & Gardens UK, Wrest Park.

## Auberon Herbert, 9th Baron Lucas
Kind: person
About: lord-lucas
Auberon "Bron" Herbert (born 25 May 1876) was the son of Auberon Edward William Molyneux Herbert and Florence Amabel Cowper, and nephew of the 7th Earl Cowper. He was educated at Bedford School and Balliol College, Oxford, rowed for Oxford, and lost a foot in the Boer War as a war correspondent. As a Liberal minister he was Under-Secretary of State for War (1908–1911) and was President of the Board of Agriculture from 6 August 1914. He inherited Wrest in 1905 and let it to Whitelaw Reid. He established a Shorthorn herd at the Home Farm.

In August 1914 he offered Wrest as a hospital. He resigned in May 1915 to join the Royal Flying Corps, trained as an observer and got his pilot's certificate in September 1915. He was in France from October 1916. On 3 November 1916 he was flying near Haplincourt, shot down by a German fighter, and died of his wounds the same day aged 40. His observer, Anderson, was captured. He is buried at the HAC Cemetery, Ecoust-St Mein. He died unmarried and his sister Nan inherited the barony and Wrest.
Sources: Bedfordshire Archives, The Barons and Baronesses Lucas of Crudwell and Wrest Park in the 20th Century; English Heritage members' magazine, Auberon Herbert.

## Nan Herbert, Baroness Lucas
Kind: person
About: nan-herbert, wrest-hospital
Nan Ino Herbert (born 13 June 1880, died 1958) was Bron's sister. Before the First World War she had been involved in theosophy and a school in Cuba. In 1914 she set up Wrest as a hospital and recruited the nurses, which was hard in wartime. Her diary called some early recruits an unsuitable mix. She became matron in February 1915 after a crash course at the Metropolitan Hospital, and later recalled beds being wheeled onto the terrace in the 1916 fire.

She succeeded as 10th Baroness Lucas (9th by English Heritage's count) in November 1916 and married Lt-Col Howard Lister Cooper on 30 April 1917. She sold Wrest that year. She gave over 3,000 prints to the British Museum in Bron's memory and fossils to the Natural History Museum, and in 1936 deposited the Wrest papers with the Bedfordshire record office. Her daughter Anne Rosemary Palmer (born 1919) inherited the barony.
Sources: English Heritage, Wrest Park nurses page and Wrest Park at War; Bedfordshire Archives, The Barons and Baronesses Lucas of Crudwell and catalogue entry for the Wrest Park [Lucas] Archive; Wikipedia, Nan Ino Cooper, 10th Baroness Lucas.
