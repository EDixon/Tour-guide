## Amun and Amun-Ra, king of the gods
Kind: reference
About: god-amun, god-min
Amun's name means "the hidden one". He is a god of the unseen: the breath of the wind, the power behind the sun, the creator nobody can see. He first appears in the Pyramid Texts (about 2350 BC) as one of eight primeval beings, the Ogdoad, with his female partner Amunet. Scholars still argue about where he began. Some see a local Theban god, others a Hermopolitan creator god brought south. Either way he was minor until Thebes became important.
His rise followed his city's. The Eleventh Dynasty kings of Thebes (about 2100 BC) reunited Egypt, and the Twelfth Dynasty founder Amenemhat I (1985 BC, in Shaw's chronology) put Amun in his own name: "Amun is at the head". After the Theban prince Ahmose drove out the Hyksos (about 1550 BC), Amun became the national god. By then he had merged with the sun god Ra as Amun-Ra, "king of the gods": hidden power joined to the visible sun.
How to spot him: usually a man, sometimes with blue skin (probably for the sky and air, which can be felt but not grasped), wearing a flat-topped crown with two very tall plumes. He also appears as a ram, or a man with a ram's head, and as a goose. The ram stood for fertility and strength, and ram-headed sphinx statues guard his temple approaches, including at Karnak.
Amun-Min-Kamutef: Amun could also take the form of the fertility god Min, shown with an upright arm holding a flail and an erect phallus. "Kamutef" means "bull of his mother", a god who renews creation by being his own father. At festivals this form linked Amun to the harvest.
Amun's fame went far. Nubia worshipped him, and Greeks identified him with Zeus as "Zeus-Ammon". Alexander the Great visited his oracle at Siwa in 331 BC. His name was hacked out of temples under Akhenaten and restored afterwards.
Sources: Wilkinson, The Complete Gods and Goddesses of Ancient Egypt (2003); Pinch, Egyptian Mythology (2002); Hornung, Conceptions of God in Ancient Egypt (1982); Shaw (ed.), Oxford History of Ancient Egypt (2000) for reign dates; Kockelmann, UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology (UEE), "Amun" (2018), for the debate about his origins; Met Museum, Heilbrunn Timeline, "Amun".

## Mut, the vulture mother of Thebes
Kind: reference
About: god-mut, theban-triad
Mut's name means "mother", and the Egyptian word for vulture was written with the same sign, so she is the vulture mother. Vultures were thought to be devoted mothers (Horapollo, a writer of about the fifth century AD, says vultures were thought to have no males, which would fit her as a mother figure, though he is not a reliable guide). She is shown as a woman in a long dress wearing a vulture headdress, usually topped by the double crown of Upper and Lower Egypt. She sometimes holds a papyrus sceptre, and can be shown with a lion's head, or even with a male body, as a mother of everything.
She is attested earlier, but she became a great goddess in the New Kingdom (from about 1550 BC) as the wife of Amun-Ra. This made a family, the Theban triad: Amun the father, Mut the mother and the moon god Khonsu their child. Their statues travelled together in boats on the Nile at great festivals. A triad (a father, a mother and a son) was the usual way Egyptians arranged a local god family. Memphis had Ptah, Sekhmet and Nefertem, and Elephantine had Khnum, Satet and Anuket.
Mut's own temple stands south of the main Karnak temples, built around a horseshoe-shaped sacred lake called Isheru. It is a rare temple with a lake wrapped around three sides. Mut was also linked with the fierce lioness goddesses. Hundreds of Sekhmet statues stood in her precinct, and the king was said to be nursed by her: kings often appear suckled by goddesses.
Her titles included "mistress of heaven" and "lady of Isheru", and when Amun was "king of the gods" she was his queen.
Sources: Wilkinson (2003); te Velde, "Mut" in Lexikon der Ägyptologie; Pinch (2002); Met Museum, Heilbrunn Timeline; Wilkinson, The Complete Temples of Ancient Egypt (2000), for the Mut precinct. Checked online: Foster, Classical Quarterly (Cambridge), on vulture signs in Horapollo and Ammianus; Wikipedia, Precinct of Mut.

## Khonsu, the traveller, moon god and child of the triad
Kind: reference
About: god-khonsu, theban-triad
Khonsu's name means "the traveller", for the Moon crossing the night sky. He is the son of Amun and Mut in the Theban triad. He appears in the Pyramid Texts (about 2350 BC) as a dangerous god, but by the New Kingdom he is gentle and youthful. The moon also counted the months, so he is linked with time and with the length of a king's reign.
How to spot him: he is shown as a mummiform young man, with the sidelock of youth, wearing a crescent moon cradling a full disc on his head. He holds a crook and flail, and often a combined was, djed and ankh sceptre. He can also be a falcon-headed god with a moon disc, or a baboon, an animal linked to the moon and Thoth.
He was a healer and protector. The Bentresh Stela (found at Karnak in 1829, now in the Louvre) is set in the reign of Ramesses II but was composed much later, and scholars disagree whether in the Persian period or the early Ptolemaic period (roughly 525 to 300 BC). It tells how a statue of Khonsu the Provider in Thebes was sent to a distant land called Bakhtan (perhaps Bactria or the Hittite land) to cure a princess, and then kept there for years until a dream told the king of Bakhtan to send it home. Khonsu was also invoked in childbirth and against evil spirits.
Sources: Wilkinson (2003); Pinch (2002); Met Museum, Heilbrunn Timeline; Lichtheim, Ancient Egyptian Literature III (1980) for the Bentresh Stela; Hornung (1982). The Karnak site section "Khonsu the moon god" covers his temple there. Checked online: Wikipedia, Khonsu (sidelock, lunar disc in crescent, menat necklace, Cannibal Hymn in the Pyramid Texts); Wikipedia, Bentresh stela (disputed date, Louvre C 284, found 1829, Bakhtan).

## Montu, the falcon war god of Thebes
Kind: reference
About: god-montu
Before Amun was famous, Thebes belonged to Montu. He is a god of war and the king's strength in battle, shown as a falcon or a man with a falcon's head, crowned with a sun disc, two tall plumes and two cobras. He could also be a bull, the Buchis bull of Armant, his main home just south of Thebes. His other temples included Medamud and Tod, and he had a precinct on the north side of Karnak.
The early Theban kings of the Eleventh Dynasty (about 2100 to 1990 BC) honoured him as family patron: several are named Montuhotep, "Montu is satisfied". They beat their northern rivals and reunited Egypt under him. In the New Kingdom, warrior kings such as Thutmose III and Ramesses II were praised as being "like Montu" in battle. Kings are shown with Montu giving them a sword or a victory, or standing behind them as they strike enemies.
As Amun rose, Montu did not vanish, he was folded in. He took the name Montu-Ra, linking him to the sun, and became Amun's fighting arm, the god who lets Egypt win its wars.
Sources: Wilkinson (2003); Pinch (2002); Shaw (2000); Met Museum, "Montu" in collection notes; Hornung (1982); UEE, "Montu".

## Ptah, Sekhmet and the Eye of Ra
Kind: reference
About: god-ptah, god-sekhmet, god-hathor, eye-of-ra-myth
Ptah was the god of Memphis, the capital of Old Kingdom Egypt. He is the patron of craftsmen, sculptors and builders. In the Memphite Theology, carved on the Shabaka Stone (about 700 BC, copying something older), he creates the world by thinking with his heart and speaking with his tongue. He is shown as a man wrapped like a mummy, with a close-fitting cap, holding a tall sceptre combining the was, djed and ankh. Some think "Egypt" comes from the Greek form of Hut-ka-Ptah, "the mansion of Ptah's spirit", a name for Memphis.
His wife is Sekhmet, "the powerful one", a woman with the head of a lioness, usually with a sun disc and a cobra. She is a goddess of war and plague, and also of healing: her priests were doctors. Amenhotep III set up hundreds of her statues, somewhere between about 600 and 730 (estimates vary; the Met says over six hundred), in his Theban temple. Their son was Nefertem, a god of the lotus flower.
The Eye of Ra: in a story known as the Destruction of Mankind, best preserved in the tombs of Sety I and Ramesses II, the aged sun god Ra heard humans plotting against him. He sent his fierce Eye, in the form of Hathor, who became Sekhmet, to punish them. She began to slaughter. Ra then regretted it. To save the rest, he had beer dyed red with ochre poured over the fields. Sekhmet thought it was blood, drank it, became drunk and calm, and woke as gentle Hathor. The people were saved. The story was said to explain a festival of drinking for Hathor.
Hathor is the gentle side: a goddess of joy, music, love and motherhood, shown as a cow, or a woman with cow ears or horns holding a sun disc, and shaking a rattle called a sistrum.
Sources: Wilkinson (2003); Pinch (2002); Lichtheim, Ancient Egyptian Literature I (1973) and III (1980); British Museum collection pages on Sekhmet statues; Hornung, Conceptions of God (1982).  Checked online: British Museum collection page EA79 and Met Museum object 544484 (Amenhotep III's statues, originally from his mortuary temple, many later moved to the Mut temple); Wikipedia, Sekhmet statues (estimates up to 730); World History Encyclopedia, Book of the Heavenly Cow (red ochre in beer; best-attested versions in the tombs of Sety I and Ramesses II). I could not confirm the Tutankhamun attestation or any pomegranate reading, so both are left out.

## Ra and the journey of the sun
Kind: reference
About: god-ra, god-khepri, solar-cycle
Ra (also written Re) is the sun god, and for much of Egyptian history the greatest god. His cult centred at Heliopolis, near modern Cairo. From the Fourth Dynasty (about 2600 BC) the king was called "son of Ra". In the Fifth Dynasty, kings built sun temples centred on a squat obelisk, the same shape later raised at Karnak.
The Egyptians saw the sun's daily path as a voyage. By day Ra sails across the sky in the Day Boat, the Mandjet. At sunset he enters the underworld, the Duat, in the Night Boat, the Meseket. During the twelve hours of night he passes twelve gates, bringing light and life to the dead. His great enemy is the giant serpent Apophis, who tries to stop the boat, and the gods on board fight it off every night. At dawn Ra is reborn.
Ra took different forms through the day: Khepri, the scarab beetle, at dawn (it rolls a ball of dung, as the beetle rolls the sun); Ra himself at midday as a falcon-headed man with a sun disc and a cobra; and Atum, an old man, at sunset. He merged with other gods, such as Amun-Ra and Ra-Horakhty, "Ra, Horus of the two horizons", who is a falcon-headed man with a sun disc.
Ra's golden age came at the beginning of time, when he ruled gods and humans. Eventually he grew old and left for the sky, leaving the king to rule on earth. The sun's rebirth every dawn made Ra the model for the king and for the dead, who hoped to ride in his boat.
Sources: Wilkinson (2003); Pinch (2002); Hornung, Conceptions of God (1982); Hornung, The Ancient Egyptian Books of the Afterlife (1999); Shaw (2000) for dates; British Museum, "Ra".

## Horus, Osiris and Isis: the king and the dead king
Kind: reference
About: god-horus, god-osiris, god-isis, osiris-myth
Horus is the falcon god of the sky, whose two eyes were the sun and moon. Every living king was Horus on earth: the first of the king's five names, his "Horus name", was written in a frame showing a falcon. The healed Eye of Horus, the wedjat, became a charm for protection and wholeness.
The story comes from fragments, with the fullest telling in Plutarch, a Greek writer around AD 100. Osiris, a good and generous king, was murdered by his jealous brother Seth, god of storms and chaos. His wife Isis, with her sister Nephthys, found his body. Isis used her magic to conceive their son Horus. In later temple scenes she hovers over him as a kite, a bird of mourning that the Pyramid Texts already link to Isis and Nephthys. She hid the child in the Delta marshes, where he grew up to challenge Seth. Their battles went on for years, and in some versions Horus lost an eye, restored by Thoth. The gods judged in Horus's favour. Seth was beaten, or became the god of the desert and foreign lands.
So the system fits together: the living king is Horus, the dead king becomes Osiris, ruler of the underworld, and the new king is Horus again. By the Middle Kingdom ordinary people hoped to become "an Osiris" after death. Osiris is shown as a mummiform man with green or black skin (the colour of new growth and of rich Nile mud) wearing the atef crown, holding crook and flail. His main cult centre was Abydos.
Isis, whose name means "throne", is a woman with a throne sign on her head, or with a sun disc and cow horns. She is the model wife and mother, and a great magician, who knew Ra's secret name. Her worship spread across the Roman Empire.
Sources: Wilkinson (2003); Pinch (2002); Griffiths, Plutarch's De Iside et Osiride (1970); Met Museum, Heilbrunn Timeline and collection notes on Osiris and Isis; Hornung (1982). Checked online: Graham, Birmingham Egyptology paper on Isis and Nekhbet (Isis and Nephthys as kites from the Pyramid Texts); Wikipedia, Osiris myth (earliest mentions in the Pyramid Texts, end of the Fifth Dynasty, 24th century BC).

## The Aten and Akhenaten's religion
Kind: reference
About: god-aten
The Aten was the visible disc of the sun. The word existed long before Akhenaten, but under Amenhotep III (about 1390 to 1352 BC) it was becoming popular. His son Amenhotep IV began as a worshipper of Aten and Ra-Horakhty. In his fifth year (about 1348 BC in Shaw's chronology) he changed his name to Akhenaten, "effective for the Aten". He moved to a new city, Akhetaten (modern Amarna), and tried to make the Aten the only god worshipped.
The Aten is shown as a plain disc with rays ending in small hands, which hold ankh signs to the noses of the king and queen. This is a very different picture from older gods. There is no animal head, no statue: only the sun. Temples were open to the sky. Ordinary people prayed to the royal family, who alone spoke to the god. The Great Hymn to the Aten praises the sun that makes everything live, and its likeness to Psalm 104 was noticed long ago. The god's name was put in two cartouches, like a king.
Scholars argue about what this was. Some call it monotheism, the first in history. Others prefer terms such as "monolatry" (one god worshipped, others not denied) or point out how much Akhenaten kept from older solar religion. Amun was singled out: his name was cut out of monuments across Egypt, Karnak among them. Akhenaten died about 1336 BC, and within a few years the old gods returned. Tutankhamun, whose name began with the Aten, "living image of the Aten", changed it to include Amun, and the Aten's temples were pulled down.
Sources: Wilkinson (2003); Hornung, Akhenaten and the Religion of Light (1999); Redford, Akhenaten: The Heretic King (1984); Shaw (2000); British Museum, "Akhenaten" and Amarna collection pages; Met Museum, Heilbrunn Timeline, "Amarna Period". The Karnak sections "Akhenaten and the restoration" give one site's view.

## Why Egyptian gods have animal heads
Kind: reference
About: god-khepri, god-thoth, god-anubis, god-wadjet-nekhbet, animal-cults
Egyptians did not think their gods were animals. The animal stood for qualities the god possessed, as a kind of picture-writing. A god could appear as a person, an animal, or a mix. The mixes are the gods we see on walls, and a god could have several forms (Horus is a falcon, a boy, a man with a falcon head).
The choices came from watching nature. The falcon soars highest, so Horus and Ra rule the sky. The jackal prowled the desert cemeteries, so Anubis, a jackal or jackal-headed man, became guardian of the dead and of embalming. The ibis and the baboon were linked to Thoth, scribe of the gods, who gave writing and measured time. The ram suggested fertility and power: Amun. The lioness was fierce, like Sekhmet. The scarab beetle, Khepri, rolls a ball of dung, and young beetles emerge from it, so it stood for the sun rising and for new life. The cobra rears up and spits: Wadjet, goddess of Lower Egypt, protected the king as the uraeus on his brow. The vulture, Nekhbet of Upper Egypt, shielded the king from above. Together they are "the Two Ladies", a title in the king's names.
Animal cults: Some animals were thought to host the god on earth and lived in temples. The Apis bull of Memphis (Ptah) was mummified and buried with huge honour. The Buchis bull of Armant belonged to Montu. In the last centuries of Egyptian religion (after about 700 BC) pilgrims paid for mummified ibises, cats, dogs and baboons as offerings. Huge animal catacombs held millions of them, a trade as well as a devotion.
Sources: Wilkinson (2003); Pinch (2002); Hornung, Conceptions of God (1982) on "many forms"; Ikram (ed.), Divine Creatures: Animal Mummies in Ancient Egypt (2005); British Museum, animal mummies collection pages; Met Museum, Heilbrunn Timeline, "Animals in Egyptian Art". Checked online: Wikipedia, Pyramid Texts (Thoth and Anubis among the Hermopolitan names; Sekhmet among the Memphite ones). Not confirmed online: the first attestation of Khepri, Wadjet and Nekhbet.

## Maat, and the king's job of keeping order
Kind: reference
About: god-maat, kingship-maat
Maat means truth, justice, balance and the right order of the world: the way things should be. She is also a goddess, a woman with a single ostrich feather on her head, sitting or kneeling. Her opposite is isfet: chaos, injustice and disorder, which was always trying to creep back in. The sun god's nightly fight with Apophis shows the same contest.
The king's most important job was to keep Maat. Egyptians believed the gods built the world in order and that the king, as their link on earth, had to keep it so, by justice, by defending Egypt, and above all by looking after the gods' temples. In relief after relief the king appears alone before a god, offering food, drink, incense, linen, or a small figure of Maat herself. This "offering of Maat" repeats the first act of creation. In truth, priests did the daily rituals in the king's name, but the walls always show the king.
This explains the famous scene of the king smashing the heads of foreign enemies: the enemies stand for chaos, and the king restores order. It also explains throne names: Ramesses II took "Usermaatre", "powerful of Maat is Ra", and Hatshepsut "Maatkare".
Maat also judged individuals. In the Book of the Dead's Weighing of the Heart, the dead person's heart is weighed against Maat's feather on a balance watched by Anubis, with Thoth writing down the result. A heart weighed down by wrongdoing is eaten by the monster Ammit. The Egyptians called this "the hall of the two Maats".
Sources: Wilkinson (2003); Pinch (2002); Assmann, Ma'at (1990) and The Search for God in Ancient Egypt (2001); Hornung, Conceptions of God (1982); Teeter, UEE, "Maat" (2010) is a freely available overview; British Museum, "Book of the Dead of Hunefer".

## How to recognise gods on the walls
Kind: reference
About: god-amun, god-ra, god-osiris, god-horus
Egyptian artists drew gods by a code. If you can read the crowns, you can name almost everyone. Inscriptions beside figures give the names, but a quick key helps.
Quick key:
- Ram head or man with two tall plumes on a flat crown: Amun. A blue-skinned man can be Amun, and a man with raised arm and flail is Amun-Min.
- Falcon head with sun disc and cobra: Ra or Ra-Horakhty. Falcon head with the double crown: Horus. Falcon with two tall plumes: Montu.
- Lion head with disc: Sekhmet. Cow ears or horns with disc: Hathor.
- Vulture headdress: Mut (queens also wear a vulture cap, and the vulture goddess Nekhbet is shown as a whole bird).
- Crescent moon with disc: Khonsu. A boy with a sidelock too.
- Mummy-wrapped man with tight cap: Ptah. Mummy-wrapped with green or black skin and atef crown (tall white crown with a feather on each side): Osiris.
- Throne sign on the head: Isis. A single feather: Maat. Ibis head or baboon: Thoth. Jackal head: Anubis. Beetle: Khepri.
- Sun disc with rays ending in hands: the Aten.
Crowns for kings: white crown (Upper Egypt), red crown (Lower Egypt), both together (double crown), and a blue crown, used for war.
Symbols: the ankh, a loop-topped cross, means life, and gods hold it to the king's nose. The was sceptre, a staff with a forked foot and an animal head, means power. The djed pillar, like four stacked bars on a column, means stability and is Osiris's backbone. Men are drawn with darker skin and women paler, as a convention.
Toddler game: "Spot the ram: curly horns by his ears. Spot the falcon: a beak and a round sun on his head. Spot the lion lady, the beetle and the jackal." Then count the ankh signs, the little loops of life.
Sources: Wilkinson (2003), plus the god entries; Wilkinson, Reading Egyptian Art (1992); Pinch (2002); Met Museum, Heilbrunn Timeline, "Egyptian Art: iconography"; British Museum, "Ancient Egypt: gods and goddesses".
