## The French formal garden (Le Nôtre and Versailles)
Kind: reference
About: andre-le-notre, versailles-gardens, french-formal-garden
The French formal garden reached its height under Louis XIV. Its great designer was André Le Nôtre (1613–1700), a gardener's son who became Louis XIV's royal gardener. At Vaux-le-Vicomte (1656–1661, for Nicolas Fouquet) and then at Versailles, where Louis XIV summoned him in 1662, he treated the garden as an extension of the building: a strong central axis ran out from the house, with cross-axes, long avenues, terraces and water laid out by geometry and seen best from above.

The parts have names. A parterre is a flat, level, patterned garden beside the house, meant to be viewed from the windows or a terrace. A parterre de broderie ("embroidery parterre") uses curling box hedges, like the scrolls of embroidery, on a background of coloured gravel, sand or crushed brick. Beyond the parterres came the bosquets (formal groves cut with paths), then the wider park. Water was treated the same way: still, straight-sided canals and basins that mirrored the sky. The Grand Canal at Versailles is cross-shaped, with arms of roughly 1.5 and 1.7 kilometres; sources date its digging from about 1667 or 1668 to the late 1670s.

The idea behind it was control. The garden showed that the king, like the gardener, could impose order on nature. His influence travelled in books and pupils: Dezallier d'Argenville's treatise on gardening (French edition 1709) was translated into English by the architect John James in 1712, and English designers adapted the French pattern, usually on a smaller scale.
Sources: Château de Versailles, "André Le Nôtre" (en.chateauversailles.fr/discover/history/great-characters/andre-notre), read; Wikipedia, "André Le Nôtre" (birth 12 March 1613, death 15 September 1700) and "Grand Canal of Versailles" (dates and lengths differ between sources, flagged); Metropolitan Museum of Art, catalogue entry for Dezallier d'Argenville, The Theory and Practice of Gardening (1712 English edition, translator John James); the definition of parterre de broderie is general knowledge.

## The Dutch formal garden and William III
Kind: reference
About: dutch-formal-garden, het-loo, hampton-court-gardens, william-iii
The Dutch style is not the same as the French. Where French gardens were vast, with long views over a whole landscape, Dutch gardens were small, walled or hedged, and full of detail. They were laid out in a rectangle, often with a straight canal along one side, and packed with clipped yews and box (topiary), pleached hedges, small compartments, summerhouses, statues, tulips and other bulbs, and orange trees in tubs. Water was normally a canal rather than a cascade, because the Netherlands is flat and waterlogged: a canal drained the garden as well as decorating it.

The best-known royal example is Het Loo, near Apeldoorn. The palace was built in 1684–1686 for William of Orange as Stadtholder, and the great garden was laid out from about 1686; sources differ over how much was due to Daniel Marot and how much to others such as Claude Desgots. After William and Mary were invited to take the English throne in 1688, they brought Dutch taste with them. Historic Royal Palaces credits them (1689–1702) with the Great Fountain Garden on the east front of Hampton Court, with Marot as designer, which had radiating walks of yew in the patte d'oie form (see below). It was laid out beside Charles II's earlier Long Water canal of 1661–1662.

Dutch fashion reached the gentry as well: Westbury Court in Gloucestershire (laid out by Maynard Colchester between 1696 and 1705, given to the National Trust in 1967) is a rare surviving small Dutch water garden, with a Long Canal about 137 metres long. Straight canals, clipped evergreens and ranks of potted citrus became fashionable in England in the 1690s and early 1700s, and remained so until the move to a more "natural" look in the 1730s.
Sources: Wikipedia, "Het Loo Palace" (dates, with designers disputed); Historic Royal Palaces, "The gardens at Hampton Court Palace" (hrp.org.uk, as summarised in search; not opened in full) and Parks & Gardens UK, Hampton Court record; Historic England list entry 1000786 (Westbury Court) and National Trust, History of Westbury Court Garden, as summarised in search; general characteristics of Dutch gardens are general knowledge.

## Wildernesses, rides and garden buildings as eye-catchers
Kind: reference
About: formal-woodland-garden
In the early 1700s, English owners with space and money made formal gardens out of woodland. A "wilderness" meant not wild country but a planted grove cut with geometric paths, often in the shape of a maze, star or set of diagonal walks, usually behind the flower garden. A patte d'oie ("goose foot") is a spread of three or more straight avenues radiating from one point, like the toes of a goose. It was borrowed from France and used to organise a wood, so that walkers could see along each ride to something at the end.

That something was an eye-catcher: a temple, pavilion, statue, obelisk, urn or gate placed so that a ride framed it. This was a main use of garden buildings. The building did two jobs: it drew the eye and the visitor along the path, and once reached it gave a seat, shade or shelter and a new view back. A fine example of the formal woodland garden with garden buildings is the early layout at Stowe in Buckinghamshire, where Charles Bridgeman worked from 1711 (National Trust).

Many such gardens were altered or swept away after about 1740 by the fashion for the landscape style, but the lines of rides and the buildings at their ends often survived, and today they are among the clues that an estate had a formal garden.
Sources: National Trust, "The gardeners that made Stowe" (nationaltrust.org.uk, Stowe), read; Historic Royal Palaces on Hampton Court's patte d'oie; Historic England, Tottenham House garden eye-catcher listing (c. 1720–1730, Lord Burlington) as an example of an eye-catcher; the definitions of wilderness and patte d'oie are general knowledge.

## The English landscape garden
Kind: reference
About: english-landscape-garden, charles-bridgeman, william-kent, stowe-gardens, ha-ha
From the 1720s a new idea developed in England: that a garden should look like an idealised natural landscape, in the manner of the paintings of Claude and Poussin. The poet Alexander Pope and the Earl of Shaftesbury's followers argued for "nature", and designers began to soften the straight lines.

Charles Bridgeman (about 1690–1738), royal gardener from 1728, was the bridge between the two styles. At Stowe, where he worked from 1711, he kept a formal outline but built what the National Trust calls England's first ha-ha, and he carried out the Serpentine in Hyde Park, made about 1730 for Queen Caroline (sources vary between 1730 and 1732), a lake with curving shores. A ha-ha is a ditch with a vertical retaining wall on the garden side and a sloping bank on the other, so that the view runs out into the park without a visible fence, but cattle and deer cannot cross. The idea came from France: Dezallier d'Argenville's book, in John James's 1712 English translation, calls it an "ah-ah", and the name is said to come from the surprised cry of someone coming upon it.

William Kent (about 1685–1748; sources give 1684 to 1686), a painter and architect from Bridlington who had trained in Italy and was a protégé of Lord Burlington, took the next step. He worked at Stowe from 1730, and from about 1737 he developed Rousham in Oxfordshire from Bridgeman's layout. In such gardens he arranged trees, water and classical temples as a series of "pictures" to be found on a circuit walk. Horace Walpole, in his essay On Modern Gardening, famously said that Kent "leaped the fence, and saw that all nature was a garden". Serpentine streams, clumps of trees and open grass followed. Lancelot "Capability" Brown (see his own section) later turned this into a whole-landscape style.
Sources: National Trust, "The gardeners that made Stowe" (Bridgeman from 1711, first ha-ha, Kent from 1730), read; Wikipedia, "The Serpentine" (1730, Queen Caroline, Bridgeman) and "Charles Bridgeman" (about 1690–1738; royal gardener 1728 per Gardenvisit.com); Wikipedia and Britannica, "William Kent" (birth year varies 1684–1686; died 12 April 1748); Rousham House and TCLF pages (Bridgeman layout completed about 1737, Kent's work over the following years); Metropolitan Museum of Art catalogue entry for Dezallier d'Argenville (ha-ha, "ah-ah"); Walpole quotation is the standard attribution, not re-checked against the text.

## Lancelot "Capability" Brown (1716–1783)
Kind: person
About: capability-brown
Lancelot Brown was baptised on 30 August 1716 at Kirkharle in Northumberland, and trained as a gardener there. In 1739 he went south, and from 1741 worked at Stowe in Buckinghamshire under William Kent. The National Trust says he was head gardener there from 1741 to 1751; Wikipedia dates his appointment as head gardener to 1742. He then worked on his own, and he designed or advised on more than 170 landscapes, among them Blenheim, Warwick Castle, Petworth, Croome and Chatsworth. In 1764 he became George III's Master Gardener at Hampton Court. He died in London on 6 February 1783.

The nickname is said to come from his telling clients that their land had "capabilities". His approach was to remove walls, parterres and avenues close to the house, and to bring smooth lawn right up to the windows. He then shaped the ground with gentle swells, and planted belts of trees round the edge and clumps of trees on the rises. He also dammed streams to make large lakes with winding edges which seemed to be natural rivers; a good deal of his effort went into the engineering of hidden dams, drains and sluices. The ha-ha kept livestock off the lawn. In 2016 the 300th anniversary of his birth was marked by the Capability Brown Festival, led by the Landscape Institute.

His critics, including William Chambers, thought his parks too bare.
Sources: Wikipedia, "Capability Brown" (baptism, 1739 journey south, 1742 head gardener, 1764 Hampton Court, over 170 gardens, nickname, death date); National Trust, "The gardeners that made Stowe" (head gardener 1741–1751); Landscape Institute, Capability Brown Festival "About the Festival" (competitions.landscapeinstitute.org/capability-brown/about-festival/). The Chambers criticism is general knowledge (Chambers's Dissertation on Oriental Gardening, 1772), not re-checked.

## Humphry Repton and the Picturesque
Kind: person
About: humphry-repton, picturesque
Humphry Repton (1752–1818) set up as a landscape gardener in 1788, after his business ventures failed, in effect as Brown's successor. The Gardens Trust credits him with some 400 landscapes. He was a good artist, and he is famous for his "Red Books": hand-written reports bound in red morocco, with watercolours whose flaps lifted to show "before" and "after" views of an estate. Clients could see exactly what they were buying. He published Sketches and Hints on Landscape Gardening in 1795.

Repton stood between Brown's smooth parkland and the wilder Picturesque taste. Writers such as Uvedale Price and Richard Payne Knight argued that Brown's lawns were bland and that landscape should be rugged, varied and irregular, like a painting by Salvator Rosa. Repton took a middle way. He kept Brown's lakes and clumps, but he put back a terrace or flower garden beside the house, so that the house had a setting, and he suggested cottages, rustic bridges and winding walks. His reintroduction of terraces and formal flower beds near the house helped to prepare the way for the revived formal gardens of the nineteenth century.
Sources: The Gardens Trust, "Humphry Repton: his life and work" (thegardenstrust.org/?p=5464) and Repton exhibition boards (c. 400 landscapes; Red Books not all red at first), as summarised in search; Metropolitan Museum of Art, Sketches and Hints on Landscape Gardening (Brandsbury Red Book the first of at least 300). The Price and Knight debate is general knowledge (Britannica).

## The Victorian return to formal gardens
Kind: reference
About: victorian-formal-garden, nesfield, charles-barry, bedding-schemes
By the 1830s many owners felt that Brown's bare lawns left a country house floating without a setting. Gardeners and writers (J. C. Loudon, and later Reginald Blomfield, whose 1892 book The Formal Garden in England argued for architecture in the garden) pressed for terraces and parterres to come back near the house, with the park beyond.

Two men led this. Sir Charles Barry (1795–1860), architect of the Houses of Parliament, laid out Italianate terraced gardens in the 1840s at Trentham (Staffordshire) and at Shrubland Park (Suffolk, finished 1854), with terraces, balustrades, statues, urns and fountains. William Andrews Nesfield (1793–1881; his baptism is given as 1794 in the Oxford DNB), a former soldier and watercolourist, became a leading garden designer. He revived the parterre de broderie, with scrolls of box against coloured gravels, at Castle Howard (1850s) and Witley Court (whose great fountain he called his "monster work"), and he laid out the setting of the Palm House at Kew. Italianate gardens had terraces, balustrades, fountains and urns. French revival gardens used scrolling box.

New plants made the colour. Greenhouse-raised tender plants like pelargoniums ("geraniums"), calceolarias, verbenas and lobelias could be bedded out in hundreds by early summer, and glass was now cheap. This gave "bedding schemes" and later "carpet bedding". Cheaper glass and better heating helped. The style was criticised later, by William Robinson and Gertrude Jekyll, who preferred looser planting.
Sources: Wikipedia, "William Andrews Nesfield", Parks & Gardens UK, "William Andrews Nesfield", Country Life and Garden Museum pages (Castle Howard, Witley Court, Kew), as summarised in search; Historic England list entry 1000155 (Shrubland Hall) and Gardenvisit.com biography of Sir Charles Barry (Trentham, Shrubland, finished 1854). Bedding plants, Loudon and Blomfield (1892) are general knowledge, not re-checked.

## Orangeries: how citrus was grown and heated
Kind: reference
About: orangeries-general, orangeries-and-heating, glass-tax-repeal
Oranges and lemons do not survive English winters, but they were wanted as status symbols from the 1600s. The solution was to grow them in tubs and carry them outdoors for the summer, then shelter them in a building from about October to May. That building was the orangery: a tall, south-facing room with big windows on one side and a solid roof and walls on the others. Citrus need frost-free conditions rather than heat, so early orangeries had little heating: charcoal or coke stoves or braziers, or small furnaces with flues (brick or tile channels) running through the walls or under the floor. A heated floor on the hypocaust principle (hot gases under a raised floor) borrowed an idea from Roman buildings. Shutters covered the glass on freezing nights.

A greenhouse or "stove" was different: a hot, glass building for tropical plants such as pineapples. Early ones used tan-bark pits (the rotting bark gave off heat). Heating by hot water was invented in Paris about 1777, shown in Britain by Count Chabannes (a hothouse at Sundridge Park, Kent, in 1816), and developed by Anthony Bacon and Atkinson, who began experiments in 1822; Angier March Perkins's high-pressure system followed in 1831. Pipes were more even and safer than flues. Cost of glass was another limit. A duty on glass by weight, in place from 1746, made big panes expensive; it was repealed in 1845. After that glasshouses became larger. Joseph Paxton's Great Conservatory at Chatsworth (1836–1840) and his Crystal Palace (1851) were the best-known results.
Sources: Gardenvisit.com, "History of hot water greenhouse heating" (gardenvisit.com), read; Grace's Guide, "Angier March Perkins" (1831); Grimsdyke, "A Brief History of the Victorian Glasshouse" and ceramics.org, Crystal Palace history, as summarised in search (glass duty 1746, ended 1845; window tax ended 1851). The citrus-in-tubs practice, flues, tan pits and the hypocaust principle are general knowledge; no orangery-specific page was opened, and the exact season dates are approximate.

## Joseph Paxton, Chatsworth and the Crystal Palace
Kind: person
About: joseph-paxton, glass-tax-repeal, chatsworth-great-conservatory, crystal-palace
Joseph Paxton (1803–1865; Britannica gives 1801) was a farmer's son from Bedfordshire who became head gardener to the 6th Duke of Devonshire at Chatsworth in 1826. He built the Great Conservatory there in 1836–1840, then the largest glasshouse in the world, using large sheets of glass from Chance Brothers. In 1849 he was the first in Britain to flower the giant Amazon water lily (Victoria amazonica), in a special house at Chatsworth. He is said to have taken inspiration from the leaf's ribbed underside.

When Prince Albert's Great Exhibition of 1851 needed a building in Hyde Park within months, the official designs failed. Paxton submitted his own design, and the Crystal Palace was built of cast-iron columns, wooden sashes and hundreds of thousands of standard-sized glass panes, in a few months. It was a greenhouse on a vast scale. It was later moved to Sydenham in south London, where it stood until it burned down in 1936. Paxton was knighted in 1851. The repeal of the glass duty in 1845 had made his kind of building affordable.
Sources: Britannica, "Joseph Paxton" (britannica.com/print/article/447455) and Parks & Gardens UK, "Joseph Paxton", as summarised in search (birth year differs: 1803 or 1801); Coventry Society page (knighted after the Exhibition); reviews of Colquhoun's Paxton biography (1849 lily). Dimensions of the Great Conservatory differ between sources and are omitted; the Crystal Palace's move to Sydenham and its burning in 1936 are general knowledge, not re-checked.

## Garden follies and buildings: temples, bath houses, hermitages and ruins
Kind: reference
About: garden-buildings, cold-bath, hermitage
Historic England describes garden buildings as structures that give pleasure, shelter or a focus in a designed landscape. Many are called follies, though most had a use.

A temple or summerhouse, often in Greek or Roman style, was a place to sit and admire a view, and a banqueting house was a small room, often away from the house, for the sweet course and wine. The Gothic taste (see Batty Langley) produced Gothic towers and arches. A bath house or cold plunge bath was a small building with a stone-lined pool, filled with spring water. Cold bathing became fashionable for health after Sir John Floyer's book on bathing (first published in 1697 as An Enquiry into the Right Use and Abuses of the Hot, Cold and Temperate Baths, and reissued in 1702 as The Ancient Psychrolousia Revived). Cold plunges were thought invigorating and good for rheumatism. Owners built them near a spring, usually unheated and with a small room to undress.

A hermitage was a rustic hut or cell, sometimes with a paid hermit, to promote reflection. Charles Hamilton of Painshill (Surrey) is said to have advertised for one, offering a large sum if he stayed seven years without cutting his hair or speaking to the servants; the story says the first hermit lasted about three weeks and was dismissed after being found in a pub. The tale is well known but may be partly apocryphal. A sham ruin was a fake medieval castle, abbey or Roman arch, built to give age and romance to a view, a trend that peaked in the mid 1700s. Grottoes, shell houses and rustic root-houses are others. Such buildings were meant to surprise, with a route planned to reach each at the best moment.
Sources: Leeds University Library Special Collections catalogue records for Floyer (1697 and 1702 titles); Wikipedia, "Garden hermit", Smithsonian Magazine, "Ornamental hermits" and Hermitary.com (Painshill advertisement; amount and dates vary between accounts); Historic England list entries (Tottenham House garden folly; Bromley Palace Park folly) and county HER thesaurus definition of "folly". ## Chinoiserie in English gardens
Kind: reference
About: chinoiserie, william-chambers, kew-pagoda, chinese-designs-book, willow-pattern
Chinoiserie means European design inspired by (and often misunderstanding) China. It was fashionable across Europe in the mid 1700s, as trade brought in porcelain, lacquer and wallpaper. In English gardens it meant brightly painted pavilions, lattice bridges and pagodas with bells and dragons at the roof corners, usually set among water.

William Chambers (1723–1796) had visited Canton as a young man working for the Swedish East India Company, and published Designs of Chinese Buildings in 1757. He designed buildings at Kew for Augusta, Princess of Wales, including the ten-storey Pagoda of 1761–1762, about 163 feet (50 metres) high, whose eighty wooden dragons were gilded and later lost. Chambers later claimed in his Dissertation on Oriental Gardening (1772) that Chinese gardens were a guide to irregular design, though he admitted he had to be a little fanciful. Thomas Chippendale also popularised the taste, with "Chinese Chippendale" furniture in the 1750s and 1760s. Amateur pattern books, such as those by Edwards and Darly, spread the fashion.

The "Willow Pattern" is a later piece of English chinoiserie. Blue-and-white china printed with a bridge, pagoda, willow and two birds was developed by English potters. Early versions came from Thomas Turner's Caughley factory in Shropshire from about 1780, and the standard pattern is usually placed at Spode about 1790; sources disagree over who exactly devised it and when. The story of the lovers was added afterwards. It was not a Chinese pattern, though it drew on Chinese blue-and-white porcelain.
Sources: History Today, "The Great Pagoda at Kew" and the Historic England listing for the Pagoda (ten storeys, 163 feet), as summarised in search; Wikipedia, "Willow pattern" and "Thomas Turner (potter)" (dates uncertain, flagged); University of Liverpool, "Story of the Willow Pattern". Chambers's Canton voyages, the 1757 and 1772 books and Chinese Chippendale are well-established background (Britannica/ODNB) not re-checked here.

## Lead garden statuary
Kind: reference
About: lead-garden-statuary, john-nost, john-nost-ii, andrew-carpenter, john-cheere
Before the 1680s, garden statues in England were mostly marble or stone. Lead was cheaper and easier to work than carved stone or cast bronze, and one mould could produce many copies, so statuary became affordable beyond the greatest houses. A sculptor made a model, a mould was taken, and the figure was cast in a hard lead alloy.

The Flemish sculptor Artus Quellin was probably the first to work in lead in Britain; in 1685 he supplied four Stuart monarchs for Glamis Castle. His assistant Jan (John) Van Nost, from Mechelen, married Quellin's widow and took over the business as John Nost. From the 1690s he specialised in lead garden figures, and he died in 1710. His successor, John Nost II (often called Van Nost the younger), ran the business until he died in April 1729. Andrew Carpenter (Andries Carpentière), a Frenchman who had worked for Nost, set up on his own by 1703 near Hyde Park Corner, announced in January 1736 that he would stop casting lead figures, and died in 1737; his stock and moulds were sold in 1744.

John Cheere (1709–1787), who trained under Nost, and his brother Henry took the lease of Nost's former yard near Hyde Park Corner in 1739. John ran it until his death and became probably the most prolific lead sculptor of Georgian Britain. He later used Carpenter's moulds as well as his own subjects.

Lead figures were painted. The Glamis figures were painted to look like brass, Nost's figures at Drayton like white stone, and Cheere's to resemble bronze, marble or lifelike colours. The weathered grey of surviving figures is not how they were meant to look.
Sources: The Garden History blog (thegardenhistory.blog), "The Golden Age of Lead" (2024/05/04) and "John Cheere: The Man at Hyde Park Corner" (2024/05/11), read; Wikipedia, "John Nost" (which gives 1729 for the elder Nost, in conflict with the blog's 1710 for the elder and 1729 for Nost II; the pack follows the blog and flags the conflict). The Oxford DNB was not opened to settle it.

## Trompe-l'œil and painted rooms
Kind: reference
About: trompe-l-oeil
Trompe-l'œil is French for "deceive the eye": a painting so realistic that it looks like a real object, or a flat surface made to look three-dimensional. The idea is ancient. Roman villas at Pompeii had painted walls with fake windows, columns and garden views. It returned in Renaissance Italy, and in the 1600s and 1700s was used in English houses: a painted niche with a statue, a door that does not open, a ceiling that seems to open on to the sky, or a landscape painted over a wall, used to suggest more space or grandeur than was there.

Foreign painters such as Antonio Verrio and Louis Laguerre, who came to England in 1683, did big painted staircases and ceilings. Laguerre supplied such decoration for houses and palaces between 1683–1684 and 1721. Grisaille, painting in shades of grey to look like stone carving, was a cheaper way to give a room carved ornament. Painted wooden "dummy boards" (cut-out figures of people or animals) were also set up in rooms and gardens from the 1600s.

Painters used shadows, careful perspective and a fixed viewing point; trompe-l'œil usually works best from one spot, and falls apart once you move.
Sources: Victoria and Albert Museum collections, Laguerre trompe-l'oeil staircase fragment (collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O77709) and Pandora sketch for Petworth (O133909); Chatsworth, "The Great Stairs murals" (Verrio and grisaille); Wikipedia, "Louis Laguerre". The meaning of trompe-l'œil and its ancient Roman use at Pompeii are general knowledge; dummy boards are general knowledge, not checked.
