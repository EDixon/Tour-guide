## The House of Habsburg: who they were
Kind: reference
About: habsburg-rise-1273, habsburg-castle, rudolf-i, habsburg-end-1918
The Habsburgs were one of the most important ruling families in European history. They took their name from Habsburg Castle (the "Hawk's Castle", Habichtsburg) in what is now the Swiss canton of Aargau, traditionally dated to about 1020, and built by Count Radbot and Bishop Werner of Strasbourg, ancestors of the family. They began as minor counts in the area that is now Switzerland, Alsace and south-west Germany.
Their rise began in 1273, when the German princes elected Count Rudolf of Habsburg as King of the Romans, partly because he seemed too weak to be dangerous. In 1278 he defeated King Ottokar II of Bohemia at the Battle of the Marchfeld, and in 1282 he gave the duchies of Austria and Styria to his sons. Austria then stayed in the family for more than six hundred years. From 1438 almost every Holy Roman Emperor was a Habsburg.
The family grew mainly by marriage rather than conquest. A Latin saying, "Bella gerant alii, tu felix Austria nube" ("Let others wage war; you, happy Austria, marry"), sums this up, but nobody knows who first said it. It has been credited to King Matthias Corvinus of Hungary in the late fifteenth century, and one Austrian reference dates the idea to Duke Rudolf IV's seals in 1364, so treat any single author with caution. It also ignores the luck and the wars that helped the family. Maximilian I's marriage to Mary of Burgundy in 1477 brought the Netherlands and Burgundy; his son Philip married Joanna of Castile and Aragon in 1496, and their son Charles V inherited Spain and its American empire. Marriages with Hungarian and Bohemian heirs added those crowns in 1526.
In 1556 the family split into a Spanish and an Austrian branch. The Spanish line died out in 1700. The Austrian line continued through the female line: Maria Theresa's marriage to Francis Stephen of Lorraine in 1736 created the house of Habsburg-Lorraine. The Holy Roman Empire ended in 1806, the Austrian Empire became Austria-Hungary in 1867, and the monarchy ended in November 1918 after the First World War. Habsburgs were famous for the "Habsburg jaw", a prominent lower jaw seen in many portraits.

## Charles V and the split of the Habsburg lands
Kind: person
About: charles-v-emperor, habsburg-split-1556
Charles V (born in Ghent on 24 February 1500, died 21 September 1558) inherited more than any Habsburg before or after him. From his Burgundian grandparents (Mary of Burgundy and Maximilian I) he received the Netherlands; from his mother Joanna's parents, Ferdinand and Isabella, he received Spain, Naples, Sicily, Sardinia and the new lands in the Americas. He became King of Spain in 1516 and, after an election costing huge bribes funded by the Fugger bankers, Holy Roman Emperor in 1519. Spanish writers said the sun never set on his empire.
His reign was one long crisis. He faced Martin Luther and the Reformation (the Diet of Worms, 1521), wars with Francis I of France, Ottoman armies at the gates of Vienna (1529), and the sack of Rome by his unpaid troops in 1527. He could not be everywhere, so he governed through his relatives. His younger brother Ferdinand ruled the Austrian lands, and was made King of Bohemia and Hungary in 1526.
Worn out, Charles gave up the Netherlands to his son Philip in 1555 and Spain in 1556, then retired to a monastery at Yuste in Spain, where he died. His brother Ferdinand I took over the imperial title (formally elected in 1558). From then on there were two Habsburg branches: the Spanish Habsburgs (Philip II and his successors), who held Spain, Portugal for a time, much of Italy, the Netherlands and the Americas; and the Austrian Habsburgs, who held the Austrian lands, Bohemia and Hungary and usually the imperial crown. The branches intermarried constantly and generally cooperated, but they were separate states.

## Charles II of Spain and the end of the Spanish Habsburgs
Kind: person
About: charles-ii-of-spain, charles-ii-death
Charles II of Spain (born 6 November 1661, died 1 November 1700) was the last Spanish Habsburg. He became king aged three and was sickly all his life. He was slow to walk and talk, had a very prominent jaw and was unable to chew properly, and his court called him "El Hechizado" ("the Bewitched"), as people believed his condition must be caused by witchcraft. His two marriages (to Marie Louise of Orléans, then Maria Anna of Neuburg) produced no children.
Historians discuss the cause with care. The Habsburgs had married their cousins, nieces and uncles for generations. A 2009 genetic study of the family tree (Alvarez, Ceballos and Quinteiro, in PLoS ONE) calculated Charles's inbreeding coefficient as 0.254, about the same as a child of two siblings (it was 0.025 for the dynasty's founder, Philip I). The authors speculated that two recessive conditions could explain much of his illness. This is a pedigree-based estimate and a speculative diagnosis, not a proven one.
The real importance of his weakness was political. For decades everyone knew he would die childless, and Europe's powers argued over who would inherit his vast empire. Louis XIV of France and Emperor Leopold I, both married to Charles's relatives, each had a claim. The powers drew up partition treaties in 1698 and 1700, but Spain wanted its empire kept whole. Shortly before he died Charles left everything to the French candidate, Philip of Anjou, Louis XIV's grandson. He died on 1 November 1700 and the Spanish Habsburg line ended with him.
Sources: Alvarez, Ceballos and Quinteiro, "The Role of Inbreeding in the Extinction of a European Royal Dynasty", PLoS ONE 4(4): e5174 (2009), doi 10.1371/journal.pone.0005174 (figures confirmed from search summaries of the paper; full text not opened); general knowledge for Charles's dates, nickname, marriages and the 1698 and 1700 partition treaties (not individually checked online).

## The War of the Spanish Succession (1701–1714)
Kind: reference
About: war-spanish-succession, spanish-succession, battle-of-blenheim, treaty-of-utrecht, treaty-of-rastatt, philip-v-of-spain, duke-of-marlborough, prince-eugene
Why there was a war over Spain. When Charles II died in 1700, whoever held Spain would also hold its empire in Italy, the Netherlands and the Americas. Louis XIV's grandson Philip of Anjou took the throne as Philip V, in line with the will. The Habsburgs claimed it for Archduke Charles, younger son of Emperor Leopold I. England, the Dutch Republic and others feared France and Spain united under one family, and also feared for their trade. In September 1701 they renewed the Grand Alliance with the Emperor, and Louis's recognition of the exiled James Edward Stuart as "James III" angered England further. War was declared in May 1702.
The war was fought in the Low Countries, Germany, Italy and Spain. The Duke of Marlborough, with Prince Eugene of Savoy, won famous victories: Blenheim (13 August 1704, saving Vienna from a French and Bavarian attack), Ramillies (1706), Oudenaarde (1708) and the very bloody Malplaquet (11 September 1709). English forces took Gibraltar in 1704 and Minorca in 1708. In 1711 Archduke Charles became Holy Roman Emperor when his brother Joseph I died, and Britain feared a revived empire of Charles V; the new Tory government sought peace.
The Treaty of Utrecht (1713) let Philip V keep Spain and its American empire, on condition that the crowns of France and Spain never be united. Britain gained Gibraltar and Minorca, Newfoundland and Nova Scotia (Acadia), and the slave-trading contract (asiento). The Emperor, still unsatisfied, fought on until the Treaties of Rastatt (March 1714) and Baden (September 1714) gave Austria the Spanish Netherlands, Milan, Naples and Sardinia. Barcelona, which had backed Charles, fell to Philip's army on 11 September 1714. Britain kept Gibraltar, and still does.

## Charles VI and the Pragmatic Sanction
Kind: person
About: emperor-charles-vi, pragmatic-sanction, war-austrian-succession
Charles VI (born 1 October 1685, died 20 October 1740) was the Archduke Charles who the Allies backed as King of Spain ("Charles III" to them). He was proclaimed king in Vienna on 19 September 1703, reached Spain in 1704 and found his support mainly in Catalonia and Aragon, but in 1711 his elder brother Joseph I died without sons and Charles went home to be elected Holy Roman Emperor. He was forced to accept the Peace of Rastatt (1714), which gave Spain to Philip V, but kept the idea of being Spain's rightful king for the rest of his life.
His great worry was the succession. His only son died as a baby in 1716, leaving three daughters. Under a family agreement of 1703 between Charles and his brother Joseph I (the Mutual Pact of Succession), daughters could inherit if the male line died out, but the daughters of the elder brother Joseph came first, ahead of Charles's own. In 1713 Charles issued the Pragmatic Sanction, a family law that said the Habsburg lands were indivisible and could pass to a daughter if there was no son. In practice this meant his eldest daughter, Maria Theresa.
Charles spent much of his reign persuading others to accept it: the Estates of the realms concerned had to accept it formally (Hungary's Diet passed its own version in 1723), Britain and the Dutch Republic agreed in the Treaty of Vienna of 16 March 1731 in return for the closure of his Ostend Company, and France accepted it in the Treaty of Vienna that ended the War of the Polish Succession (definitive in 1738), in return for the duchy of Lorraine. The price of this diplomacy was high. When he died in 1740, Frederick II of Prussia invaded the rich province of Silesia in December, and Bavaria, France and others challenged the succession in the War of the Austrian Succession (1740–1748).

## Maria Theresa (1717–1780)
Kind: person
About: maria-theresa, pragmatic-sanction, war-austrian-succession, seven-years-war
Maria Theresa (born 13 May 1717, died 29 November 1780) was the only woman to rule the Habsburg lands in her own right. She was never trained for government. When her father Charles VI died in 1740 she was 23, pregnant, with an empty treasury and a small army, and Frederick II of Prussia promptly seized Silesia. She went to Pressburg (Bratislava) for her coronation as Queen of Hungary in 1741 and, according to a famous story, appealed to the Hungarian nobles holding her infant son; they are said to have cried "We will die for our king!" Historians doubt the details, but Hungary did provide troops. She held on to nearly everything except Silesia.
She married Francis Stephen of Lorraine in 1736, an unusually happy love match, and had sixteen children. Her husband was elected emperor in 1745 (Francis I), but she ruled the lands herself and is often called "Empress" for convenience. After his death in 1765 her son Joseph II became co-ruler.
She reorganised the monarchy: regular taxes and a stronger army, and she introduced a state schooling system (compulsory in principle from 1774). In 1767 smallpox killed her daughter-in-law Maria Josepha of Bavaria (28 May) and her daughter Archduchess Maria Josepha (15 October), and Maria Theresa herself fell ill but survived. In 1768 she had her family inoculated by the Dutch physician Jan Ingenhousz. In the Seven Years' War (1756–1763) she overturned old alliances, allying with France, her long-time enemy, in the "Diplomatic Revolution" of 1756. The alliance was sealed by the marriage of her youngest daughter, Marie Antoinette, to the future Louis XVI.
Her daughters were married into royal houses all over Europe: Maria Carolina to Naples, Maria Amalia to Parma, Marie Antoinette to France. She lived mostly at Schönbrunn in Vienna, which she transformed into the splendid summer palace seen today.

## Marie Antoinette and the Petit Trianon
Kind: reference
About: marie-antoinette, maria-theresa, versailles-petit-trianon, petit-trianon-versailles-gift, queens-hamlet, petit-trianon-built
Marie Antoinette (born in Vienna on 2 November 1755, executed in Paris on 16 October 1793) was the fifteenth child of Maria Theresa and Francis I. She was married at 14, in 1770, to the future Louis XVI, to seal the Austro-French alliance of 1756 between two houses that had been rivals for two centuries. The marriage was not popular in France, where she was nicknamed "l'Autrichienne" (a pun on "Austrian" and "bitch"). Her fame for extravagance is partly deserved and partly invented; "Let them eat cake" is not recorded in her lifetime.
The Petit Trianon is a small neoclassical château in the grounds of Versailles, designed by Ange-Jacques Gabriel for Louis XV and built between 1762 and 1768 (one French source starts it in 1761). After Louis XVI became king in 1774 he gave it to his queen, who made it her private retreat. She had the gardens replanted in the new English style, a small theatre built where she acted, and, between about 1783 and 1786 (some sources say 1785), the Queen's Hamlet (Hameau de la Reine) by Richard Mique, with the painter Hubert Robert influencing the design. It was a lakeside group of rustic cottages (sources count between ten and twelve), with a working farm, where the queen and her friends played at country life. It was part of a fashion for make-believe farms.
The Revolution began in 1789. The royal family was brought to Paris in October 1789, Louis XVI was guillotined in January 1793, and Marie Antoinette was tried and executed in October. Her sister Maria Carolina of Naples turned bitterly against revolutionary France, which links to Louis-Philippe's queen.

## Maria Carolina of Naples and Maria Amalia, Queen of the French
Kind: person
About: maria-carolina, maria-amalia, louis-philippe, marie-antoinette, maria-amalia-marriage
How were Marie Antoinette and Louis-Philippe's queen related? Marie Antoinette was Queen Maria Amalia's aunt. Their common ancestor was the Empress Maria Theresa, who was Marie Antoinette's mother and Maria Amalia's grandmother.
Maria Carolina (born 13 August 1752, died 8 September 1814) was Maria Theresa's thirteenth child and Marie Antoinette's older sister. She married Ferdinand IV of Naples (Ferdinand III of Sicily) by proxy on 7 April 1768. She was far the stronger character, and effectively ruled the kingdom of Naples and Sicily, with her favourite Sir John Acton, who helped run the kingdom from 1778. She was a fierce enemy of the French Revolution after her sister's execution in 1793. She allied Naples with Britain and Austria against France, and was driven out of Naples in 1799 and again in 1806. She bore eighteen children, of whom seven reached adulthood.
Her daughter Maria Amalia (born 26 April 1782, died 24 March 1866) married Louis-Philippe, then Duke of Orléans and an exile with no kingdom, at Palermo on 25 November 1809. He was the son of Philippe Égalité, who had voted for the death of Louis XVI. After Napoleon fell, the couple returned to France. In 1830 the July Revolution put Louis-Philippe on the throne as "King of the French" and she became queen. Devout and hardworking, she was said to be a model wife and mother. They had ten children, and she survived most of her family.
The revolution of February 1848 forced Louis-Philippe to abdicate. They escaped to England, landing at Newhaven on 3 March 1848 under assumed names, and Queen Victoria lent them Claremont House in Surrey. Louis-Philippe died there on 26 August 1850 and Maria Amalia on 24 March 1866.

## Franz Joseph and the end of the Habsburg monarchy
Kind: person
About: franz-joseph, habsburg-end-1918, charles-i-of-austria, sarajevo-1914, ausgleich-1867, hre-dissolved-1806
Francis II, the last Holy Roman Emperor, declared himself Emperor of Austria in 1804 and gave up the old imperial crown in 1806 under pressure from Napoleon, ending the thousand-year-old Holy Roman Empire. The Habsburgs then ruled the Austrian Empire.
Franz Joseph (born 18 August 1830, died 21 November 1916) became emperor on 2 December 1848, aged 18, in the revolutions of that year. He reigned for nearly 68 years. He lost territory in Italy (1859, 1866) and the war with Prussia in 1866, which ended Austrian leadership of Germany. In 1867 the Compromise (Ausgleich) created Austria-Hungary, a double monarchy in which he was Emperor of Austria and King of Hungary. In 1854 he married Elisabeth of Bavaria ("Sisi"), who was assassinated in 1898. His life was marked by personal tragedy: his son Rudolf died in 1889 at Mayerling and his brother Maximilian was shot in Mexico in 1867.
In 1914 his nephew and heir, Archduke Franz Ferdinand, was assassinated at Sarajevo on 28 June. Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia a month later, starting the First World War. Franz Joseph died in 1916 and was followed by his great-nephew Charles I, who tried to negotiate peace in vain. With military defeat and national movements tearing the empire apart, Charles gave up the right to take part in government on 11 November 1918. Austria became a republic and Hungary followed. Charles died in exile on Madeira in 1922. In Austria the family was banished unless it renounced its claims; the eldest son, Otto von Habsburg, later did so and died in 2011.
