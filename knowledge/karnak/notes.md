## The Quay and Tribune (the temple's old harbour)
Kind: site
About: quay, sety-ii-obelisk, nile-level-texts, taharqa-quay-ramp, hakoris-bark-chapel
The quay is the stone landing stage at the western end of the temple, with a raised platform, the tribune, at its centre. On the on-site board it is feature 1, the Port of the Temple. Today it overlooks dry land, but for well over a thousand years the Nile flowed right up to it. Boats carrying the statue of Amun tied up here, and a road lined with ram-headed sphinxes led from the quay to the temple gate.

What you can see. On the tribune stands a small quartzite obelisk carved for Sety II, who reigned about 1200 to 1194 BC. Catalogue entries give 3.3 and 3.57 metres, so say "about 3.5 metres". Sety II's name is carved on all four sides, and it was probably one of a pair. The tribune was once dated to the Nineteenth Dynasty partly because of these obelisks, but its date is uncertain. A stele of Sety II is also recorded here.

When was the quay built? Elaine Sullivan, in the UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology, writes that a new quay and a huge revetment (a retaining wall faced with stone) began about the time of Shoshenq I, around 945 BC. The stone wall shows that the Nile then flowed right up to its edge, in front of Shoshenq's new entrance. Excavators led by Mansour Boraik found the wall about 50 metres south of the present quay, preserved to 3.5 metres high. Inscriptions suggest it was built in the Twenty-second Dynasty, with building and repairs continuing into the fourth century BC. Michel Ghilardi, a geoarchaeologist, also dates the quay to the early 900s BC, but calls this a hypothesis. A Wikipedia article says the terrace was built under Ramesses II; it is a pointer only and disagrees with the excavators.

The flood records. Kings from the Twenty-second Dynasty onwards had the height of the annual Nile flood cut into the quay, dated by regnal year. Georges Legrain copied the texts in 1896, and Jürgen von Beckerath and Gerardus Broekman later studied them. Many are worn or lost.

Later changes. In the Twenty-fifth Dynasty the Kushite king Taharqa built a paved stone ramp south of the quay, running down into the river. Texts inside it show it was used for water rituals, including the New Year festival. Ghilardi describes it as built over the quay to ease the arrival of the royal barque. Under Hakoris (reigned 393 to 380 BC), a small boat chapel stood outside the west gate, on the site of the later First Pylon. Its extra wide west door let the god's boat be lifted from the river and carried straight in, still parallel to the bank. The Romans repaved the quay and the road, reusing older blocks.

The river. A narrow channel linking the quay to the main Nile was still in use in late Roman times and was given up in the Byzantine and Islamic periods (Ghilardi 2015). See "The Nile at Karnak" for how the river moved.
Sources: Sullivan, UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology, "Karnak: Development of the Temple of Amun-Ra" (2010), pp. 14 to 22; Ghilardi, "The Nile Valley in Upper Egypt during the Dynastic period (ancient Karnak area)" (2015); Wikidata entry and Wikimedia Commons categories for the obelisk and stele of Seti II (pointers only; the Wikidata entry cites the Karnak database SITH, which could not be opened); Legrain 1896, von Beckerath 1966 and Broekman 2002 as listed on Wikipedia, "Nile Level Texts" (pointer only); on-site board (source 9).

## The Nile at Karnak (how the river moved)
Kind: reference
About: quay, first-pylon
Karnak was not always beside the river, and it was not always on dry land that stayed dry. Sediment cores show the temple began on a sandy river terrace that stood as an island between Nile channels. The island was about 10 hectares, and the terrace is about 72 metres above sea level. Pennington and colleagues (Antiquity, 2025) took 61 cores, up to 11.65 metres deep, and date the first permanent occupation to the Old Kingdom, after about 2520 BC, give or take 420 years.

In the early Middle Kingdom, about 2000 BC, a western channel 90 to 150 metres wide ran so close that its eastern bank lay near the present Third Pylon and within the courtyards of the Seventh to Tenth Pylons. A major eastern branch, 220 to 500 metres wide in the Third Intermediate Period, ran to the east. Minor channels crossed the terrace, one near the First Pylon. The western channel silted up in stages, its south first, and the main Nile migrated west to where it runs today. Each strip of new land let the temple grow west: the Seventh to Tenth Pylons and the First to Third Pylons stand on ground the river gave up. Charloux and colleagues (Antiquity, 2021), excavating in the Ptah temple, link the temple's formation about 2200 to 2000 BC to the retreating riverbank. Pennington and colleagues cite a shift away from river cutting at Luxor about 4,000 years ago.

Ghilardi drilled a 25-metre borehole in front of the First Pylon. About 18 metres of it is Nile sediment with pottery, brick fragments and wood, from about 1450 BC (a wood fragment gave 1494 to 1402 BC) to about AD 350. Nile deposits west of the temples are about 20 metres thick, against under 6 metres to the east.

Texts and pictures agree. A painting in the Theban tomb of Neferhotep (TT 49) shows a T-shaped basin joined to the Nile by a canal on the temple's west side, so the river was in the Eighteenth Dynasty close enough to dig a harbour. By the Twenty-second Dynasty the river was at the quay, and it stayed in reach until Roman times.

Disagreements. François Larché has suggested that the Nile ran east of the temples in the early second millennium BC; Ghilardi calls this an assumption to check. The main published reconstruction used here is Pennington and colleagues (2025). Eliot's own geoarchaeology plan, with its AS cores and P profiles, could not be identified, so none of its labels are used; the AS core names in Pennington and colleagues (for example AS012 and AS040) show the same project's naming, but that is not proof of a match.
Sources: Pennington and others, "Conceptual origins and geomorphic evolution of the temple of Amun-Ra at Karnak (Luxor, Egypt)", Antiquity 99(408), 2025, 1535 to 1549 (figures 1 to 4); Charloux and others, "Shifting Nile and the origins and development of ancient Karnak", Antiquity 95(382), 2021, 919 to 939; Ghilardi (2015), chapter 12 (figures 3 and 4); Sullivan, UEE (2010), pp. 14 to 15.

## Avenue of ram-headed sphinxes
Kind: site
About: rams-avenue
Forty ram-headed sphinxes line the approach to the First Pylon, and fifty-two more stand inside the great court beyond. Each is a lion's body with the head of a ram, the sacred animal of Amun. They are sandstone, about 3.6 to 3.8 metres long and 1.1 to 1.2 metres wide, on pedestals 1.35 by 1.70 metres. A few keep traces of original paint. Between the front paws of each stands a small figure of Ramesses II as Osiris, with his titles on it. The ram shelters the king; this is the usual reading of the pose, not a statement from a Karnak text.

Whose are they? The traditional answer is Ramesses II (reigned 1279 to 1213 BC), whose name is on the statuettes. Agnès Cabrol argues that the sphinxes were carved under Thutmose IV and Amenhotep III on style, stood at Luxor Temple, were usurped by Ramesses II when he altered that temple, and reached Karnak only in the Twenty-first Dynasty, when Pinedjem I added his own inscriptions to the bases. Sullivan reports both versions. Digital Karnak follows the second.

How the avenue changed. Pinedjem may have placed a line of 100 or more along a road of about 200 metres from the old temple gate, the Second Pylon, to the quay. About 945 BC Shoshenq I built the great court, which cut the line nearly in half, so some sphinxes were moved to line its north and south walls (a later move, perhaps when Taharqa's kiosk was added). Nectanebo I built the First Pylon across the road. In Roman times the sphinxes outside were regrouped as seen today, and a gap was left between the seventh and eighth for access to buildings south of the quay.

Do not confuse this avenue with two others. Tutankhamun's ram sphinxes on the road to the Mut precinct were recarved from earlier figures, probably of Akhenaten and Nefertiti. The Luxor avenue is separate: see "The Avenue of Sphinxes to Luxor".

Look for the tiny king under a ram's chin; the closest pair show it best.
Sources: Digital Karnak, "Western Processional Way" (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/?p=4300; cites Cabrol 2001, Lauffray 1971, Murnane 1979) and "Other Processional Ways" (?p=4531); "Shoshenq I Court" (?p=4245); Sullivan, UEE (2010), pp. 17 to 18 and 21 to 22; Wikimedia Commons file descriptions only for photographs.

## The First Pylon
Kind: site
About: first-pylon, nectanebo-enclosure
The First Pylon is the biggest pylon in Egypt and was never finished. Nectanebo I (reigned 380 to 362 BC, Thirtieth Dynasty) began it as the monumental western entrance to the temple. It is about 113 metres wide and 14.5 metres thick at the base. The south tower rises 45 courses of stone, about 31.65 metres; the north tower only 32 courses, about 21.7 metres. Finished, the towers would have been 38 to 40 metres high. Digital Karnak's wording for the 113 metres is ambiguous, so treat the width as that of the whole front.

Why is it unfinished? Egyptian builders raised the masonry first, using ramps, then dressed and carved the surface from the top down as the ramps came away. The work stopped before that stage, so the front is bare and the upper edge ragged. The sources used do not say why work stopped; Nectanebo I's reign ended in 362 BC.

What it replaced. Shoshenq I's court (about 945 BC) had a western wall and gateway. The sources say Nectanebo removed the wall but kept the unfinished central stone gateway, 27.5 metres high and 7.4 metres wide, and built the pylon around it. Digital Karnak thinks Shoshenq I probably built the gateway. A smaller doorway, 10.85 metres high, was fitted in later, with wooden doors.

The wall. Nectanebo also enclosed the Amun-Ra temple, and the Mut and Montu temples, in huge mud-brick walls. The Amun-Ra wall was about 21 metres high and 12 metres thick, of mud brick and sandstone; he planned twelve gates and only four were finished. Digital Karnak suggests the pylon and wall may have been meant to protect the temple from foreign invaders.

Details. Look for the tall vertical slots in the front. Great wooden flagstaffs with cloth banners stood in notches in the stone, held by clamps. The front faces west, so at 08:30 it is in shade; the afternoon sun lights it.

Dates. Digital Karnak and Sullivan give 380 to 362 BC. The Historical Marker Database says about 350 BC, and one Commons description says it "may have been built by Nectanebo I" without certainty. An unsourced blog says Shoshenq I planned it as a copy of the Second Pylon; Sullivan says only that he began a gateway.
Sources: Digital Karnak, "1st Pylon" (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/1st-pylon; cites Hölscher 1943, Carlotti 1995, Lauffray 1970) and "Enclosures and Gates" (?p=730); Sullivan, UEE (2010), pp. 17 and 21; Great Hypostyle Hall Project, "Constructing the Hall" (University of Memphis).

## Builders' ramp behind the First Pylon
Kind: site
About: builders-ramp, first-pylon
The sloping heap of mud brick against the inside of the First Pylon, in the great court, is what is left of the ramp used to build it. Digital Karnak says plainly that remains of the mud-brick ramps "can still be seen inside the court today". The pylon was never completed, and the ramps were never taken down. The Great Hypostyle Hall Project captions its photograph "Remains of the mud-brick ramp used in the construction of the First Pylon". The stop sits on the south side, behind the south tower (the position is from photographs and map placement, not stated by Digital Karnak).

How it worked. The Great Hypostyle Hall Project describes the Egyptian method: builders laid a course of stone, then buried the whole area with earth before laying the next, and hauled each layer up mud-brick ramps until the top was reached. Afterwards the earth and ramps were removed, and masons smoothed the walls on the way down. The method needed no cranes: the building rose inside a mound of earth. Dieter Arnold's Building in Egypt (1997) is the standard reference for the ramp types, but it has not been read for this note.

What the ramp shows. Where the ramp survives, the carving and smoothing, which normally came last, were never done. This is the best place at Karnak to see the method in progress.

The court. To make room for ramps like this, Nectanebo I dismantled part of the north and south walls and columns on the far west side of Shoshenq I's court. Digital Karnak says the work was not completed and column drums were left completely undressed, and that the unfinished state is easy to spot. Rams from the avenue stand along the colonnades inside the court.

3D model. Digital Karnak's model of the pylon shows the unbuilt upper parts as transparent blocks; Sullivan's Architectural Features guide has an image of the pylon with remnants of the mud-brick ramp.

Access. The ramp is a fragile mound and is usually fenced. It is clearly visible from the path within a few metres.
Sources: Digital Karnak, "1st Pylon" and "Shoshenq I Court" (?p=4245); Sullivan, Architectural Features (Digital Karnak, 2008), caption "Pylon I with remnants of mud brick ramp in the Shoshenq I Court", citing Arnold 2003; Great Hypostyle Hall Project, "Constructing the Hall"; Wikimedia Commons file description for the brick photograph (pointer only).

## The Avenue of Sphinxes to Luxor and its 2021 reopening
Kind: reference
About: sphinx-avenue-reopening, luxor-sphinx-avenue
A processional road about 2.7 kilometres long (1.7 miles) joins Karnak to Luxor Temple. Sullivan gives about two kilometres. It was the route of the Opet festival, when statues of Amun and his family travelled to Luxor in a procession with dancers, and boats on the river celebrated the flood.

The road was paved and lined with sphinxes. Mansour Boraik says Amenhotep III started it and Nectanebo I completed it; Sullivan says Nectanebo added the sandstone paving and a series of human-headed sphinxes along the whole route, about 700 in all by their spacing, enclosed by mud-brick walls and planted with trees. Most of the sphinxes seen today are Nectanebo's. Part of the avenue was found in front of Luxor Temple in 1949 (Zakaria Ghonaim), and Mohamed Abdel Kadir cleared the first stretch between 1958 and 1960. Work halted in 2005 and resumed in 2017. About 50 years of excavation, removing hundreds of homes and mosques, preceded the reopening.

On 25 November 2021 Egypt held a televised ceremony, attended by President Abdel Fatah el-Sisi, to reopen the restored avenue. The next day it opened to visitors.

The ram avenue at the First Pylon is a different, short road.
Sources: PBS NewsHour, "Egypt unveils renovated Avenue of the Sphinxes in Luxor" (26 November 2021); The Art Newspaper, 25 November 2021; Digital Karnak, "Other Processional Ways"; Sullivan, UEE (2010), p. 21.

## Nectanebo I
Kind: person
About: nectanebo-i, nectanebo-enclosure
Nectanebo I (Nakhtnebef) was the first king of the Thirtieth Dynasty and reigned from 380 to 362 BC in Shaw's chronology. He was one of the last native Egyptian kings before the Persians returned in 343 BC, after the reign of Nectanebo II (360 to 343 BC). He launched a huge building programme across greater Karnak: he enclosed the temples of Amun-Ra, Mut and Montu in mud-brick walls, began the First Pylon, laid a sandstone pavement and rows of human-headed sphinxes along the avenue to Luxor, and added a gateway to the temple of Opet.
Sources: Sullivan, UEE (2010), p. 21; Digital Karnak, "1st Pylon" and "Enclosures and Gates"; Shaw (ed.), Oxford History of Ancient Egypt (2000), chronology; general knowledge for the return of the Persians in 343 BC.

## Sety II
Kind: person
About: sety-ii, sety-ii-obelisk
Sety II was a Nineteenth Dynasty king who reigned about 1200 to 1194 BC (Shaw), a son of Merenptah and grandson of Ramesses II. At Karnak he put a three-roomed quartzite and sandstone bark shrine in front of the old western gate (the Second Pylon of his day), with sanctuaries for Amun, Mut and Khonsu, where their boats rested on festival processions. He also had a small quartzite obelisk set up on the quay, probably one of a pair. His reign was short, about six years. His shrine is described with the great forecourt. The on-site board calls the shrine the "Chapels of Sety I", but the scholarly sources give Sety II.
Sources: Sullivan, UEE (2010), p. 16; Wikidata entry for the Karnak obelisk of Seti II (pointer); on-site board (source 9).

## The Triple Shrine of Sety II (the "Chapels of Sety I")
Kind: site
About: sety-ii-shrine, sety-ii
The small three-roomed building on the north (left) side of the great court is the triple bark shrine of Sety II, a Nineteenth Dynasty king who reigned from about 1200 to 1194 BC (Shaw's chronology). The on-site board calls it the "Chapels of Sety I". Scholarly sources disagree with the board: Digital Karnak and Henri Chevrier, who published the shrine in 1940 as "Le temple reposoir de Séti II", both give it to Sety II. Sety I (reigned about 1294 to 1279 BC) built the Hypostyle Hall just beyond, which may explain the mix-up, but that is a guess.

It is a bark shrine: a resting place for the portable boats (barks) of the gods when they were carried out of the temple on festival days. Its three sanctuary rooms were for Amun in the middle, Mut on the west and Khonsu on the east, the Theban triad. Sullivan writes that the boats "would have paused here during festival journeys before crossing the Nile". Digital Karnak adds that it may also have been a place where ordinary Egyptians could gather within the temple complex and pray.

When Sety II built it, it stood outside the temple's western entrance, in front of Horemheb's Second Pylon, on the east-west processional route. Ramesses III later built his own bark shrine opposite it. About 250 years later, Shoshenq I (945 to 924 BC) built a huge court around both shrines, so what was outside is now inside.

The building is small and survives almost complete. Digital Karnak gives its size as 21 metres across, 16.5 metres deep and 7.1 metres high, with a central doorway 4.5 metres high and side doorways 3.79 metres. The foundations, door sills and first course of blocks are quartzite, a very hard stone, and the rest is sandstone. The north-east corner and the west wall were left undressed, which suggests the work was never finished. The walls carry relief scenes of the king, and on the south face and entrance Sety II is shown offering to the Theban gods.

Digital Karnak notes that the shrine may have been partly dismantled when Nectanebo I (380 to 362 BC) built the First Pylon, then only partly rebuilt. It says "may", and the evidence is not given on the page.

What to look for: three doorways side by side, the king's figure in the carved scenes, and the plain, unfinished stonework on the less visible sides.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Sety II Shrine (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/Sety-II-Shrine); Sullivan, "The Development of the Temple of Karnak" (Digital Karnak, 2010), phase summaries for Sety II and Shoshenq I; Shaw (ed.), Oxford History of Ancient Egypt (2000), reign dates; site board (source 9); Chevrier 1940 cited in the Digital Karnak bibliography (not read).

## The Kiosk of Taharqa (the single column)
Kind: site
About: taharqa-kiosk, taharqa
The tall single column in the middle of the great court is the only one still standing of ten that made up the Kiosk of Taharqa, built between 690 and 664 BC (Shaw's chronology). The site board calls it the "Pavilion of Taharqa". Taharqa was a Kushite king of the Twenty-fifth Dynasty, from Nubia in modern Sudan.

Digital Karnak describes a free-standing structure of two rows of five large open-papyrus columns, joined by low screen walls, with doorways on all four sides. The overall footprint was about 20.5 metres by 29 metres. Each column was 2.99 metres across and about 21 metres high. The materials were limestone and rose granite, with a calcite (Egyptian alabaster) shrine at the centre which supported the bark of the god. Sullivan notes that the width of the kiosk, over 16 metres, "could not have been bridged with a roof", so scholars generally agree it was left open to the sky. It is possible that stone beams linked the tops of the columns. This is the clearest evidence about roofing in the court (see the section on the great court).

Excavations suggest Taharqa's kiosk replaced an earlier wooden kiosk on the same spot, and the alabaster altar is thought to belong to that earlier building. Much later, Ptolemy IV added decoration to the screen walls showing nome-gods (the gods of Egypt's regions) in procession, and the rose granite paving may have been added then too.

What it was for is unclear. Some scholars think it was used in the "union of the disc", a yearly rite involving the cult statue of Amun-Ra. Jean Lauffray, who studied it in 1970 and 1975, titled his work on it a "colonnade-propylée", a columned entrance porch, but Digital Karnak does not adopt a single explanation.

Taharqa also appears in the Bible as "Tirhakah king of Ethiopia" (2 Kings 19:9), who set out against the Assyrian king Sennacherib. That story is set in 701 BC, but Taharqa did not become king until about 690 BC (Shaw). Scholars explain this in different ways: that he was then a young prince leading an army for an older king, that the biblical writer used the title he later held, or that the text refers to a different campaign. The question is debated and is not settled (secondary summary only, verify).

What to look for: the huge papyrus-flower capital, the drums of the shaft (built up of stone courses), the stumps of the other nine columns and the calcite altar.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Taharqo Kiosk (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/Taharqo-Kiosk); Sullivan, "The Development of the Temple of Karnak" (2010), Taharqo entry; Digital Karnak, Shoshenq I Court; Shaw (2000); Wikipedia, Taharqa, as a pointer to Kitchen (1996) and Pope (2022) on 701 BC (secondary, not checked against those works).

## The Temple of Ramesses III in the forecourt
Kind: site
About: ramesses-iii-temple, ramesses-iii
On the south (right) side of the great court is a complete small temple built by Ramesses III, who reigned about 1184 to 1153 BC (Shaw's chronology). The site board lists it as "Temple of Ramesses III". Digital Karnak calls it a bark shrine: it was built to house the sacred barks for the gods' festival day journeys. It sits at right angles to the traditional east-west processional route and was set opposite the shrine of Sety II.

The temple takes the shape and size of a small temple. Digital Karnak gives its overall size as 27.5 metres across, 60.5 metres deep and 14 metres high. From the court you reach it through a small pylon carved with scenes of the king smiting his enemies, with a granite gateway and no flagstaffs. Beyond lies an open court with pillars carrying statues of the king, a small vestibule, a hypostyle hall 21.25 metres wide and 9 metres deep, and a sanctuary. Instead of a single holy of holies, the sanctuary has three bark shrines, for Amun, Mut and Khonsu.

The statues in the court show the king in the form of the god Osiris. Digital Karnak says "sixteen engaged statues" stood on the pillars. Other descriptions give eight, which may count one side only. I could not settle the count from sources I could open, so treat it as uncertain and let the family count them.

It is a good model for the ruined parts of Karnak. Almost all the other temples here survive as plans or fragments. Here a pylon, a court, a hall and sanctuary rooms stand together at small scale, so you can see how the standard pieces of an Egyptian temple fitted.

About 240 years after it was built, Shoshenq I built his great court around the front part of the temple, so the pylon end is now inside the court. The Oriental Institute's Epigraphic Survey published the temple's walls in "Reliefs and Inscriptions at Karnak" (1936 and later), and Chevrier published the building in 1933. Ramesses III also began the Temple of Khonsu in the south of the precinct.

The inner rooms may be gated. If so, the court, pylon and sanctuary doors are all visible from the doorway.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Ramesses III Temple (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/Ramesses-III-Temple); Sullivan, "The Development of the Temple of Karnak" (2010); Epigraphic Survey, Reliefs and Inscriptions at Karnak I (OIP 25, 1936), landing page only; Shaw (2000); Madain Project and Smithsonian record for the differing statue counts (secondary).

## The Bubastite Portal and Shoshenq I's campaign relief
Kind: site
About: bubastite-portal, shoshenq-campaign, shoshenq-i
In the south-east corner of the great court a side doorway leads out to the south. It is called the Bubastite Portal, after Bubastis in the Nile Delta, home city of the king who built it, Shoshenq I (945 to 924 BC, Shaw). Digital Karnak says the doorway is just under 5 metres wide. The Epigraphic Survey (1954) describes it as the south entrance to the first court, designed as a uniform part of the court's wall and colonnade, built to fill the space between the Second Pylon and the Ramesses III temple. Two closed papyrus columns inside the court mark it.

The relief is carved on the outer (south) face of the wall, beside and over the doorway. It shows Shoshenq, the largest figure, striding forward to smite his enemies. Amun and the goddess of Thebes hold cords that bind the hands and throats of rows of captives. Each captive is a name ring, an oval shaped like a crenellated city wall, holding the name of a town or people. Digital Karnak counts 156 rings, while an introduction in Wikipedia gives 155 and notes many names are lost. The relief is dated to about 925 BC.

It matters because it is a rare Egyptian account of Canaan in the tenth century BC. Shoshenq is usually identified with Shishak, who took treasures from Jerusalem in 1 Kings 14:25 to 26 and 2 Chronicles 12. Jerusalem is not among the surviving names on the list. Scholars have proposed that it was erased, that Rehoboam's tribute saved the city, or that the list was partly copied from an older king. The identification is widely accepted but debated, and so is how historical the biblical account is: Israel Finkelstein treats the plunder story as a theological construct, and newer excavation dating at Tel Gezer fits the biblical date poorly (all via Wikipedia, verify).

A quarry stela at Gebel el-Silsila, dated to Shoshenq's year 21 and published by Caminos in 1952, records a new quarry for a pylon and festival hall at Karnak, for "the first campaign of victory". A fragment of a victory stela with his name was found at Megiddo in 1925, though some now suggest it may be architectural.

Digital Karnak records that no attempt was made to repair the broken or damaged areas of the relief, so what you see is the surviving original.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Bubastite Portal (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/bubastite-portal); Sullivan, "The Development of the Temple of Karnak" (2010); Epigraphic Survey, Reliefs and Inscriptions at Karnak III: The Bubastite Portal (OIP 74, 1954), introductory text; Wikipedia, Bubastite Portal and Shoshenq I, as pointers to Caminos (1952), Wilson (2005), Finkelstein (2002, 2006) and Webster and others (2023), not checked against those works; Shaw (2000).

## Shoshenq I's court (the great court or first court)
Kind: site
About: shoshenq-court, shoshenq-i
The great open court between the First and Second Pylons is the work of Shoshenq I (945 to 924 BC). The site board labels it the "Bubastite Courtyard". Digital Karnak gives it as 82 metres wide and 101 metres deep. It enclosed the shrine of Sety II and the front of the temple of Ramesses III, which had stood outside the earlier entrance. Its north and south sides were lined with sandstone columns with papyrus-bud capitals. The western side had a monumental gate with an opening of 17.7 metres and a total height of 27.5 metres. A stela of his year 21 describes the project as a "festival court for the house of his father Amun-Ra".

Nectanebo I (380 to 362 BC) removed the court's west wall to build the First Pylon. Parts of the side walls and columns on the far west were taken down for construction ramps, then rebuilt with new stone, and the work was left unfinished: the column drums there are undressed. Remnants of the mud-brick ramp still stand in the court. Only the west wall has gone.

The ram-headed sphinxes along the sides may have been moved here when the court was built or when Taharqa added his kiosk. Sullivan says that today's arrangement dates from the Roman reorganisation of the entrance.

Was the court ever roofed? I found no source that says so. The sources describe an open court. The only structure that is explicitly said to be unroofed is Taharqa's kiosk, whose 16 metre span was too wide to roof. A court 82 by 101 metres could not have been roofed in stone with Egyptian beams, but that is my inference. The side colonnades and the rooms of the Ramesses III temple and Sety II shrine would have had roofs, and Digital Karnak does not say which roof slabs survive. Treat the roofing of the colonnades as unconfirmed.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Shoshenq I Court (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/?p=4245); Sullivan, "The Development of the Temple of Karnak" (2010); Digital Karnak, Taharqo Kiosk; Shaw (2000); site board (source 9).

## Bark shrines and festival journeys
Kind: reference
About: sety-ii-shrine, ramesses-iii-temple
Egyptian gods were believed to live in their cult statues. On festival days the statue was placed in a small shrine on a model boat, called a bark, and carried on poles on the shoulders of priests. The most famous journeys at Karnak were to Luxor Temple at the Opet festival and across the Nile to the west bank for the Beautiful Festival of the Valley (general knowledge).

Along the route, small bark shrines gave the boats a place to rest. Digital Karnak says Sety II's shrine and Ramesses III's temple were each a bark station of this kind, placed on the western approach to the temple in front of Horemheb's Second Pylon. Sullivan's account of the Sety II shrine says the boats of Amun, Mut and Khonsu "would have paused here during festival journeys before crossing the Nile". Ramesses III's version grew into a small temple, with a court, hall and three sanctuaries.

Taharqa's kiosk had a calcite shrine at its centre which supported the bark of the god. It may have served the same purpose.
Sources: Sullivan, "The Development of the Temple of Karnak" (2010); Digital Karnak pages for Sety II Shrine, Ramesses III Temple and Taharqo Kiosk; general knowledge for the festival background.

## Shoshenq I (the biblical Shishak)
Kind: person
About: shoshenq-i, bubastite-portal
Shoshenq I founded the Twenty-second (Libyan) Dynasty. He reigned from about 945 to 924 BC in Shaw's chronology, which Kitchen also used. Other scholars give different dates: Krauss argued for 943 BC and Schneider for 962 to 941 BC (from Wikipedia's summary, verify). His family came from Bubastis in the Delta and descended from Libyans who had settled in Egypt (general knowledge).

At Karnak he built the great court and carved the Bubastite Portal relief. A quarry stela at Gebel el-Silsila dated to his year 21 records a new quarry for a pylon and festival hall at Karnak, overseen by his architect Horemakhet. Much of his Karnak work was left unfinished, apart from the campaign scenes.

The campaign is usually dated about 925 BC. He is generally identified with Shishak (1 Kings 14:25 to 26; 2 Chronicles 12:2 to 9), who attacked Judah in the fifth year of Rehoboam. The Karnak list names places from the Negev to Galilee but not Jerusalem. The identification has its critics, and so has the usual date: Finkelstein has argued that it rests on the biblical reference alone.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Bubastite Portal and Shoshenq I Court; Wikipedia, Shoshenq I, as a pointer to Caminos (1952), Krauss, Schneider, Finkelstein (2006) (not checked against the originals); Shaw (2000).

## Taharqa
Kind: person
About: taharqa, taharqa-kiosk
Taharqa was a king of the Kushite Twenty-fifth Dynasty, which came from Nubia and ruled Egypt as well as Kush. His reign is dated about 690 to 664 BC (Shaw). He is named in the Bible as Tirhakah (2 Kings 19:9, Isaiah 37:9). He built at Karnak, notably the kiosk in the great court, a porch of twenty columns at the Khonsu temple and another at the eastern temple of Ramesses II (Sullivan). He is also known for a large pyramid at Nuri in Sudan (Wikipedia, as a pointer; verify).

Assyria invaded Egypt during his reign: Esarhaddon took Memphis in 671 BC and Taharqa fled south, and Ashurbanipal's forces later reached Thebes. He died in 664 BC (Shaw), and the Assyrian sack of Thebes is dated 664 BC in the tour's timeline. The Kawa stelae, texts from his temple in Nubia, say a predecessor sent young Nubians north to Thebes and Taharqa was among them. One reading puts him at about twenty then, though scholars warn the text is not a reliable chronicle (Wikipedia's summary of Broekman 2015, verify).
Sources: Sullivan, "The Development of the Temple of Karnak" (2010); Shaw (2000); Wikipedia, Taharqa, as a pointer to Kitchen (1996), Pope (2022) and Welsby (1996) (not checked).

## Sety II and Ramesses III
Kind: person
About: sety-ii, ramesses-iii, sety-ii-shrine, ramesses-iii-temple
Sety II (reigned about 1200 to 1194 BC, Shaw) built the triple bark shrine, in front of the Second Pylon. Ramesses III (about 1184 to 1153 BC) built his bark temple opposite it, and the Temple of Khonsu in the south. Only about ten years separate the end of Sety II's reign from the start of Ramesses III's (Shaw), though Sullivan notes the two shrines were added roughly a century after Horemheb's Second Pylon. The two buildings show a difference of ambition: Sety II's is a plain three-roomed shrine, while Ramesses III's grew into a miniature temple with its own pylon, court and hall. Between them they gave the temple's western approach two bark stations facing each other, until Shoshenq I enclosed both in his court.

Ramesses III is the last great warrior king of the New Kingdom. His main monument is his mortuary temple at Medinet Habu on the west bank, and Papyrus Harris I records the wealth he gave to Amun's temples (general knowledge, verify). At Karnak, the Epigraphic Survey of the Oriental Institute published his bark temple in Reliefs and Inscriptions at Karnak, volumes I and II.
Sources: Sullivan, "The Development of the Temple of Karnak" (2010); Shaw (2000); Digital Karnak, Ramesses III Temple; general knowledge for Medinet Habu and Papyrus Harris I.

## The Second Pylon and the colossus of Ramesses II
Kind: site
About: second-pylon, second-pylon-colossus
The Second Pylon is the tall, ruined gateway between the Great Court and the Great Hypostyle Hall. Horemheb, the last king of the Eighteenth Dynasty (ruled 1323 to 1295 BC in Shaw's chronology, which this guide uses), built it as a new western entrance to the temple. It stayed the front of the temple for centuries. About a hundred years after Horemheb's death Sety II and Ramesses III built bark shrines in front of it; Pinedjem I set ram-headed sphinxes before it; and Shoshenq I (945 to 924 BC) later enclosed the space and the two shrines in the great court. The University of Memphis hall project calls the pylon the hall's "huge front door".

What is inside it: Horemheb tore down the temples of Akhenaten in eastern Karnak and reused their small, standard sandstone blocks (talatat) as internal fill in his pylons, the Second among them. So the grand entrance to the hall is stuffed with the rubble of a king the Egyptians later tried to forget.

The carved passage through the pylon was decorated by Ramesses I, who reigned for less than two years (about 1295 to 1294 BC). He carved in raised relief. Ramesses II later recut the scenes in sunk relief with his own name, leaving faint traces of the first carving, and he was officially given credit for the pylon. Ramesses I also built a small "station of the king" against the pylon's south-east side; only a calcite floor inscribed with the "nine bows", the traditional symbol of Egypt's enemies, survives. Part of the gateway's roof fell in the first millennium BC and damaged the reliefs below; Ptolemy VI later had the worst-hit scenes in the eastern passage recarved.

The colossus beside the doorway is Ramesses II with his daughter Bent'anta (also written Bintanath) shown tiny at his feet; her size marks her rank, not her height. Digital Karnak dates it to the Nineteenth Dynasty (1279 to 1213 BC) and records the stone as sandstone, and Wikimedia Commons also lists sandstone. Some captions say pink granite; the statue in photographs looks like sandstone. It was usurped: Digital Karnak says the High Priest Pinedjem and Ramesses VI (1143 to 1136 BC) both put their names on it. Digital Karnak lists Pinedjem first, but Ramesses VI reigned long before Pinedjem, so his inscription must be the earlier. Pinedjem I, High Priest of Amun at Thebes, ruled Upper Egypt almost as a king; a 2015 article by Gabriella Dembitz studies an offering of Maat in Pinedjem's name on this statue. Re-cutting names on old statues was common at Karnak. The height is not given in Digital Karnak; one art-archive listing says about 15 metres, which is not confirmed.
Sources: Sullivan, "The Development of the Temple of Karnak" (UEE 2010 / Digital Karnak), the Horemheb, Ramesses I, Pinedjem I and Shoshenq I entries and the section on the Nineteenth Dynasty; Sullivan, "Construction Methods and Building Materials" (Digital Karnak, 2008), the section "Reuse of building materials"; Great Hypostyle Hall Project (Brand), "Entrance" page (memphis.edu/hypostyle/tour_hall/entrance.php); Digital Karnak, Hypostyle Hall page (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/hypostyle-hall/) for Ramesses I's station; Digital Karnak, "Colossal Statue of Ramesses II and Daughter" (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/?p=6438); Wikimedia Commons category "Statue of Pinedjem I in Karnak" (material, pointer only); Dembitz 2015 (cited in Wikipedia's Pinedjem I article, pointer only).

## The Second Pylon nearly falls, 1899 to 1900
Kind: site
About: second-pylon, hypostyle-collapse
The Second Pylon nearly fell in 1899 to 1900. When the hall's columns fell on 3 October 1899, two of them fell against the north half of this pylon. By December blocks were falling, cracks had opened and the wall bulged 8 to 10 metres up. On 19 January 1900 a small avalanche of stones fell in the passage between the court of Taharqa and the hall, and the two overhanging beams, the remains of the lintel, almost lost their balance. Engineers, including M. Ehrlich, who was working on the Aswan dam, propped the pylon with timber shoeing between its two halves, a wall of sandbags on a stone base, an east buttress 11 metres long and 11.85 metres high, and cement and pebbles poured into the cracks. Maspero warned that if the pylon fell, the falling stones could topple columns in a chain and ruin the whole hall.

The same report shows how the work was financed and organised. A special credit of £2,600 from the Caisse de la Dette (the international commission that controlled Egypt's debt) paid for taking down the columns, and a further £1,600 was asked for the pylon, whose repair M. Ehrlich was asked to take charge of. The Egypt Exploration Fund's editors listed the preservation of Karnak, "where columns falling and a pylon giving way threaten irreparable disasters", among the tasks facing Maspero as he began his second term as head of the Antiquities Service. The report calls this gateway the "pylon of Rameses II"; this guide treats it as the Second Pylon, between the court of Taharqa and the hall.
Sources: Egypt Exploration Fund, Archaeological Report 1899 to 1900, pp. 9 to 11, quoting Maspero's official report (archive.org dli.ministry.27742); Egypt Exploration Fund, Archaeological Report 1900 to 1901, pp. 8 to 9 (Ehrlich's report in Annales du Service des Antiquités 1, p. 200); Great Hypostyle Hall Project, "Entrance" page (Ramesses II credited with the Second Pylon).

## The Great Hypostyle Hall
Kind: site
About: hypostyle-hall
A hypostyle hall is a hall whose roof is held up by columns. Karnak's is the largest in Egypt: 134 sandstone columns in a space about 103 metres wide and 52 metres deep (Digital Karnak), roughly 5,000 square metres. The Memphis project says almost all of Notre-Dame cathedral in Paris would fit inside. It lies between Horemheb's Second Pylon at the west end and Amenhotep III's Third Pylon at the east end.

Twelve huge columns line the central nave. They have open papyrus-flower capitals 5.4 metres across (the Memphis project says wide enough to hold a hundred men), each topped by an abacus 1.4 metres deep. On either side stand 122 smaller columns, 61 on each side, with closed-bud papyrus capitals. The column heights vary by source. For the central twelve: about 20 metres (Memphis architecture page), 21 metres (Digital Karnak, Sullivan, and another Memphis page). For the 122 smaller columns: 14 metres (Memphis architecture page), 15 metres including the capital with a 12-metre shaft (Digital Karnak), and 12 metres (Sullivan, and another Memphis page). The differences probably come from whether capitals and abaci are counted. A figure of 24 metres sometimes quoted for the central columns is the height of the top of the clerestory windows (Digital Karnak), not the columns. This guide says about 20 to 21 metres and about 12 to 15 metres.

Who built it: Sety I (ruled 1294 to 1279 BC) built the walls, columns and roof. His son Ramesses II (1279 to 1213 BC) finished the decoration, switching from fine raised relief to quicker sunk relief. Earlier scholars gave the hall to Horemheb, Ramesses I or Amenhotep III. Peter Brand showed that the clerestory windows and architraves above the central nave were carved while the construction ramps were still in place, and that they bear Sety I's name, which proves Sety built the hall. The Memphis project says it may have begun under Ramesses I, who reigned under two years; Digital Karnak says Ramesses I added only a small station at the Second Pylon. Some scholars suggest Horemheb built a papyrus-column peristyle on the site first. Later, Ramesses IV added cartouches and scenes to the central columns, and Ramesses VI carved his name over Ramesses IV's.

What it was for: in the Eighteenth Dynasty coronation and jubilee rituals took place in the Wadjet Hall; in the Nineteenth Dynasty the hypostyle hall became the grander setting. Inscriptions suggest some Thebans could enter on special occasions to see the king or the god in his portable boat. The papyrus columns are usually read as a stone papyrus marsh, the first land of creation (general knowledge). In many of the side aisles the roof slabs have fallen (photographs show open sky between the columns), and the Memphis project's own pages differ on how many slabs remain.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Hypostyle Hall (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/hypostyle-hall/); Great Hypostyle Hall Project (Brand), "About the Architecture", "About the Hall" and "Great Columns" pages (memphis.edu/hypostyle/); Sullivan, "The Development of the Temple of Karnak" (UEE 2010 / Digital Karnak), the Sety I and Ramesses II entries and the section on the Nineteenth Dynasty; Brand 2004 and Brand 2000 as cited there; general knowledge (papyrus-marsh reading).

## How the Hypostyle Hall was built
Kind: site
About: hypostyle-hall
There were no cranes. The builders worked with earth and mud brick. Digital Karnak and the Memphis project describe the sequence: first the foundations, then the column bases and the lowest courses of the walls, then the whole area was buried in earth. Each new course was laid on top and buried in turn, and mud-brick ramps let the builders drag the next layer of stone up. When the roof was done, the earth and ramps were removed and the masons smoothed the walls from the top down. The quarrymen had only dressed the tops and bottoms of the blocks, so the outer faces were rough until then.

Foundations: a trench was dug and partly filled with clean sand. According to Digital Karnak the hall's foundations were about half a metre of sand inside a stone lining, topped by a layer of stone. Sand spreads weight and soaks up shocks. The upper foundation courses used small sandstone talatat blocks from Akhenaten's demolished temples, a weak link (see the collapse of 1899).

The stone: sandstone, probably from Gebel el-Silsila, about 160 kilometres south of Thebes, floated downstream on barges. Quarry inscriptions of Sety I's early years there mention blocks intended for Thebes, and the hall was likely a main destination (Sullivan, citing Brand). Shipping records from Ramesses II's reign, for blocks sent from Gebel el-Silsila to his mortuary temple on the west bank, show barges carrying five to seven large blocks each, with ten ships delivering sixty-four large blocks in a day.

Columns: shafts and capitals were stacked from drums or half-drums, centred with plumb lines. Maspero's official report on the rescue of 1899 to 1900 says the half-drums of column 46 weighed about five tons each. Digital Karnak says wooden dovetail cramps joined the hall's architraves and column drums. Digital Karnak adds that carved details were usually cut after the drums were stacked.

The roof: the architraves, the beams spanning from column to column, are made of up to four separate blocks (two over two), because single blocks that long were too hard to quarry and move. Ceiling slabs rested across the architraves. One architrave, linking columns 26 and 17, measured 15 cubic metres and weighed about 37 tons.

Carving: most walls and columns were carved from scaffolding after the ramps came down. The clerestory, 24 metres up, was carved while the ramps were still there. The Memphis project notes that scaffolding appears in the tomb of Rekhmire.
Sources: Great Hypostyle Hall Project, "Building the Hall and Decorating the Walls" (memphis.edu/hypostyle/tour_hall/constructing_hall.php); Sullivan, "Construction Methods and Building Materials" (Digital Karnak, 2008), sections on foundations, building techniques, quarrying, column construction and roofing (citing Brand 2000: 359; Golvin and Goyon 1987; Arnold 1991; Josephson 2005); Egypt Exploration Fund, Archaeological Report 1899 to 1900, p. 10 (Maspero's report); Great Hypostyle Hall Project, 2011 field report (the ground level raised course by course); Great Hypostyle Hall Project, "The Great Hypostyle Hall" page (clerestory height).

## Light in the Hypostyle Hall: the clerestory
Kind: site
About: hypostyle-hall
The roofed hall was dim. The twelve central columns are taller than the 122 beside them, so the central roof stands higher than the side roofs. The gap between the two roof levels was filled with stone window grilles, the "clerestory", set on the first rows of smaller columns on either side of the nave. The Memphis project calls the grilles the main source of light in the building. Digital Karnak says each grille was made of two sections stacked one on the other and held in grooves cut in the side of the piers. They let in sunlight while keeping the space private and secret.

The roof slabs also carried small square holes or slits to admit faint light, and some architraves have small slots. The effect was shafts and stripes of light in a forest of columns, with the roofed parts dark. Digital Karnak's 3D model includes a rendering of the roofed hall, to show how much darker it was.

The clerestory windows are decorated too: the window frames are inscribed, and on the outside of the clerestory the architraves repeat Sety I's titles. Ramesses II later recut some of Sety's cartouches on the grilles. Digital Karnak places the top of the clerestory at 24 metres.

Today the picture is different. The Memphis project's architecture page says nearly all the roof slabs have fallen, so the hall is flooded with sunlight; another of its pages says almost none of the roof slabs have fallen. Photographs show the side aisles open to the sky in many places and the central nave still roofed, with the grilles standing high on either side. The underside of an architrave (the soffit), sheltered from weather, still keeps its bright original colours in places.

Look for: the best view of a grille is down the central aisle looking up, with the two rows of tall columns on each side.
Sources: Great Hypostyle Hall Project, "Clerestory Roof" page (memphis.edu/hypostyle/tour_hall/clerestory_roof.php) and "About the Architecture" page; Sullivan, "Construction Methods and Building Materials" (Digital Karnak, 2008), section "Lighting"; Digital Karnak, Hypostyle Hall page (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/hypostyle-hall/) and Sullivan, "Development of the Temple of Karnak" (model rendering caption); Wikimedia Commons photographs by Olaf Tausch (Karnak Tempel Hypostyl series) for the present state; the present-day extent of the central roof is from photographs, not a text source.

## The collapse of 1899 and Legrain's rebuilding
Kind: site
About: hypostyle-collapse, hypostyle-restoration, georges-legrain
On 3 October 1899 eleven columns of the hall's northern wing fell in a chain reaction; the crash was reportedly heard at Luxor Temple, a few miles south. Maspero's official report, quoted by the Egypt Exploration Fund, says five more columns (numbers 23, 26, 32, 44 and 46) were badly damaged. Two of the falling columns hit the Second Pylon.

Why: the Memphis project says the foundations were made of small sandstone talatat blocks from Akhenaten's demolished monuments, which could not carry the weight of 134 columns and a stone roof, and which were crumbling in salt-laden groundwater. Digital Karnak adds a second view. The foundations were sand inside a stone lining, topped by the talatat; older scholars blamed the talatat, but one Egyptologist (probably Josephson 2005) has suggested that archaeologists' trenches, dug to drain the flooded hall, disturbed the sand layers and unsettled the foundations. A 2011 Memphis report calls the event an earthquake, which does not match the other accounts. The columns had stood for over 3,000 years.

The rescue: Georges Legrain arrived on 11 December 1899. He piled a great bank of earth around column 46 and raised a differential pulley on it. The abacus came off on 29 December, then the half-drums, each about five tons, one by one. By 10 January 1900 the column was a stump 6 metres high. Column 44 took twelve days to dismantle. A special grant of £2,600 from the Caisse de la Dette paid for it. Column 26 was linked to column 17 by an architrave of 15 cubic metres weighing about 37 tons. In the 1900 to 1901 season Legrain removed the fallen drums and architraves and cleared the ground for new foundations; he reported in the Annales du Service des Antiquités (volume 1, pages 121 and 193).

The northern half was taken apart and rebuilt on new foundations. The southern wing was saved without dismantling: new brick foundations were put under most columns, one column at a time, while they stood. Wikipedia's Legrain article (a pointer) says he recorded the work as complete on 15 May 1902. Some northern architraves were replaced with hollow metal-and-concrete copies. Legrain never re-erected about half of the displaced abaci, and some that went back are held together with modern cement. In one case (column 96, probably re-erected by Legrain) Sety I's relief ended up on the wrong side.
Sources: Great Hypostyle Hall Project, "Early Recording and Restoration" (memphis.edu/hypostyle/project/r_methods.php), "About the Architecture" and 2011 field report (memphis.edu/hypostyle/project/2011_seasonreport_web.php); Egypt Exploration Fund, Archaeological Report 1899 to 1900, pp. 9 to 11 (Maspero's report via Quibell), and Archaeological Report 1900 to 1901, pp. 8 to 9 (archive.org dli.ministry.27742 and dli.ministry.27743); Sullivan, "Construction Methods and Building Materials" (Digital Karnak, 2008), section "Foundations", citing Josephson 2005; Wikipedia, Georges Legrain (pointer; cites Annales du Service 4, 1902, p. 38).

## Paint, cleaning and decay in the Hypostyle Hall
Kind: site
About: hypostyle-cleaning, hypostyle-hall
The hall was once painted. Sety I's sculptors finished some reliefs in paint alone, and a few paint-only details survive, such as the fans beside the bark shrine on the north wall. Sety's artists may also have drawn coloured cartoons as a stand-in for carving, as survives unfinished at Abydos (the Memphis project's suggestion). In the south-east corner, partly sheltered from sun and weather, much original colour remains. Many of the fallen blocks lying north of the hall still have traces of paint, and Digital Karnak notes that remains of colourful paint on Karnak's columns show that some were extensively painted. The soffits of architraves keep bright colours, and traces of paint survive on the abaci and on some columns.

Cleaning: in July 2021 the Egyptian antiquities authorities began cleaning the columns, starting with the twelve central ones. The first column, once the sand and dust were removed, revealed colours and engraved details applied in antiquity (Ahram Online, reported in Archaeology magazine, August 2021). By January 2023 thirty-seven columns were done, cleared of sand, dust and bird debris. In January 2024 the Supreme Council of Antiquities said 95 per cent of the columns were restored, with their original engravings and colours revealed, by Egyptian restorers working to international standards. Reports differ on how many columns were in the first phase (twelve central columns in 2021; twenty-eight in the 2024 report). The reports describe cleaning; one summary also mentions strengthening weak colours, which I could not confirm.

Decay: groundwater carrying salts rises in the stone and attacks the surface, destroying the reliefs. The Memphis project says it is most visible in the southern half, and that since disastrous flash floods in autumn 1994 large patches along the base of the walls are crusted with salt. The northern wing, with Sety I's raised reliefs, shows the likely end: large portions are missing and in places the whole lower register is gone. Reliefs also fall from the walls: more than 300 carved blocks lie in the yards around the temple.
Sources: Great Hypostyle Hall Project, "Interior Wall Scenes" (memphis.edu/hypostyle/tour_hall/interior_scenes.php), "Clerestory Roof", 2011 field report, "Blocks" page and "Monuments" page on salt (memphis.edu/hypostyle/project/e_monuments.php); Sullivan, "Construction Methods and Building Materials" (Digital Karnak, 2008); Archaeology magazine, 10 August 2021 (archaeology.org/news/2021/08/10/210811-karnak-columns-cleaned/) and 19 January 2023 (archaeology.org/news/2023/01/19/230120-egypt-clean-columns/); Egyptian Streets, 21 January 2024 (reporting the Supreme Council of Antiquities). The Ahram Online originals were not fetched (access refused).

## The battle reliefs of Sety I on the north wall
Kind: site
About: sety-battle-reliefs
The outside of the hall's north wall is covered with the earliest of the great Ramesside war scenes: Sety I (ruled 1294 to 1279 BC) fighting in Canaan, Lebanon, Syria and Libya. The date is about 1290 BC, within his reign. The scenes are laid out symmetrically on either side of the north gateway, in three rows. As you face the wall from outside, east is on your left.

The east half shows Sety's first-year campaign. He is told the Shasu, nomads of Canaan, are plotting rebellion, marches along the fortified "Ways of Horus" through Sinai to Gaza, fights the Shasu, attacks Yenoam, receives tribute and makes Lebanese chiefs cut cedar trees. The top row on this half has not survived. The west half shows later wars. In the surviving top scene he attacks Kadesh, on the Orontes. The middle row shows the Libyan war, the best preserved, with Sety spearing a Libyan chief from his chariot. The bottom row shows the Hittite war, in which Sety defeats the Hittite army and kills the king of Carchemish, a Hittite prince. The Memphis project says the Hittites soon regained the area. This is the conflict Ramesses II inherited.

Everywhere the king wins alone. The Egyptian army is absent; only a small figure behind the king in some scenes is labelled Crown Prince Ramesses. Beneath it is another name: the Epigraphic Survey found the original figure was a military officer named Mehy, later replaced by Ramesses II. Why Mehy appears is unexplained. Digital Karnak says the king dwarfs all the other figures and is instantly recognisable in his chariot or smiting enemies. The reliefs are royal propaganda, but they are also evidence for Egypt's foreign relations before Ramesses II's peace treaty with the Hittites.

The war scenes are in bold sunk relief, unlike the fine raised relief Sety's carvers used inside the hall. Salt-laden groundwater is damaging the reliefs of the hall's northern wing, and in places the lowest register is lost. The Epigraphic Survey of the University of Chicago recorded the scenes in the 1970s and 1980s and published them as Reliefs and Inscriptions at Karnak, volume IV (1985 to 1986; the Memphis page gives 1985, Digital Karnak cites 1986). On the hall's south wall Ramesses II added his own war scenes (the Memphis project says wars in Syria, Canaan, Lebanon and Libya). The north wall faces north, so in March it is in shade all day.
Sources: Great Hypostyle Hall Project, "War Scenes of Seti I" (memphis.edu/hypostyle/tour_hall/seti_scenes.php) and "The War Scenes of Seti I" (memphis.edu/hypostyle/sety-i_war-scenes/index.php), "Reliefs and Inscriptions" page, "Monuments" page on salt; Digital Karnak, "North Exterior Wall" (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/north-exterior-wall); Sullivan, "The Development of the Temple of Karnak" (UEE 2010 / Digital Karnak), Sety I and Ramesses II entries; Epigraphic Survey, Reliefs and Inscriptions at Karnak IV: The Battle Reliefs of King Sety I (OIP 107, 1986); Murnane, The Road to Kadesh (SAOC 42, 1990), cited by the Memphis page. The north wall's orientation is from the hall's east to west axis (general knowledge).

## Digital Karnak and the 3D model of the hall
Kind: reference
About: hypostyle-hall
Digital Karnak is a 3D computer model and website, begun at the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) and now hosted by the University of California, Santa Cruz (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu). It reconstructs the temple of Amun-Ra as it stood in different reigns, so you can see what has been lost.

Its model of the Hypostyle Hall is based on the published drawings of Carlotti (1995), Golvin (1987) and Clarke and Engelbach (1990). It shows the hall as Sety I and Ramesses II left it, with the roof on. Most of the hall and its columns are given a plain sandstone texture, patterned to echo the size of the big column drums. Photographs of the "daily ritual" reliefs on the north part of the east interior wall are placed in the model. The carved scenes on the other walls and on all 134 columns are not in the model. It does not include the theory that Horemheb built a papyrus-column peristyle on the site first. A rendering with the roof shows how much darker the hall once was.

The site also has pages for the Second Pylon, the north exterior wall (with Sety I's battles), the colossus, and essays by Elaine Sullivan on the development of the temple and on its construction methods, which this guide uses.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Hypostyle Hall page (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/hypostyle-hall/); Digital Karnak, North Exterior Wall page; Sullivan, "The Development of the Temple of Karnak" (Digital Karnak, 2010).

## Sety I
Kind: person
About: sety-i
Sety I was the second king of the Nineteenth Dynasty. He ruled from 1294 to 1279 BC in Shaw's chronology. He was the son of Ramesses I and the father of Ramesses II.

At Karnak he built most of the Great Hypostyle Hall (the walls, the columns and the roof), built it against the Third Pylon, covering that pylon's west face, and began the hall's south exterior wall. His sculptors decorated the northern half of the hall in fine raised relief, and the outside of the north wall with his wars. He died with the south wing only partly decorated, and Ramesses II finished it. The Memphis project says his finest reliefs are at his own temple at Abydos, where the limestone allowed finer carving; Karnak's coarser sandstone is less fine.

The war scenes show him in a chariot defeating Shasu nomads, Hittites and Libyans, and capturing Kadesh. Quarry inscriptions of his early years at Gebel el-Silsila mention sandstone for Thebes, probably for the hall.
Sources: Sullivan, "The Development of the Temple of Karnak" (UEE 2010 / Digital Karnak); Digital Karnak, Hypostyle Hall and North Exterior Wall pages; Great Hypostyle Hall Project, "Interior Wall Scenes" and "War Scenes of Seti I" pages; Shaw (ed.), The Oxford History of Ancient Egypt (2000), for the reign dates.

## Ramesses II at Karnak
Kind: person
About: ramesses-ii
Ramesses II ruled from 1279 to 1213 BC (Shaw's chronology), for 66 years. At the Great Hypostyle Hall he finished what his father Sety I had started. The Memphis project says the decoration was completed in the first two years of his reign, and he switched from slow raised relief to quicker sunk relief, recutting some of his father's scenes in the south wing. He decorated the twelve central columns with scenes of himself offering to the gods, and his later additions to those columns date to before his twenty-first year. He was credited with the Second Pylon and recut reliefs of Horemheb and Ramesses I there. On the outside of the south wall he added his own war scenes. A colossus of him stands at the Second Pylon, with his daughter Bent'anta at his feet; it was later re-inscribed by Ramesses VI and Pinedjem I.

He kept his father's war with the Hittites going. Sety I had captured Kadesh and failed to hold it; Ramesses II failed to retake it in his fifth year, according to the Memphis project.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Hypostyle Hall page; Great Hypostyle Hall Project, "Interior Wall Scenes", "Great Columns" and "Entrance" pages, "War Scenes of Seti I"; Sullivan, "Development of the Temple of Karnak" (UEE 2010 / Digital Karnak); Shaw (ed.), The Oxford History of Ancient Egypt (2000).

## Ramesses I
Kind: person
About: ramesses-i
Ramesses I was the first king of the Nineteenth Dynasty and the father of Sety I. He reigned for less than two years, about 1295 to 1294 BC (Shaw's chronology, as used by Digital Karnak). He had been a soldier under Horemheb and may have been his chosen successor (general knowledge).

At Karnak he decorated the passage through the Second Pylon in raised relief (Ramesses II later recut it) and built a small "station of the king" against the pylon's south-east side. Only a calcite floor inscribed with the nine bows survives. The Memphis project says the Hypostyle Hall may have begun under him; Digital Karnak and Brand's study of the carved clerestory credit it to Sety I. Inside the hall, Sety I's carvers honoured his father on the west wall, and Ramesses II in turn honoured Sety on the south wall. The Memphis project reads these scenes as stressing royal parentage in a young dynasty. Sety I's reign began in 1294 BC, so his father's brief reign is easy to miss: only the passage and the floor are certain.
Sources: Great Hypostyle Hall Project, "Entrance" and "About the Architecture" pages; Digital Karnak, Hypostyle Hall page; Sullivan, "The Development of the Temple of Karnak" (UEE 2010 / Digital Karnak); general knowledge (his earlier career).

## Horemheb
Kind: person
About: horemheb
Horemheb was the last king of the Eighteenth Dynasty, a former general who ruled from 1323 to 1295 BC (Shaw's chronology). At Karnak he built the Second, Ninth and Tenth Pylons (the Tenth had been started by Amenhotep III and left unfinished). He had Akhenaten's temples in eastern Karnak pulled down block by block and reused the small talatat blocks as fill in his pylons. Sullivan says this was a deliberate policy against Akhenaten, not ordinary reuse. He also took over the sphinxes of Tutankhamun along the way to the Mut temple. His three pylons still stand.

The Second Pylon was the first major move west since Thutmose II. If a painting in the Theban tomb of Neferhotep (TT 49) has been read correctly, a large T-shaped basin linked to the Nile by a canal lay near the pylon's site and would have been filled in first. The Second Pylon became the temple's western entrance. Later kings, Ramesses I and Ramesses II, decorated it, and Ramesses II recut Horemheb's reliefs there. Some scholars suggest Horemheb had a peristyle of papyrus columns built where the Hypostyle Hall now stands, before Sety I's colonnade; this is a suggestion, and Digital Karnak's model does not show it.
Sources: Sullivan, "The Development of the Temple of Karnak" (UEE 2010 / Digital Karnak), the Horemheb entry and the sections on the Late Eighteenth Dynasty; Great Hypostyle Hall Project, "Entrance" page; Digital Karnak, Hypostyle Hall page.

## Georges Legrain
Kind: person
About: georges-legrain
Georges Legrain was a French Egyptologist, born in Paris on 4 October 1865 and died in Luxor on 22 August 1917. From 1895 he was in charge of the antiquities and the conservation works at Karnak for the Egyptian Antiquities Service.

After the collapse of eleven Hypostyle columns on 3 October 1899 he led the rebuilding. He arrived at the site on 11 December 1899, took down the damaged columns, cleared the ground, rebuilt the northern half on new foundations and underpinned the southern wing with brick. He wrote about the work in the Annales du Service des Antiquités (volume 1, 1900) and recorded it complete in May 1902. He later found the Karnak Cachette, a pit of about 800 stone statues and 17,000 bronzes (1903 to 1907), and published Les Temples de Karnak (1929). Some of his rebuilding was imperfect: about half the displaced abaci were never put back and one column faces the wrong way.
Sources: Wikipedia, Georges Legrain (pointer; birth and death dates, Cachette, Annales citation); Great Hypostyle Hall Project, "Early Recording and Restoration" page and 2011 field report; Egypt Exploration Fund, Archaeological Report 1899 to 1900 (archive.org dli.ministry.27742) and 1900 to 1901 (dli.ministry.27743).

## Pinedjem I
Kind: person
About: pinedjem-i
Pinedjem I was High Priest of Amun at Thebes in the Twenty-first Dynasty and ruled Upper Egypt almost as a king. The dates vary. Wikipedia (a pointer) gives him as High Priest from 1070 to 1055 BC and as king from 1054 to 1032 BC; Sullivan lists "High Priest of Amun Pinedjem" at 1054 to 1039 BC.

At Karnak he moved rows of ram-headed sphinxes (probably from a temple of Amenhotep III) to line the way before the Second Pylon, then the temple's entrance, and also the way to the Khonsu temple. Digital Karnak says the avenue before the Second Pylon probably reached as far as the later First Pylon and a quay. The sphinxes had probably been reused before: Sullivan thinks they were carved in the mid-Eighteenth Dynasty, then taken by Ramesses II, then by Pinedjem. He put his name on the colossus of Ramesses II at the Second Pylon, as Ramesses VI had before him.
Sources: Sullivan, "The Development of the Temple of Karnak" (UEE 2010 / Digital Karnak), the Pinedjem entries; Digital Karnak, "Colossal Statue of Ramesses II and Daughter" (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/?p=6438); Wikipedia, Pinedjem I (pointer only, for dates and titles).

## The obelisks of Thutmose I and Hatshepsut
Kind: site
About: thutmose-i-obelisk, hatshepsut-obelisk, fourth-pylon, wadjet-hall, thutmose-iii-obelisk-walls
Two obelisks stand in line on Karnak's main axis beyond the Third Pylon. The older is Thutmose I's, raised about 1500 BC (he reigned about 1504 to 1492 BC, Shaw's chronology) in front of the Fourth Pylon, which was then the temple's main western gate. The Institute of Egyptian Art and Archaeology at Memphis gives it as one shaft of red granite from Aswan, about 21.7 metres (71 feet) high and about 143 tons; a Wikipedia list gives 21.2 metres. Only the central line of writing on each face is Thutmose I's, saying he made it as a monument for his father Amun-Ra. The flanking lines were added about 350 years later by Ramesses IV, and Ramesses VI carved over one of Ramesses IV's names. It was one of a pair, and Memphis calls it the last of four obelisks that once stood before the Fourth Pylon.

Further east, in the Wadjet Hall between the Fourth and Fifth Pylons, stands the northern obelisk of Hatshepsut. Digital Karnak gives its height as 30 metres; other sources give 29.5 metres (97 feet). The weight is quoted as about 320 to 375 tonnes (323, 343 and 374 appear in different sources), so say "roughly 340 tonnes, estimates vary". It is the tallest standing obelisk in Egypt, though the Lateran obelisk in Rome, which also came from Karnak, is taller. Its pair, the southern obelisk, fell; see the section on the fallen obelisk.

The hall itself was Thutmose I's: a courtyard with a roofed colonnade of fluted sandstone columns and seated statues of the king in niches in the Fourth Pylon's east face. Hatshepsut removed the stone columns, put in five gilded wooden columns (which gave the hall its name, Wadjet), left the centre open to the sky and raised her two rose granite obelisks. Their texts record her jubilee in her year 16, and small scenes show her, as a male king, making offerings.

Her years were counted from Thutmose III's accession in 1479 BC, so year 16 falls about 1464 BC. Popular sources say about 1457 BC, probably counting from her 1473 BC coronation, which is not how her years were reckoned.

Later Thutmose III built a limestone gateway inside the hall that enclosed the lower parts of the obelisks, and added lines of his own text beside hers on the visible upper parts. Digital Karnak points out that her cartouches were intact when the walls went up, so this was not the start of the campaign against her memory, and a newer study of the hall's chronology suggests she may have enclosed the obelisks herself. It is often said that the walls protected the shaft; the sources I could read do not say so.

What to look for: Thutmose I's obelisk with three lines on each face; Hatshepsut's, taller, beyond; and the walls and rubble round the foot of hers.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Obelisks of Festival Hall East Pair, Obelisks of Wadjet Hall, Wadjet Hall, 4th Pylon and Enclosure; Sullivan, "The Development of the Temple of Karnak" (Digital Karnak, 2010); Institute of Egyptian Art and Archaeology, University of Memphis, Karnak tour; The National, 10 April 2022; site board (source 9) numbers 12 and 14; Shaw (2000) for reign dates; general knowledge for the Lateran obelisk.

## Hatshepsut's inscription: seven months and the electrum
Kind: site
About: hatshepsut-obelisk, hatshepsut
Long texts are carved on Hatshepsut's obelisk, and they are why so much is said about how it was made. I could read only a web translation (by Amir Hussein) and summaries; the standard scholarly translation is Breasted's Ancient Records of Egypt, volume 2, which I could not open. Treat the details below as the reading of those translations.

In them Hatshepsut says she made two great obelisks of red granite "of the south" for her father Amun at Karnak, and set them up in front of his gate. The work is dated from year 15 to year 16 of her reign, and the text says it lasted seven months from the start of the arrangements in the mountain, that is the quarry. Lehner, on the NOVA obelisk pages, summarises it as seven months to build her obelisk. How the two dated years fit with seven months is read in different ways, so give "seven months" as what she claims, not as a measured fact.

The tops were covered in electrum, the pale natural alloy of gold and silver. The translation says their upper parts were of the best electrum of the Two Lands. Digital Karnak reports electrum on the pyramidions of Thutmose I's obelisks too, where it would have flashed in the sun. I found no report of any electrum surviving at Karnak.

The text also speaks to doubters. It warns readers against saying they do not know why she did this, and against calling her words exaggerations. Thutmose I, her father, had raised his own obelisks here a generation earlier, and she presents the work as done for Amun and in her father's line.

Year 16 falls about 1464 BC counting from Thutmose III's accession in 1479 BC (Shaw's chronology). Popular sources give about 1457 BC, which is nearly the end of her reign.
Sources: Hatshepsut obelisk text, web translation by Amir Hussein (timetrips.co.uk), weak and unchecked against Breasted, Ancient Records of Egypt II; NOVA Online, "Raising the Obelisk", Q and A (2000); Digital Karnak, Obelisks of Festival Hall East Pair and Obelisks of Wadjet Hall; Shaw (2000).

## Quarrying, moving and raising an obelisk (and the theories)
Kind: reference
About: hatshepsut-obelisk, thutmose-i-obelisk
Karnak's obelisks are red (pink) granite from the quarries at Aswan, about 200 kilometres upstream by river.

Quarrying. The Unfinished Obelisk at Aswan shows the method. Engelbach, who studied it in 1922, measured it at 41.75 metres and 1,168 English tons (about 1,190 tonnes; a Wikipedia list gives nearly 1,090 tonnes, so say about 1,100 to 1,200 tonnes). Workers cut trenches round the shaft by pounding the granite with balls of harder stone, dolerite (sometimes called diorite), and had no iron tools for it. Cracks appeared and the work was abandoned. Engelbach could find no firm date for it, and a link to Hatshepsut is only a guess. In the NOVA test the team found it hard just to find a patch of uniform granite big enough for a 40-ton obelisk, and a hairline crack appeared as it was pulled from the quarry.

Moving. Obelisks went down the Nile on barges. Lehner says a record survives of a barge built to carry an obelisk for Thutmose I, and a relief at Hatshepsut's temple at Deir el-Bahari shows two obelisks end to end on a huge barge towed by many boats. The drawing is schematic, so crew numbers and the towing arrangement are open to argument.

Raising. No theory is settled, so call them theories. Engelbach proposed a ramp, with the obelisk then lowered into a funnel of sand, the sand being emptied out so that the base slid onto its pedestal and the shaft tipped up. Martin Isler argued this does not explain the groove cut in some bases, and proposed that the base pivoted into the groove. Isler's method worked for a model of two to three tons; for a 450-ton obelisk there is not enough room to lever at the tip. The NOVA test used a 40-ton obelisk, and levers the size of railway sleepers snapped. Hatshepsut's obelisk is roughly eight times heavier. A counterweight method was not tested.
Sources: Engelbach, The Aswan Obelisk (Cairo, 1922); NOVA Online, "Raising the Obelisk", Q and A (2000); Digital Karnak, Obelisks of Contra Temple (mentions the Deir el-Bahari reliefs); Wikipedia, Unfinished obelisk (pointer only); general knowledge for the Aswan distance and the absence of iron tools.

## The fallen obelisk of Hatshepsut and its top
Kind: site
About: fallen-obelisk, obelisk-top-reerected, hatshepsut-obelisk
Hatshepsut's southern obelisk in the Wadjet Hall fell in ancient times, probably in an earthquake, though that is a guess; the NOVA Q and A says earthquakes are thought to be the main reason several obelisks fell at Karnak. Its upper section lay on rubble above the hall until the early twentieth century, when the French team under Georges Legrain (who took charge of works at Karnak in 1895) cleared the hall and moved the intact top to the north-west corner of the Sacred Lake. It lay there for over a century.

On the night of 9 April 2022 Egypt's Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities re-erected it, with the Engineering Authority of the Armed Forces, after approval by the Permanent Committee of Egyptian Antiquities. The Secretary-General of the Supreme Council of Antiquities, Mostafa Waziri, said its position on the ground may have harmed it over time. The piece is about 11 metres tall and about 90 tonnes. The National and Daily News Egypt say it was re-erected near the Sacred Lake. Ancient Origins says the lower two thirds of the shaft were never found, so the top stands on a modern base. If the pair matched the standing obelisk, which is about 30 metres, roughly a third survives. Older guidebooks and photographs show it lying down, so they are out of date.

The pyramidion, the pointed tip, carries a scene of Hatshepsut kneeling before Amun. Fragments from the obelisk went to foreign museums, including the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, and the Nicholson Museum in Sydney.

Some writers say it was pulled down deliberately when Hatshepsut's memory was attacked. Digital Karnak does not say so, and no source I read proves it.

Where to find it: at the north-west corner of the Sacred Lake, beside the giant scarab.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Obelisks of Wadjet Hall; Sullivan (2010); The National, 10 April 2022; Ancient Origins, April 2022; Daily News Egypt, 11 April 2022; NOVA Online Q and A (2000); Wikimedia Commons caption for "Hatshepsut before Amun"; knowledge/karnak/dates.json for Legrain's dates.

## The Third Pylon of Amenhotep III
Kind: site
About: third-pylon, amenhotep-iii
The Third Pylon is the great gateway at the east end of the Hypostyle Hall, beside the obelisks. Digital Karnak gives its height as 28 metres, so Hatshepsut's obelisk still rises above it. Amenhotep III (reigned about 1390 to 1352 BC) built it, and the University of Memphis says he inscribed it in the last decade of his reign, preparing for his first Heb-Sed (jubilee festival). Its reliefs celebrate that festival and a festival journey to Luxor Temple. Only the lower parts remain.

To make room he cleared away the "festival court" of Thutmose II, with its pillars, bark shrines and other monuments. Digital Karnak says these were used in the pylon's foundation and fill. Among them were the White Chapel of Senusret I, the calcite chapel of Amenhotep I, loose blocks of Hatshepsut's Red Chapel and about a thousand blocks of Thutmose IV's peristyle. Hidden for over three thousand years, they kept traces of their paint. Excavators removed them in the twentieth century, and they now make up much of the Open Air Museum. The new pylon also moved the temple's main entrance eastward, reversing the earlier westward growth.

Amenhotep IV (Akhenaten) added a vestibule to the front, decorated in the early years of his reign, and its scenes were left unfinished. Sety I covered the pylon's west face with his Hypostyle Hall and also hid the Akhenaten scenes. That wall has since been rebuilt in the Open Air Museum. The pylon's wooden flagstaffs stood in notches in the masonry and were removed when the hall enclosed the face.

On the east face, the north tower shows Amun's gilded barge towed by the royal barge, with Amenhotep III shown twice; the south tower keeps lower inscriptions of Amenhotep III and Ramesses III. Akhenaten damaged images of Amun's barge, and Tutankhamun and Horemheb repaired and altered them.
Sources: Digital Karnak, 3rd Pylon, White Chapel, 4th Pylon and Enclosure; Sullivan, "The Development of the Temple of Karnak" (2010); University of Memphis, Great Hypostyle Hall Project, Third Pylon; site board (source 9) number 11.

## The giant scarab of Amenhotep III
Kind: site
About: scarab, amenhotep-iii, scarab-moved
The giant granite scarab stands on a granite plinth at the north-west corner of the Sacred Lake. Digital Karnak dates it to the reign of Amenhotep III (about 1390 to 1352 BC) and records no size. A faintly cut scene on the plinth shows the kneeling king offering to the god Khepri, seated on a low throne, with a winged sun disc above. Digital Karnak lists the plinth as red granite.

Khepri was the god of the rising sun, and the scarab, a dung beetle, was his sign. Egyptians likened the beetle's rolling of its dung ball to the sun being rolled across the sky each day. The statue is, in effect, a monument to the sunrise.

The scarab did not start here. Digital Karnak, citing Blyth (2006) and Porter and Moss, says it first stood at Amenhotep III's mortuary temple on the west bank of Thebes, and was probably moved to Karnak by Taharqa (reigned about 690 to 664 BC), some 700 years later. Because the Taharqa move is "probably", it is a scholar's inference, not a record.

Do not confuse it with the small "commemorative scarabs" of Amenhotep III, which are seals recording events such as a lake dug for his queen Tiye. They are a different group of objects.

Visitors now walk round the scarab for luck. The usual story is seven times, and some say it brings luck, love or a wish granted. This is a modern tradition. I found no ancient basis for it, and accounts differ on the number of circuits. Photographs show a rope or rail round it, so people walk round the outside.

Beside it stands the top of Hatshepsut's fallen obelisk, re-erected in 2022 (see the fallen obelisk section), so the two are seen together.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Scarab of Amenhotep III (Zarnoch and Sullivan; cites Blyth 2006, p. 111, and Porter and Moss); Egypt Museum, "Colossal Khepri" (custom of walking round it); UCL Digital Egypt, commemorative scarabs (pointer); Wikimedia Commons photographs of 2009 and 2025; general knowledge for Khepri and the dung beetle.

## The granite sanctuary of Philip Arrhidaeus
Kind: site
About: sanctuary, philip-arrhidaeus, thutmose-iii-sanctuary
This is the stone shrine at the heart of Karnak where Amun's portable boat (the bark) rested between festivals. It is the granite bark shrine of Philip Arrhidaeus, Alexander the Great's half-brother, who was king of Egypt in name from 323 BC and died in 317 BC (Shaw). Digital Karnak gives the shrine as 323 to 316 BC. It has two rooms and is cut from rose granite, 6.34 metres wide, 17.83 metres deep and 6.85 metres high. The granite came from Aswan, like the obelisks (general knowledge).

Philip's inscription says he made a faithful copy of the shrine of Thutmose III that stood on the spot. That in turn replaced Hatshepsut's Red Chapel, about year 46 of Thutmose III, about 1434 BC; the Red Chapel's blocks are now rebuilt in the Open Air Museum. Very little of Thutmose III's shrine is known. A few carved fragments from its north wall were found near Philip's. Digital Karnak says Amun's images on it were cut out in the Amarna period and repaired under Sety I, and that it may have been damaged again in the Assyrian or Persian invasions. Sullivan adds that it seems to have served as the main bark shrine for hundreds of years.

Inside, the ceiling is painted with red, white and blue stars. On the south outside wall the sacred bark is shown on its processional routes, leaving and returning to the temple.

One scholar has asked whether Nectanebo II (about 360 to 343 BC) renewed the shrine and left it uninscribed until Philip's reign. Sullivan reports this as a possibility; the usual account is that Philip's shrine is his.

The shrine stands inside the Palace of Ma'at, a suite of rooms that Hatshepsut built before the Middle Kingdom temple. Photographs taken on 10 March 2024 show the chambers open to visitors and people walking round the outer walls.

In front, in the vestibule beside the Sixth Pylon, stand two heraldic pillars of Thutmose III. The board (source 9) numbers the sanctuary 17.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Central Bark Shrine; 6th Pylon and Court; Sullivan, "The Development of the Temple of Karnak" (2010); Shaw (2000); Wikimedia Commons photographs by Marsupium, 10 March 2024; site board (source 9).

## The heraldic pillars of Thutmose III
Kind: site
About: heraldic-pillars, thutmose-iii
In the vestibule in front of the granite sanctuary, just east of the Sixth Pylon, stand two tall granite pillars set up by Thutmose III, who reigned from 1479 to 1425 BC (Shaw). Each carries a plant emblem. The papyrus pillar is on the north (left, facing east) and stands for Lower Egypt, the Delta. The lotus pillar is on the south (right) and stands for Upper Egypt, the Nile valley. Together they say the king ruled both lands.

They stand alone and hold up nothing. They are called heraldic because the plants act as emblems of the two halves of Egypt, rather like badges. The pillars carry figures and inscriptions of Thutmose III as well.

The University of Memphis calls them elegant reminders of the importance of a unified Upper and Lower Egypt. Lotus and papyrus tied together is a common motif of the "union of the Two Lands" (general knowledge). The pillars share the vestibule with the doorway of the sanctuary, so they are seen on the way to it.

Digital Karnak's page on the Sixth Pylon and court gives the pylon itself as small, with a granite gate covered in electrum, but does not discuss the pillars.
Sources: University of Memphis, Institute of Egyptian Art and Archaeology, Karnak tour; Wikimedia Commons, "Karnak 34" (Jon Bodsworth) and the Gaspa photograph of the lotus pillar; Digital Karnak, 6th Pylon and Court; general knowledge for the "union of the Two Lands" motif.

## Thutmose I
Kind: person
About: thutmose-i
Thutmose I, an early Eighteenth Dynasty king, reigned from about 1504 to 1492 BC (Shaw). He was Hatshepsut's father. At Karnak he built the Fourth and Fifth Pylons and the hall between them, and raised a pair of red granite obelisks before the Fourth Pylon, then the temple's western entrance. Digital Karnak lists these as his major works. The Fourth Pylon was 23.5 metres tall, 62.6 metres wide and 10.5 metres deep, a sandstone core faced with limestone. In his hall, fluted sandstone columns carried a roof round the edge, and niches in the pylon held seated statues of him as Osiris, with larger painted Osiride statues about 3.15 metres tall between them.

One of his obelisks, about 21 metres high, survives. Thutmose II's pylon later enclosed them, and Amenhotep III pulled that pylon down to build the Third Pylon. Thutmose III later hid the niches behind a stone lining.

A record of a barge being built to carry an obelisk for Thutmose I is cited by Mark Lehner on NOVA. I have not seen the original, and the official who wrote it is probably Ineni, his architect, so treat that attribution as unconfirmed.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Obelisks of Festival Hall East Pair, 4th Pylon and Enclosure, Wadjet Hall; Sullivan (2010); NOVA Online Q and A (2000); Shaw (2000).

## Hatshepsut
Kind: person
About: hatshepsut
Hatshepsut ruled Egypt as king from about 1473 to 1458 BC (Shaw). She began as regent for her young stepson and nephew, Thutmose III, who became king in 1479 BC, and her years were counted from his accession. Her images as king show her in male form. She was a daughter of Thutmose I.

At Karnak she remodelled Thutmose I's hall as the Wadjet Hall and raised two obelisks in it; raised a second pair in east Karnak, just east of the Akhmenu, which were larger and survive only in fragments (Deir el-Bahari reliefs claim 108 cubits, about 62 metres, which Egyptologists think inflated; Senenmut oversaw their raising); built the Red Chapel; added the Palace of Ma'at; and built the Eighth Pylon on the southern road. She removed Amenhotep I's central bark shrine and chapels. At Deir el-Bahari, across the Nile, she built her great temple, whose reliefs show obelisks being moved.

After her death Thutmose III removed her names and images from some of her monuments, including the east obelisks. The timing is debated. Her cartouches on the obelisks in the Wadjet Hall were intact when he built his walls round them.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Obelisks of Wadjet Hall, Obelisks of Contra Temple, Wadjet Hall; Sullivan (2010); Shaw (2000); general knowledge for the family relationships (verify).

## Thutmose III
Kind: person
About: thutmose-iii, akhmenu, botanical-garden, lateran-obelisk
Thutmose III reigned from 1479 to 1425 BC (Shaw). He came to the throne as a child, and Hatshepsut ruled beside him until about his twenty-second year. He then fought a series of campaigns into Syria and Palestine, the first ending at Megiddo in his Year 23 (about 1457 BC). His "Annals" at Karnak record the campaigns of his Years 22 to 42.

At Karnak he built the Akhmenu, the Seventh Pylon, the Sacred Lake, the Temple of Ptah and a new enclosure wall, and he remodelled the core of the temple. He put a contra-temple on the east side of the Akhmenu, and raised red granite obelisks in front of the Seventh Pylon. He also began the great obelisk now in Rome, which his grandson Thutmose IV raised. Hatshepsut's name and images were attacked late in his reign, after his forty-second year, and Sullivan argues that this was not the reason for his earlier changes.
Sources: Sullivan, UEE (2010); Shaw (2000); Digital Karnak, Akhmenu; general knowledge for Megiddo.

## Amenhotep III
Kind: person
About: amenhotep-iii
Amenhotep III, an Eighteenth Dynasty king, reigned from about 1390 to 1352 BC (Shaw) and was Akhenaten's father. At Karnak he built the Third Pylon, using stones from older monuments as fill, and tore down the pylon of Thutmose II to do it. He began the Tenth Pylon on the southern road but only a few courses were laid before he died, and the work stopped until Horemheb resumed it. Two colossal statues of him flank its door.

He also built Luxor Temple and a vast mortuary temple on the west bank, where the giant scarab first stood (general knowledge for Luxor Temple). The Third Pylon's reliefs celebrate his Heb-Sed jubilee, and a journey of Amun's barge to Luxor Temple. His son Akhenaten added a vestibule to the front of the Third Pylon, which was left unfinished, before turning against Amun. The University of Memphis dates the decoration of the pylon to the last decade of Amenhotep III's reign, when he was preparing for his first jubilee.
Sources: Digital Karnak, 3rd Pylon, Scarab of Amenhotep III; Sullivan (2010); University of Memphis, Great Hypostyle Hall Project, Third Pylon; Shaw (2000).

## Philip Arrhidaeus
Kind: person
About: philip-arrhidaeus
Philip Arrhidaeus was a half-brother of Alexander the Great and king of Egypt from Alexander's death in 323 BC until about 317 BC (Shaw). Digital Karnak gives his reign as 323 to 316 BC. Alexander had taken Egypt from the Persians in 332 BC (general knowledge). Philip was king in name only, under regents, and was put to death in the struggles that followed Alexander's death (general knowledge, verify).

At Karnak his name is on the granite sanctuary at the heart of the temple, which his inscription says is a faithful copy of Thutmose III's shrine. Digital Karnak and Sullivan class this as part of the Greco-Roman phase of Karnak about 1,100 years after Thutmose III's time. One scholar has suggested that Nectanebo II made the shrine and Philip only added the carvings. The shrine's south outside wall shows the sacred bark on its processional routes, and its ceiling is painted with stars (Digital Karnak).
Sources: Digital Karnak, Central Bark Shrine; Sullivan (2010); Shaw (2000); general knowledge for the regency, the conquest and his death (verify).

## The Middle Kingdom court (Senusret I's temple)
Kind: site
About: senusret-temple, senusret-i
The Middle Kingdom court is the open, empty-looking space between the granite sanctuary and the Akhmenu. It is the oldest part of Karnak that visitors can stand on. The temple of Amun-Ra that stood here was built by Senusret I, who reigned alone from about 1956 to 1911 BC in Shaw's chronology, after about ten years as co-ruler with his father (1971 to 1926 BC in some other chronologies).

What it looked like depends on the reconstruction. Luc Gabolde of the Franco-Egyptian centre (CFEETK) rebuilt it on paper from blocks found at Karnak since the early 1900s and from his excavations in this court. His version is a limestone temple about 38 metres square and about 6 metres high, with four doorways whose thresholds were red granite. In front was a portico 3 metres deep with twelve square pillars, each carrying a statue of the king as Osiris. Some of those pillars are now in the Cairo Museum. The sanctuary was off the central line, so a priest had to turn ninety degrees left to reach it. A calcite altar held the shrine of the Amun-Ra statue. A mud-brick wall enclosed the whole.

It was not the first building here. Below the level of the thresholds Gabolde found small sandstone platforms, none larger than 10 metres by 10 metres, which he dated to the early Eleventh Dynasty. He reads them as the first temple and portico of Intef II (about 2100 to 2050 BC), taken down soon after his reign and rebuilt by later Eleventh Dynasty kings and by Amenemhat I. So Senusret I enlarged an existing cult rather than founding it. His was the first large stone temple, which is why the app's timeline says "first large stone temple".

Later kings built over it. Amenhotep I (1525 to 1504 BC) added lines of stone chapels and store rooms along the court. Hatshepsut removed the Osiris portico and added her "Palace of Maat" in front of the temple, with the Red Chapel inside it. Thutmose III's Akhmenu, just east of here, echoes the layout of Senusret I's temple, with a sanctuary reached by a turn.

Sources disagree on three points. Size: Gabolde's temple faces west; François Larché argues for a smaller temple with a double portico facing east, towards a Nile channel in east Karnak that has since gone. Demolition: Sullivan's traditional account has the temple razed, perhaps about 323 BC, when the granite shrine of Philip Arrhidaeus was installed and the ground raised; Larché says Amenhotep I took it down because the stone was decaying; Digital Karnak's court page says part may have stood until the fourth century AD, when local people carried off the blocks. Sullivan's account is the best supported but is not certain. Orientation: some scholars say the axis was set on the winter solstice sunrise from the start, and others doubt that for so early a date (see the section on the main axis).

What to look for: a low mud-brick wall, large flat stone slabs inside it, an information board, and Hatshepsut's standing obelisk to the west. There is no shade.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Middle Kingdom Court; Sullivan, "Karnak: Development of the Temple of Amun-Ra", UEE (2010); Sullivan, The Development of the Temple of Karnak (Digital Karnak, 2010); Shaw (ed.), Oxford History of Ancient Egypt (2000).

## The Akhmenu (Festival Hall of Thutmose III)
Kind: site
About: akhmenu, thutmose-iii
The Akhmenu, "the most glorious of monuments", is the large temple Thutmose III built behind what was then Karnak's east wall. Digital Karnak says building began in his twenty-third year and Sullivan says it was built after it. Thutmose III reigned from 1479 to 1425 BC (Shaw), so that is about 1457 to 1456 BC. Building took about seven years, the botanical room was decorated two years after building began, and the great hall probably marks his jubilee (heb-sed), likely celebrated in his thirtieth year, about 1450 BC. Its exact religious purpose is still unclear; Sullivan suggests ceremonies for the renewal of the king.

It is 40 metres deep and 77.5 metres wide. The main door is in the south-west corner. Sources disagree about a second door found by CFEETK: Digital Karnak puts it in the middle of the west wall and Sullivan in the east wall. Thutmose III also built a new sandstone wall around the Akhmenu and a small contra-temple on its east side.

The great hall is the tent. Two rows of unusual painted columns represent the poles of a portable tent, and the ceiling was painted blue with yellow stars. North of it are three chapels, the largest holding a quartzite group of Thutmose III with Amun and Mut. The south-east holds a suite of rooms for Sokar, the falcon god of the dead at Memphis (general knowledge), around a hall of eight fluted columns. There is a main sanctuary with eight niches and a larger niche at the back for the god's shrine. A stair in the north-east corner of the hall climbs to a solar shrine on the roof.

Beside the entrance corridor is the small Hall of Ancestors, which showed Thutmose III honouring sixty-one earlier kings, the earliest names being from the Fourth Dynasty. The reliefs were taken to the Louvre in 1843.

The building changed later. The upper solar shrine was altered in the Ramesside period. Decoration in the rooms for the king and for Amun was altered under Alexander the Great, and inscriptions credit him with restoring one of the twin sanctuaries. The central hall later became a church (see the section on the church).

Access: visitors have reported that few rooms are open. The Southern Chapels, to the right of the main entrance (seven linked shrines and two columned rooms), were restored by the CFEETK and reported complete in June 2025, with new signs and ramps. The Past says the public could see them for the first time. Whether the inner rooms are open in March 2027 is not known.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Akhmenu; Sullivan, UEE (2010); Sullivan, The Development of the Temple of Karnak (Digital Karnak, 2010); Ancient Pages, 17 June 2025 (citing Ahram Online); The Past, 21 August 2025; Luxor Living Lab, Layers of Heritage; Shaw (2000); general knowledge for Sokar.

## The botanical room of the Akhmenu ("botanical garden")
Kind: site
About: botanical-garden, akhmenu
The botanical room is in the north-east part of the Akhmenu. Its walls are carved with plants and animals that Thutmose III and his men saw on their military campaigns abroad, according to Digital Karnak and Sullivan. It had four papyrus columns, and its decoration was begun about two years after building started, so about 1454 BC.

The carving is in two bands: plants above and animals below. Wikipedia counts about 275 different kinds of plant. That figure is not from a source I could open, so treat it as approximate. Early French scholars called it the "botanical garden" because of the plants. Nathalie Beaux's 1990 book on the room is titled "Le cabinet de curiosités de Thoutmosis III", the cabinet of curiosities, which is closer to how it is now understood: a showcase of exotic things from the lands of his campaigns, not a garden.

What it was for is argued. Popular accounts say Thutmose III brought the plants home and introduced species such as the iris, but that is not established. Wikipedia lists a 2007 study by Dimitri Laboury that links the room's function to ritual. Treat both as ideas, not facts.

The campaigns themselves are recorded elsewhere in Karnak. Thutmose III's "Annals", carved in the sanctuary area, describe campaigns from his twenty-second to his forty-second year, which is about 1458 to 1438 BC (Sullivan). The room can be read alongside that written record.

What to look for: low, shallow carvings of leaves, flowers, fruit, birds and animals. Photographs suggest the carved wall can be seen from a roped walkway on the outside of the room, which may help if the inner door is closed. The carving is faint in strong light, so look along the wall at an angle.

For the family: this is the best place in the east end to find animals and flowers for a two-year-old.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Akhmenu; Sullivan, UEE (2010); Wikipedia, "Botanical garden of Thutmosis III" (pointer only, citing Beaux 1990 and Laboury 2007); Shaw (2000).

## The Akhmenu as a church: painted saints on the columns
Kind: site
About: christian-karnak, akhmenu
In the fourth century Karnak's role as a working temple ended. The usual account is that pagan worship was banned across the Roman empire, with Constantius II ordering temples closed in 356 and Theodosius I issuing bans in 391 and 392 (general knowledge, to be verified). Sullivan reports that by the fourth century Karnak's monuments were being torn down rather than built: two great obelisks were taken away, and the other fallen obelisks were broken up for door thresholds and millstones. Byzantine mud-brick walls have been found west of the quay.

Christians then founded churches among the ruins. The best known is in the central hall of Thutmose III's Akhmenu, where painted saints and Coptic inscriptions survive. Wikipedia gives this and cites Blyth (2006, page 234), which I could not open. A local heritage site, Luxor Living Lab, says the church dates from the fourth century and that saints are painted on six columns. I found no scholarly source that confirms the century, so it is uncertain. The label on the app's timeline, AD 400, is a round figure.

What you can see: faint painted figures with halos on some of the columns, put over the older painted decoration. Photographs show one high on the column shaft. Visitor accounts mention them (one blogger noticed "several paintings of saints on the columns"). They are faint and easy to miss, so look slowly along the column shafts, not just up at the ceiling.

Wikipedia says Coptic Christians used the building for church services, as the Christian art and inscriptions show, until the site was abandoned. The same hall was a tent of stone for a king's festival, and later the nave of a church.

Elsewhere the Christian and Roman layers are easier to date. Sullivan records a small Roman chapel for the imperial cult just outside the First Pylon, and Roman repairs to the quay and sphinx avenue, followed by Byzantine levels.
Sources: Sullivan, UEE (2010); Wikipedia, History of the Karnak Temple complex (pointer only, citing Blyth 2006, page 234); Luxor Living Lab, Layers of Heritage (heritage site, not scholarly); a travel blog, November 2018; general knowledge for the closure edicts.

## The Temple of Ptah and the Sekhmet statue
Kind: site
About: ptah-temple, ptah-gates
The Temple of Ptah is a small sandstone temple in the northern part of the Amun precinct. Thutmose III built it, possibly on the site of an earlier mud-brick temple (Sullivan). Reports of the CFEETK's work say the same: Thutmose III's foundation stele (Cairo CG 34013) records that he found an older temple of brick, with wooden columns and doors, falling into ruin, and rebuilt it. One account places the building shortly before his return from the Megiddo campaign in Year 23, about 1457 BC. I could read this only through summaries of the CFEETK and Egyptian Archaeology reports, so treat the exact year as approximate.

It was a temple for Ptah, the god of craftsmen and builders, with Sekhmet his lioness-headed wife and Nefertem their son (general knowledge, and the CFEETK). A hall with two columns fronted a triple sanctuary. Excavations since 2008 by a Franco-Egyptian team have confirmed an earlier mud-brick sanctuary, which may go back to the Seventeenth Dynasty. The full survey is Biston-Moulin and Thiers, Le temple de Ptah à Karnak (2016).

The gateways to the west were added over about 700 years. Shabaqo, a Kushite king, added two about 700 BC. The Ptolemaic kings Ptolemy VI and Ptolemy XII extended the entrance with more. Reliefs of the Roman emperor Tiberius (AD 14 to 37) were added to one gate. Nectanebo I's great wall (about 380 to 362 BC) had a gate in the north wall beside this temple.

The Sekhmet statue is in the southern side chapel, the right-hand room as you go in. It is about 2 metres tall and is lit only by a hole in the roof (Wikimedia Commons description). According to the CFEETK, it was re-erected and is not in its original place, and the opening above gives it sun, and on some nights moonlight. An Egyptian Archaeology report says the roof slabs over the chapels were removed by Georges Legrain to let light in, so the effect is modern (reported, not checked). The northern chapel keeps its original roof.

Sekhmet is the fierce goddess: a lioness of war and plague who could also heal (general knowledge, to be verified). Ptah is the Memphite creator who, in later texts, made the world by thought and word (general knowledge).

Access: in a 2020 blog (updated in 2024), a room in the temple was locked until a guard opened it, and the Sekhmet statue was seen in the second sanctuary. Carry small change for a tip. If it is shut, the row of gateways can be seen from outside.
Sources: Sullivan, UEE (2010); Digital Karnak, Ptah Temple (location and bibliography only); CFEETK and Egyptian Archaeology 38 (2011), Thiers and Zignani (read only through summaries); Wikimedia Commons file description of the Sekhmet statue; a travel blog, updated September 2024; general knowledge for the gods.

## The Temple of the Hearing Ear and the eastern end
Kind: site
About: hearing-ear-temple, nectanebo-east-gate
At the east end of Karnak is a small temple that Ramesses II built to "Amun-Ra, Ramesses II, who hears prayers", usually called the Temple of the Hearing Ear. Digital Karnak says it lay at the far east side of the Amun precinct, built around a single obelisk (see the section on the Lateran obelisk). It is about 32 metres wide and 25.5 metres long, in sandstone with a mud-brick gateway, and had a pillared hall with a central false door. Two side doors led out to the obelisk. Column drums of Thutmose III were reused in it, so Ramesses II probably rebuilt an earlier shrine of Thutmose III.

Its purpose was public. The temple stood outside the closed inner temple, behind the Akhmenu and Thutmose III's contra-temple, so people who were not allowed inside could come to petition the god. Sullivan says it seems to have worked like a contra-temple, and that the public came for oracular judgements; Digital Karnak does not use the word "oracle". Ramesses II reigned from 1279 to 1213 BC; the tour uses about 1250 BC. One encyclopedia entry on the temple (Gallet, UEE; seen only as an outline) gives "years 40 to 46" of his reign, with a question mark, so the date is uncertain.

Taharqa, the Kushite king, added a porch of twenty papyrus columns in four rows of five, 9.5 metres high and about 1 metre across, joined by low screen walls (690 to 664 BC). It has fallen. Ptolemy VIII, in the second century BC, enclosed the rear colonnade and the base of the obelisk and cut a new doorway through the false door.

Thutmose III's contra-temple just west of it was a calcite shrine with a statue group of the king and Amun, between two obelisks of Hatshepsut. Her obelisks are destroyed; the CFEETK has been documenting their pieces.

The eastern gate beyond is Nectanebo I's. Digital Karnak says it was begun by Nectanebo I and was about 20 metres high. His wall was 12 metres thick and 21 metres high. He planned twelve gates and finished four. Ramesses II had earlier marked the east entrance with two red granite obelisks and two granite sphinxes.

Beyond the gate lay Akhenaten's temples to the Aten, built about 1350 BC and later pulled apart by Horemheb and reused as building fill (Sullivan).

Access: the end is quiet. One blog (2020, updated 2024) found the eastern gate locked. If it is closed, it can still be seen from the temple side.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Ramesses II Eastern Temple; Digital Karnak, Enclosures and Gates; Sullivan, UEE (2010); Sullivan, The Development of the Temple of Karnak (Digital Karnak, 2010); a travel blog, updated September 2024; Shaw (2000).

## The Lateran obelisk (the "unique" obelisk of east Karnak)
Kind: site
About: lateran-obelisk, lateran-obelisk-to-rome, lateran-obelisk-reerected, constantius-ii, domenico-fontana, thutmose-iv
The obelisk that stood at the east end of Karnak now stands in front of St John Lateran in Rome. NOVA calls it the largest standing obelisk in the world. Turismo Roma gives 32.18 metres for the shaft and 45.7 metres with the base and cross. It is red granite from the Aswan area.

It was begun for Thutmose III, who reigned from 1479 to 1425 BC, and was not finished in his lifetime. His grandson Thutmose IV added his own lines of text around his grandfather's and raised it about 1400 BC. A text of his says it had lain on its side for thirty-five years (NOVA and Wikipedia; I did not see the inscription). Unlike the other obelisks at Karnak, which stood in pairs, this one stood alone, in the part of the temple given to sun worship (Sullivan). Ramesses II later built the Temple of the Hearing Ear at its foot.

Estimates of its weight vary. NOVA gives 455 tons, which is about 413 tonnes if these are short tons. Wikipedia gives about 300 tonnes for it as it stands now. Wikipedia also says it was re-erected about 4 metres shorter than at Karnak.

In the fourth century it was taken down and shipped to Alexandria (Sullivan, citing Habachi 1984). Sources disagree on who ordered it. NOVA says Constantine I wanted it for Constantinople but died in 337 before it left Egypt; his son Constantius II sent it to Rome instead. Wikipedia credits Constantius II in one place and Constantine in another. Constantius II had it set up in the Circus Maximus in 357 (the date is from the ancient historian Ammianus Marcellinus; general knowledge, to be verified). It reached Ostia by ship and was towed up the Tiber (Turismo Roma).

It fell some time after the fifth century and was buried in mud. Pope Sixtus V ordered a search. In 1587 three pieces were found about 7 metres down in the old Circus Maximus. The architect Domenico Fontana joined them and raised the obelisk on 3 August 1588, with a cross on top. It was consecrated on 9 August. The base inscription gets some history wrong, for instance Constantine's baptism (Wikipedia).

Shaltout and Belmonte suggest this obelisk stood exactly on Karnak's main axis, so someone at the quay might have seen its tip at the winter solstice sunrise. They call this speculation.

The obelisk stood at the rear of the Hearing Ear temple. Ptolemy VIII enclosed its base within the temple, but whether any of that can be seen now is not known.
Sources: Sullivan, UEE (2010); Shaltout and Belmonte, Journal for the History of Astronomy 36 (2005); NOVA Online, "A World of Obelisks: Rome"; Turismo Roma, The Lateran Obelisk; Wikipedia, Lateran Obelisk (pointer only); Digital Karnak, Ramesses II Eastern Temple; general knowledge for Ammianus.

## The main axis and the winter solstice sunrise
Kind: reference
About: karnak-axis, senusret-temple, akhmenu, lateran-obelisk
Karnak's main axis runs from the river in the west through the great temple to the Akhmenu and the east gate. Sparavigna measured it from satellite maps at 27.2 degrees from due east-west. At Luxor, the winter solstice sunrise is about 26 to 27 degrees south of east (my own calculation from the sunrise formula in her paper: about 26.2 degrees today and about 26.7 degrees around 1950 BC; not a published figure). So the axis points to the winter solstice sunrise, to within about a degree. The sun's disc is half a degree wide, so that is a close match but not exact. Sparavigna's paper is a preprint, not peer reviewed.

Who says what. Norman Lockyer (1894) thought the axis aimed at the summer solstice sunset. Shaltout and Belmonte (2005) report that when he asked for a check, the hills of western Thebes blocked the sunset and it never reached the inner rooms. Hawkins (1975) and others then favoured the winter solstice sunrise, and drew attention to a "high room of the sun" at the Akhmenu, perhaps built by Thutmose III, though the inscriptions honouring the sunrise are Ramesses III's. Shaltout and Belmonte think its east opening was a window. C. Traunecker (1991) argues it was a niche.

Hatshepsut's temple of Amun-Ra who hears prayers sat on the same axis but opened east, which on Shaltout and Belmonte's view made it the first building at Karnak to face the sunrise. Between her two obelisks, they suggest, the solstice sun would light the statues. Thutmose III then put a huge obelisk in front of it. The UNESCO and International Astronomical Union portal says the temple was aligned on the winter solstice sunrise since Senusret I "if not before". Belmonte's own earlier work put Gabolde's argument for a Middle Kingdom foundation date "in quarantine". So the early date is disputed.

The site may have been chosen for the river. Shaltout and Belmonte note that Thebes is the only place above the First Cataract where the average direction of the Nile is perpendicular to the solstice line. Sullivan reports that the Nile moved west over the centuries, so the local channel changed.

Each year about 21 December the rising sun is reported to shine along the axis and crowds gather (Explore Luxor and Ahram Online reports). The family visit is in early March, when the sun rises about 7 to 8 degrees south of east (my calculation), so the alignment cannot be seen directly.
Sources: Sparavigna, "The sunrise amplitude equation applied to an Egyptian temple" (arXiv 1207.6942, 2012); Shaltout and Belmonte, "On the orientation of ancient Egyptian temples: (1) Upper Egypt and Lower Nubia", Journal for the History of Astronomy 36 (2005); UNESCO and IAU Astronomy and World Heritage portal, The Temple of Amun at Karnak; Sullivan, UEE (2010); Digital Karnak, Akhmenu; Explore Luxor, Winter Solstice Sunrise at the Karnak Temple.

## Senusret I
Kind: person
About: senusret-i, senusret-temple
Senusret I (also spelled Senwosret, or Sesostris in Greek) was the second king of the Twelfth Dynasty and reigned alone from about 1956 to 1911 BC in Shaw's chronology, after about ten years as co-ruler with his father Amenemhat I from about 1965 BC (the co-regency is accepted by most, though not all, Egyptologists; other chronologies give 1971 to 1926 BC). At Karnak he turned a small earlier temple of Amun-Ra into a large limestone building about 38 metres square, with a portico of Osiris pillars, four granite-sill doorways and a calcite altar for the god's shrine. He also built the White Chapel, now rebuilt in the Open Air Museum, and a number of small shrines along processional routes.

Almost nothing of his temple stands where it was. Hatshepsut removed its portico, and the rest was dismantled, though scholars disagree about when. Its blocks were reused in later buildings, which is how Gabolde and others have been able to rebuild it on paper. Larché also identified parts of a sandstone colonnade of Senusret I among the blocks found in the Fifth Pylon, though where it stood is unknown.

Some scholars think the temple's axis was set to the winter solstice sunrise. That is disputed.
Sources: Sullivan, UEE (2010); Digital Karnak, Middle Kingdom Court; Shaw (2000).

## Ramesses II
Kind: person
About: ramesses-ii, hearing-ear-temple
Ramesses II reigned from 1279 to 1213 BC (Shaw), which is sixty-six years. In the east end of Karnak he added an entrance marked by two red granite obelisks and a pair of red granite sphinxes (Sullivan; Digital Karnak). Nectanebo I's great wall later enclosed those obelisks.

He also built the small temple of "Amun-Ra, Ramesses II, who hears prayers" at the foot of the lone obelisk of Thutmose IV. It had a mud-brick gateway and a pillared hall with a false door, and it reused column drums of Thutmose III, so it probably replaced an earlier shrine. The tour dates it about 1250 BC; one encyclopedia entry suggests Years 40 to 46 of his reign, with a question mark. The temple was open to ordinary people, who could petition the god there. Later kings changed it: Taharqa added a porch, and Ptolemy VIII enclosed the rear.

His other Karnak work, on the Hypostyle Hall and elsewhere, is covered in other sections.
Sources: Sullivan, UEE (2010); Digital Karnak, Ramesses II Eastern Temple; Sullivan, The Development of the Temple of Karnak (Digital Karnak, 2010); Shaw (2000).

## Constantius II and Domenico Fontana (the obelisk's movers)
Kind: person
About: constantius-ii, domenico-fontana, lateran-obelisk-to-rome, lateran-obelisk-reerected
Constantius II (born 317, emperor from 337, died 361) was the son of Constantine I. Some sources say Constantine had the Karnak obelisk taken down and sent to Alexandria, meaning it for Constantinople, and that Constantius sent it on to Rome instead (NOVA). Constantius visited Rome once, in 357, and the obelisk was set up in the Circus Maximus then. By Turismo Roma's account it reached Ostia by ship and was towed up the Tiber. He is also said to have ordered temples closed in 356 (general knowledge, to be verified).

Domenico Fontana (born 1543, died 1607) was the architect of Pope Sixtus V. In 1586 he raised the obelisk in front of St Peter's (general knowledge, to be verified). The Lateran obelisk was dug up in 1587 in three pieces about 7 metres down. Fontana joined the pieces, added a new marble base and raised it in front of St John Lateran on 3 August 1588. On top he set a cross, with the Peretti family's emblems, and four lions by Ludovico del Duca surround the base (Turismo Roma).
Sources: NOVA Online, "A World of Obelisks: Rome"; Turismo Roma, The Lateran Obelisk; Wikipedia, Lateran Obelisk (pointer only); general knowledge, to be verified.

## The White Chapel of Senusret I (Open Air Museum)
Kind: site
About: white-chapel, white-chapel-rebuilt
The White Chapel is a small limestone pavilion made for Senusret I (co-ruler from about 1965 BC and sole king from about 1956 to 1911 BC in Shaw's chronology) and is one of the oldest structures at Karnak. It stands on a platform about 1.2 metres high and roughly 6.8 by 6.5 metres, with a stepped ramp at each end (it is aligned north to south). Four pillars stand inside, ringed by twelve square pillars joined by low parapet walls. The pillars are about 2.6 metres high. Digital Karnak dates it to Senusret I's reign; if it marks his thirtieth-year jubilee (an inference some sources make), and his years are counted from the start of the co-regency, it would date to about 1936 BC. The reliefs record his jubilee festival (heb-sed): the king is crowned and embraced by gods, and traces of red, blue, white and yellow paint survive. Sullivan thinks it was a bark shrine built for the festival; Digital Karnak adds that it may first have been a kiosk where the king sat on a double throne, and that Amenemhat III or IV converted it into a bark shrine.

The base carries a geographical list. Medini (2022), following Lacau and Chevrier, reports that its north and south sides list the provinces of Egypt, 22 for Upper Egypt and 16 for Lower Egypt. Each entry gives the province's name, its main gods, the extent of its territory, and the number of cubits and palms to subtract from an aroura (100 cubits) to give the local aroura. It is the oldest known list of gods tied to places. These are land and distance measures, not Nile flood heights. Sources disagree on which side holds which list: Wikipedia puts Upper Egypt on the west and Lower Egypt on the east, while Digital Karnak and Medini place the lists on the north and south sides and put Nile figures and lakes on the east and west sides.

Where it first stood is debated; it may have been outside the Middle Kingdom temple's inner wall. Amenhotep III pulled it down and used the blocks as fill in the Third Pylon (about 28 metres high). Henri Chevrier, emptying that pylon, found the first piece, a limestone lintel crushed under a huge alabaster slab, on 16 November 1927. Pillars, beams and base slabs followed over several seasons. He rebuilt it in the winter of 1937 to 1938: the first base block went in on 9 December 1937, the pillars between 5 and 25 January 1938, the beams by 15 February and the roof by 11 March 1938. Reinforced concrete cores went into the pillars, steel bars and bolts held broken beams, broken faces were trimmed so fragments met in a mortar joint, and gaps were filled with plaster painted to match the limestone. Modern studies are by Lacau and Chevrier (1956 and 1969) and Arnaudiès, Beaux and Chéné (2015).

Access: the Open Air Museum is reported as included in the main ticket (about EGP 600 for foreign adults on the ministry list of 2024) and open 06:00 to 17:00, with last entry about 16:00. Ramadan hours may be shorter. Works from 2024 to 2026 may fence parts off.
Sources: Digital Karnak, White Chapel page and Sullivan 2010 development PDF; Sullivan, UEE 2010; Medini, BIFAO 122 (2022), p. 401, notes 1 and 2; Chevrier, ASAE 28 (1928) and ASAE 38 (1938); Ministry ticket list 2024; Wikipedia (pointer only).

## The Red Chapel of Hatshepsut (Open Air Museum)
Kind: site
About: red-chapel, red-chapel-rebuilt
The Red Chapel was a two-roomed bark shrine, a vestibule and a sanctuary, made for Hatshepsut to hold the sacred boat of Amun-Ra. It is red quartzite (hence the name), on a grey diorite platform, with black granite exterior doorways. Digital Karnak gives about 15 metres long, 6.5 wide and 7.2 high; other accounts give about 18 by 6 by 5.5 metres, so treat sizes as approximate. Digital Karnak says work began in her seventeenth year; Sullivan says the chapel was finished about her year 17. Her year 17 is about 1457 BC (she became king about 1473 BC). It stood in the sanctuary, in her "Palace of Maat", reworked to fit it. The outside shows her obelisks being raised and the Opet and Beautiful Feast of the Valley processions, over a frieze of kneeling Nile gods.

Thutmose III completed some decoration, then dismantled it and replaced it with a red granite shrine, dated to his year 45 (about 1435 BC). Two black granite doorways were reused nearby and the other blocks were left lying about. Her figures were chiselled out only after that, late in his reign (after his year 42, about 1438 BC); some blocks, perhaps less exposed, escaped. Amenhotep III then buried the blocks as fill in the Third Pylon.

Chevrier's team lifted them out from 1926. His report of 1926 to 1927 lists 88 blocks of red quartzite and black granite from the pylon, and his report of 1927 to 1928 counts 241 in all, plus 20 already in a museum. The pylon facing sat on some of them, so he propped the wall and replaced each block with a masonry pillar as it came out. In 1947 and 1948 the blocks were laid out course by course on brick and sand plinths tracing the plan, while Lacau studied them. The chapel was rebuilt by the Franco-Egyptian centre at the turn of the millennium: the decision dates to 1997, work on the stones was under way by 1999 and 2000, and one visitor project says it finished in early 2002 (sources disagree; Lonely Planet says 2000). Over 300 original blocks are displayed, with modern stone for gaps (reported, not confirmed). Burgos and Larché published every block as a drawn facsimile (2006 and 2008).
Sources: Digital Karnak, Red Chapel page; Sullivan, UEE 2010; Chevrier, ASAE 27 (1927) and ASAE 28 (1928) and ASAE 49 (1949); Lacau and Chevrier 1977 and 1979; Larché, JAEA 1 (2016); ISIDA project page (weak); Wikipedia and Lonely Planet (pointers only).

## Why Hatshepsut's figures were erased
Kind: reference
About: hatshepsut, red-chapel, hatshepsut-proscription
Hatshepsut ruled as king from about 1473 to 1458 BC, first beside her young nephew Thutmose III. Sullivan places the "proscription", the attack on her name and image, after year 42 of Thutmose III's reign, about 1438 BC and more than twenty years after her death. At Karnak her name was erased (the names of Thutmose I or II were often carved instead), images of her were carefully altered, and only now and then was a monument or figure violently destroyed. Her standing obelisks, already walled in by Thutmose III, were left intact. Erased figures on the Eighth Pylon were recarved under Amenhotep II.

The Red Chapel shows the order of events. Its scenes were defaced after it had been taken down and its blocks left unused for an unknown time, according to Sullivan (citing Dorman and Van Siclen 1989). Digital Karnak says some blocks, perhaps less exposed, were left intact. Sullivan notes that the changes to her sanctuary came before the erasure and so were not caused by it. No source read for this batch states a motive. Older accounts blame personal revenge, but a delay of twenty years sits badly with that. The reasons are debated, and the guide should say so.

Where to look: on the Red Chapel, find smooth patches where a figure and cartouche were chiselled away, and blocks where her image was left.
Sources: Sullivan, UEE 2010 (section "The Proscription of Hatshepsut"); Digital Karnak, Red Chapel page; Shaw (ed.) 2000 for dates.

## The calcite bark shrine of Amenhotep I (Open Air Museum)
Kind: site
About: amenhotep-i-chapel, amenhotep-ii-chapel
The white calcite ("Egyptian alabaster") bark shrine of Amenhotep I stands near the White Chapel; OpenStreetMap places it about 30 metres to the north-north-east (approximate). Amenhotep I reigned about 1525 to 1504 BC. Digital Karnak gives it as about 3.6 metres across, 6.75 deep and 4.5 high, a roofed rectangle of large calcite blocks with wooden doors in the short ends. Each outer side has a single festival scene, and the inner walls hold the oldest surviving pictures of Amun-Ra's sacred boat. Amenhotep I left it unfinished, and Thutmose I finished the decoration of the south wall. The Chevrier reports and the CFEETK call it "of Amenhotep I and Thutmose I".

Where it stood is debated. Traditionally it was the main bark shrine in the Middle Kingdom court until Hatshepsut's time. Graindorge argues the main shrine was wooden and the calcite chapel stood further west. Sullivan says Hatshepsut may have moved it north of her Eighth Pylon. Thutmose III built a calcite shrine of the same name near the Seventh or Eighth Pylon, perhaps replacing it. Amenhotep III finally dismantled it for fill in the Third Pylon, where Chevrier found its blocks in 1926 to 1928 (six pieces in the 1927 to 1928 season). Chevrier's 1949 report says it was rebuilt in the museum "last year", so about 1947; the CFEETK also dates its rebuilding to 1947. A French-Egyptian project (2024 to 2026) plans to add a much larger Amenhotep I complex of about 30 by 50 metres (search summary of CFEETK page; page not opened).

Do not confuse it with the calcite shrine of Amenhotep II (reigned about 1427 to 1401 BC), rebuilt in 2005 between copies of Thutmose I's obelisks at the museum entrance (Larché 2016). Amenhotep II's shrine is inscribed for him and may have stood in Thutmose II's festival court.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Amenhotep I Calcite Chapel page; Sullivan, UEE 2010; Chevrier, ASAE 28 (1928) and ASAE 49 (1949); Grimal, Collège de France course summary (2011); Larché, JAEA 1 (2016); OpenStreetMap; CFEETK project page (search summary only).

## The Open Air Museum (what else it holds)
Kind: site
About: white-chapel, red-chapel, amenhotep-i-chapel
The Open Air Museum is in the north-west corner of the precinct. It holds monuments that earlier kings took down and buried in pylons and walls, rebuilt from the blocks. Besides the White Chapel, the Red Chapel and the Amenhotep I shrine it has: the double peristyle of Thutmose IV, a painted sandstone colonnade rebuilt from about a thousand blocks; the calcite shrine of Amenhotep II; a calcite shrine of Thutmose III (listed in OpenStreetMap and Wikipedia only); a reconstructed wall of Akhenaten's small blocks from the Third Pylon (Digital Karnak, Third Pylon page); and statues and relief blocks. Most of it is Third Pylon fill.

Access: the ministry announced finished works in August 2023 (sandstone paving, benches, signs, visitor paths). A French-Egyptian project of 2024 to 2026 is renewing the route and adding Amenhotep I monuments, so parts may be fenced off. The 2024 price list gives Karnak at 600 EGP (foreign adult) from 06:00 to 17:00, and recent visitors say the museum is included; older guides charged extra. It was quiet in recent reviews.

## Henri Chevrier
Kind: person
About: henri-chevrier, white-chapel-rebuilt, red-chapel
Henri Chevrier (1897 to 1974) was the French architect who directed the works at Karnak from 1926 to 1954, apart from the Second World War, succeeding Maurice Pillet (a bookseller's biography; his own reports confirm he followed Pillet). He emptied the Third Pylon from 1926 onwards, using suspended supports under the standing wall faces, and took out the Red Chapel, White Chapel, Amenhotep I and Thutmose IV blocks. He dismantled and rebuilt Taharqa's column (taken down 1926 to 1927, rebuilding begun in 1928), rebuilt the White Chapel in 1937 to 1938, rebuilt the Amenhotep I shrine about 1947, and laid out the Red Chapel blocks on plinths in 1947 to 1948. He worked under Pierre Lacau, Director General of the Antiquities Service. With Lacau he published the Red Chapel (1977 and 1979) and the White Chapel (1956 and 1969).

## The Sacred Lake
Kind: site
About: sacred-lake
The Sacred Lake is the large rectangular pool in the south-east of the Amun-Ra precinct. Digital Karnak says it measures 128 metres long and 83 metres wide, and credits it to Thutmose III (reigned 1479 to 1425 BC), who "records digging a new sacred lake at the Amun-Ra temple". An engineers' survey of the lake gives about 130 metres by 80 metres and a depth of about 4 metres, so the two sources agree within a few metres. The tour's older figure of 120 by 77 metres is not supported by either.

Digital Karnak says the lake may have replaced an earlier sacred lake somewhere in the southern part of the temple. It was used during religious festivals for flotillas of sacred barks, and for the daily enjoyment of the geese of Amun, the goose being one of the god's sacred animals. The lake as we see it was further modified under Taharqa (about 690 to 664 BC), but Digital Karnak says what those changes were, and how much the lake changed, is unknown.

The sides were lined with stone. The engineers' survey says the remains are clear on the west, south and north sides, where stairs down to the water survive. Around the lake stood a "vast array of functions" in Digital Karnak's words: Taharqa's edifice and a well on the north, a small chapel of Thoth to the west, administrative buildings from the Twenty-second Dynasty to the Ptolemaic period on the east, and storerooms and an aviary on the south.

What the priests did here is partly tradition. The engineers' paper says priests washed in the lake water each day before ritual. Herodotus wrote that Egyptian priests washed twice a day and twice each night, but that is a general account and not about this lake (general knowledge, verify). In ritual terms the lake stood for the primeval water from which the world was said to have risen, a link Digital Karnak makes for the deep well beside it.

Visitors single the lake out more than almost anything else on the south side. Two guides call it one of the most atmospheric corners of Karnak, especially in early morning light. There is little shade beside it.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Sacred Lake (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/sacred-lake), citing Carlotti 2001, Lauffray and others (Karnak V, X; Kemi 19), Goyon and Traunecker 1982; Elfiky and Elsaeed, "Hydraulic study for the Sacred Lake pollution at Elkarnak Temple, Egypt" (Ain Shams and Banha universities); Digital Karnak, Taharqo Edifice and Nilometer; WeRoad Karnak guide (December 2025) for the atmosphere; Shaw (2000) for reign dates; general knowledge for Herodotus.

## Where the Sacred Lake's water comes from
Kind: site
About: sacred-lake, aswan-high-dam
The lake is not fed by a channel from the Nile. It is a pit cut about 4 metres down into the ground, and its water is groundwater. An engineering study by Abdel Fattah Elfiky (Ain Shams University) and Gamal Elsaeed (Banha University) describes how that worked before the Aswan High Dam.

Each year the Nile flood soaked the valley for several months. That raised the underground water table, and the lake filled to its highest level in November. Then the dry season lowered the water table, and by July the lake became almost dry. The study says the annual change was about 0.8 metres in the water table. Because the level rose and fell, the water was refreshed every year, like a basin with a slow tide.

The High Dam (built 1960 to 1970, general knowledge) holds the flood upstream and controls the river's flow. After it, the study says, the groundwater level became almost constant all year. The lake stopped refreshing. Luxor's heat and fast evaporation then concentrated the salts, and the water turned stagnant and unpleasant, which hurt tourism. The study, written for a World Bank funded scheme, recommended two linked pumping stations, one on the Nile bank and one at the lake, to feed in fresh Nile water and draw out the old at the same rate. It also warned that the water table must be kept steady to protect the temple foundations from settlement. I could not confirm whether this was built, or what the lake's water looks like now.

The same groundwater is a danger to the stonework. Salts carried up from the water table attack sandstone, and a rising water table was part of the story of the 1899 collapse in the Hypostyle Hall (see that section). The Cachette Court nearby shows the same effect: statues were buried in a pit that filled with water, and Legrain had to lift water by hand to dig them out.

Some popular guides say the lake is linked to the Nile by underground channels. I found no evidence for that in the technical sources. The groundwater explanation is the one the engineers give.
Sources: Elfiky and Elsaeed, "Hydraulic study for the Sacred Lake pollution at Elkarnak Temple, Egypt" (undated paper on the Banha University staff site; I could not find its year or journal); Digital Karnak, Sacred Lake (which says nothing about the water source); general knowledge for the High Dam dates.

## Looking across the Sacred Lake: Taharqa's edifice and the well
Kind: site
About: sacred-lake, taharqa-edifice
From the south-east corner the lake spreads out in front of you, and the temple rises behind it to the north-west. The water is usually still, so the buildings reflect in it, and early in the day the light is soft. The view is a favourite with photographers, and the lake is the place reviewers most often call atmospheric.

On the far (north) side of the lake stood the Edifice of Taharqa, built by the Kushite king Taharqa (about 690 to 664 BC). Digital Karnak gives it as 25 metres wide and 29 metres deep, and says only 1 to 2.5 metres of its walls survive, so there is little to see except low foundations. In front of it was a mud brick courtyard, and a deep stone well cut through that courtyard. Digital Karnak calls the well a "Nilometer", the usual name for a stair or shaft used to measure the river's level, but says this one "seems to have instead served a ritual purpose": its deep shaft linked the temple to the primeval waters, and the rituals there helped the king reaffirm his power. Parker, Leclant and Goyon published the edifice in 1979, and Kathlyn Cooney wrote about its ritual role in 2000.

The Egyptians held that the world began as a dark, limitless water, Nun, from which the first land rose. A temple's sacred lake, and a well reaching the water table, brought that water into the temple. It is a good example of ritual and practical water supply being the same thing: the pit is only possible because the water table lies close below the surface.

The café is on the north-west side of the lake. There is little shade at the viewpoint.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Taharqo Edifice and Nilometer (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/taharqo-edifice), citing Parker, Leclant and Goyon (1979), Cooney (2000), Leclant (1965); Digital Karnak, Sacred Lake; WeRoad Karnak guide (December 2025); general knowledge for Nun.

## The Seventh Pylon
Kind: site
About: seventh-pylon
The Seventh Pylon is the gateway at the south end of the Cachette Court. Digital Karnak credits it to Thutmose III, who ruled from 1479 to 1425 BC. It is 26 metres high, 63.2 metres long and 10.6 metres thick, and built of sandstone. Inscriptions on one of his chapels say he replaced an earlier mud brick pylon on the same spot.

It stands between Hatshepsut's Eighth Pylon and the southern entrance of his festival court, and it opened the temple's southern processional route. That route ran south towards the Temple of Mut and, beyond, Luxor Temple. Most processions went east to west, but the god also travelled south, and each new pylon pushed the route further out.

The south face of both towers, the side away from the Cachette Court, shows Thutmose III smiting a group of cowering captives. This is the standard way of showing a king's victory over Egypt's enemies, and it appears on the front of nearly every pylon. A public-domain photograph shows him smiting Canaanite enemies on this pylon, and the scene is linked to the Megiddo campaign of his Year 23 (about 1457 BC) in the photograph's caption. Two colossal statues of the king flanked the doorway on the south side, and two rose granite obelisks stood just south of them. Digital Karnak does not give their sizes.

From the north, in the Cachette Court, you see the back of the pylon. The court takes its name from the great pit of statues found here in 1903 (see the Cachette sections).

How a pylon was built is explained in the First Pylon sections: a rubble core, casing blocks laid in courses, and mud brick ramps raised as the walls grew. The Seventh Pylon will have been built in the same way, though Digital Karnak records no construction details.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Seventh Pylon (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/7th-pylon), citing Azim and Traunecker 1982, Carlotti 1995, Laskowski, Cline and O'Connor 2006; Shaw (2000); Wikimedia Commons caption, "Thutmose III at Karnak.jpg" (public domain) for the Megiddo link.

## The Cachette Court and the Karnak Cachette
Kind: site
About: cachette, cachette-burial, georges-legrain
The Cachette Court lies between the Hypostyle Hall and the Seventh Pylon. "Cachette" is French for a hiding place. Here, in the Greek and Roman period, many stone and metal statues were buried in one large pit, and in 1903 the French Egyptologist Georges Legrain began finding them. Emmanuel Jambon, who studied Legrain's records for the French Institute (IFAO), gives the discovery as 26 December 1903, with four seasons of work lasting until July 1907. Some sources say 1903 to 1905, but the IFAO's study supports 1907.

What was in it. The stone statues range from the Middle Kingdom to the Ptolemaic period: kings, priests, officials, gods, a great many block statues of private people. The totals vary by source and by date of counting. Legrain's first-season summaries give 457 statues and statuettes. The writer Gaston Maspero, who headed the Antiquities Service, wrote in 1905 that seven hundred stone monuments had been brought out of the water, with more than ten thousand bronzes. Popular accounts say more than 750, or nearly 800, stone statues and 17,000 or 18,000 bronzes. Legrain himself reached about 17,000 bronzes by 1906. The IFAO database, a modern catalogue, holds well over a thousand records, but that counts fragments and other objects too. Safe wording: several hundred stone statues, in the range 750 to 800 by the usual count, and about 17,000 bronzes.

Why it was buried. Nobody recorded the reason. Legrain thought the pit was filled in one go, and Jambon found no link between an object's age and its depth, which supports that. Scholars usually explain such deposits as the respectful burial of statues that were crowded out of the temple or no longer wanted, since sacred objects could not simply be thrown away (general knowledge, verify).

Legrain's other job was the Hypostyle Hall. He was rebuilding it after the collapse of 1899, and digging in this court was part of his wider work around the Seventh Pylon. He died in 1917.

Most statues went to the Egyptian Museum in Cairo, and the French database catalogues them with Legrain's own "K" numbers. Legrain's own excavation diaries are lost.
Sources: Jambon, "Les fouilles de Georges Legrain dans la Cachette de Karnak (1903–1907)", BIFAO 109 (2010), pp. 239–279; IFAO, Base Cachette de Karnak (Coulon and Jambon); Digital Karnak, Seventh Pylon (Greco-Roman burial); general knowledge for why such deposits were made.

## Digging the Cachette: water, shadufs and numbers
Kind: site
About: cachette, georges-legrain
The pit was deep and wet. The Nile flood pushes the water table up in autumn, and Legrain's season began on 28 September 1903 with the groundwater still high. Jambon says Legrain wrote repeatedly to the Antiquities Service director that October about the seepage. The pit lay below the water table, so it flooded as fast as it was dug.

Legrain solved it with the simplest local technology. He reported that "rustic apparatus", shadufs (hand-swung bucket-and-counterweight lifts, still used on the Nile), let the dig be taken down to about 10 metres in 1904 to 1905. A year later he reported reaching nearly 14 metres, and the work stopped on 14 June 1906. Jambon estimates the maximum between 10 and 15 metres. Maspero put it in a famous phrase: "twenty months we have been fishing for statues in the temple of Karnak". In May 1904 the pit was so flooded and foul that Legrain wrote it "stinks marvellously".

Not everything survived the water. In May and June 1905, at nine to ten metres, Legrain found a bank of wooden furniture and wooden statues, about ten cubic metres. Within hours of leaving the damp they dried, split and crumbled; he tried to keep the best in a railway wagon full of water, and sent a few examples to the museum, but they did not last. He also found many animal mummies.

The bronzes are a mystery. Legrain's counts rose to about 17,000 by 1906, yet Jambon says they seem to have vanished entirely since. He also notes that when Maspero reprinted his article in 1914 he removed its mention of the bronzes. Jambon suggests Maspero may have known more than we do about what happened to them. I found no explanation. So "about 17,000 bronzes" is what Legrain counted, not what a museum holds.

For the family: the Hypostyle Hall's collapse and this dig happened in the same few years, with the same man, and both were fights with the Nile's groundwater.
Sources: Jambon, BIFAO 109 (2010), pp. 239–279 (reports and letters of Legrain, Archaeological Report 1903 to 1906, and Maspero's articles of 1905 and 1914); general knowledge for the shaduf.

## The Hittite peace treaty on the Cachette Court wall
Kind: site
About: hittite-treaty, ramesses-ii
On the wall at the west side of the Cachette Court is a long text in hieroglyphs: the treaty between Ramesses II of Egypt and the Hittite king Hattusili III. The Biblical Archaeology Society describes it as inscribed in the centre of the wall between two engaged pillars, next to the Hypostyle Hall, and partly destroyed. Four carved scenes flank it, two on each side. These were once attributed to Ramesses II, but are now identified as episodes from the Canaanite campaign of his son Merneptah.

When. The treaty was agreed in the 21st year of Ramesses II, which is about 1259 BC in Shaw's chronology (some sources give 1258 BC). It ended roughly two decades of hostility after the battle of Kadesh, in about 1274 BC. I could not find out when the Karnak copy was carved, only that it is later than the treaty.

What it says. Neither state would invade the other, and each would help the other if a third party attacked. The treaty was written in both languages: each side wrote a version in its own language, and each was also set down in Akkadian cuneiform on metal, perhaps silver, tablets for exchange.

The two surviving versions. The Egyptian text survives in two places in Thebes, here at Karnak and at the Ramesseum, Ramesses II's mortuary temple. Wikipedia gives the Karnak text as 30 lines and the Ramesseum text as 10 lines, preserved only as fragments, but I could not confirm those numbers in a Karnak-specific source. Champollion copied the Karnak text in 1828. The Hittite side comes from clay tablets in Akkadian dug up at Hattusa (Boğazkale, Turkey) by Hugo Winckler's expedition, starting in 1906. Two are in the Museum of the Ancient Orient, part of the Istanbul Archaeology Museums, and one is in Berlin. The texts differ in wording, because each side framed its own.

The "oldest surviving peace treaty" is a common description. It is certainly the best-known early treaty with both sides' text surviving, but I did not check the claim against older treaties.

A copy is said to hang at the United Nations headquarters in New York. That comes from Wikipedia, citing two 1970 newspaper pieces. I could not confirm it from the United Nations, so treat it as reported but unverified.
Sources: Biblical Archaeology Society, "Understanding the Wall of Reliefs" and "A Good Peace: the Hittite-Egyptian Treaty" (library.biblicalarchaeology.org); Wikipedia, Egyptian–Hittite peace treaty (pointer only: line counts, tablets' locations, Champollion, UN claim, Winckler); Shaw (2000) for the regnal year.

## Hugo Winckler and the Hittite tablets
Kind: person
About: hugo-winckler, hattusa-tablets-found
Hugo Winckler (born 4 July 1863, died 19 April 1913) was a German Assyriologist. He excavated Hattusa, the Hittite capital in Turkey, from 1906 to 1908 with Theodore Makridi, and found the royal archives, thousands of clay tablets. Among them was the Akkadian version of the treaty with Ramesses II. The Egyptian side of the treaty was known long before, from the walls at Karnak that Champollion copied in 1828, so for the first time both sides' wording could be compared. Two tablets are in Istanbul and one in Berlin. He found them only three years after Legrain began the Cachette at Karnak, in the same decade.
Sources: Wikipedia, Egyptian–Hittite peace treaty (pointer; dates of the excavation and tablets' locations); Wikipedia, Hugo Winckler (birth and death dates, general knowledge, verify).

## The southern pylons (Eighth, Ninth and Tenth)
Kind: site
About: eighth-pylon, ninth-pylon, tenth-pylon
South of the Seventh Pylon the old southern road of Karnak runs on through three more pylons, each built a little further south, towards the Temple of Mut and on to Luxor.

The Eighth Pylon was begun under Hatshepsut (reigned 1473 to 1458 BC, Shaw; Digital Karnak dates the work 1479 to 1458 BC). It is 21 metres high, 47.7 metres long, 9.3 metres thick, and sandstone. It was the first of the surviving pylons on the southern route and may have replaced a mud brick one. Statues of Thutmose II, Amenhotep I with Ahmose-Nefertari, and Amenhotep II stood along its south face. Her own decoration is mostly lost, because later kings recarved it: Thutmose III, Amenhotep II, Tutankhamun and Sety I. The north face shows the king with the gods and a procession of the sacred bark, possibly the Opet festival. It is the oldest pylon on the southern route, but not the oldest at Karnak, since Thutmose I built the Fourth and Fifth Pylons earlier. The tour's older wording was wrong on that.

The Ninth Pylon was built by Horemheb (reigned 1323 to 1295 BC, as Digital Karnak gives it), 26.1 metres high and 65.6 metres long. Horemheb dismantled Akhenaten's temples at east Karnak and used their small sandstone blocks, the talatat, as fill inside the pylon. Ramesses II added reliefs between the Eighth and Ninth Pylons showing the Opet procession of the sacred barks.

The Tenth Pylon, the furthest south, was begun by Amenhotep III, who finished only its lowest courses. Horemheb completed it, 66.6 metres long and 21 metres high, with a rose granite doorway 15.62 metres high. The upper towers contain more of Akhenaten's blocks. Its court shows Horemheb's voyage to the land of Punt and the treasures brought back. A doorway on the east side led to offices, stores and priests' quarters.

Access is uncertain. Two visitor accounts (one undated, one written in 2020 and updated in 2024) say much of the area beyond the Eighth Pylon is closed, but Commons photographs show people at the Eighth, Ninth and Tenth Pylons on 10 March 2024 and on 4 December 2025.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Eighth Pylon (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/8th-pylon), Ninth Pylon, Tenth Pylon; Shaw (2000); Wikimedia Commons photographs dated 10 March 2024 and 4 December 2025 (access evidence); visitor accounts at thediscoveriesof.com and sailingstonetravel.com.

## The Temple of Khonsu
Kind: site
About: khonsu-temple
The Temple of Khonsu stands in the south-west corner of the precinct, facing south. The American Research Center in Egypt, which conserved it, calls it "an excellent example of a small but complete New Kingdom temple". It has a pylon, an open court with a portico of 28 papyrus-bud columns, a columned hall, and a bark chamber with the sanctuary behind. Digital Karnak gives the whole temple as about 70 metres long and 27 metres wide, with a pylon 34.5 metres long and 18 metres high.

Ramesses III (reigned 1184 to 1153 BC) began it, but not as a first temple. The Epigraphic Survey of the Oriental Institute says he used both newly quarried stone and old inscribed blocks, mostly Eighteenth Dynasty, taken from earlier buildings and recut. One reused block comes from the reign of Thutmose IV, and a column drum in the court still carries the double cartouche of Horemheb. Digital Karnak says reused blocks may even point to an earlier temple here, with some linked to Amenhotep III and possibly from his mortuary temple across the river.

Who finished it is argued. Epigraphic Survey: Ramesses XI and the High Priest Herihor built the front (the pylon, court and first hall), and "additions and alterations continued for over a millennium". Digital Karnak lists inscriptions of Ramesses III, Ramesses IV, Ramesses XI, Ramesses XII, Herihor and Pinedjem. About 690 to 664 BC Taharqa added a porch of four rows of five papyrus columns in front of the pylon. Later the Ptolemies added the great gateway south of the temple (the Bab el-Amara).

The temple was heavily used for oracles. The Epigraphic Survey says the god Khonsu gave oracular appearances here and that texts record land purchases and royal restorations.

The tour's date of about 1170 BC for the start is a mid-reign marker only. Ramesses III's reign gives the limits, and no source I found gives a start year.

The temple has been conserved by ARCE from 2006 to 2018, and further work began later.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Khonsu Temple (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/khonsu-temple); Epigraphic Survey, The Temple of Khonsu, vol. 1 (OIP 100, 1979) and vol. 2 (OIP 103, 1981), prefaces; ARCE, Khonsu Temple (arce.org/?p=3600); Shaw (2000).

## Khonsu the moon god
Kind: reference
About: khonsu-temple, opet-temple
Khonsu was the Egyptian moon god, and in Thebes the child of Amun and Mut, so the third member of the Theban triad. His name is usually translated "traveller", for the moon's journey across the night sky. He was also seen as a healer and protector of people travelling at night. The Bentresh stela tells of a princess of a far country cured when an image of him was sent there. His main cult centre was Karnak, and much Ramesside building here was done for him. (General knowledge, from Wikipedia, verify.)

At the Khonsu temple he is shown in the usual Egyptian way for a child god: a boy with a plaited sidelock of youth (general knowledge, verify). A Commons photograph of a carving in the columned hall is captioned as Khonsu in the form of a baboon, an animal linked with the moon and with Thoth, the god of writing.

Digital Karnak adds a Theban myth about rebirth. In one version Amun dies as Osiris, and is reborn through the mother goddess Opet. Khonsu, as son of Amun-Ra, was seen as the newly reborn sun god. That ties his temple to the Opet temple beside it, which has a doorway directly opposite one in the Khonsu temple.

The Epigraphic Survey's volumes show how real this was for the temple's builders. Herihor is shown several times in a scene of the Opet festival, with the barks of Amun, Mut and Khonsu being towed on the Nile, in the Khonsu temple. A procession of the portable barks of the Theban triad is carved on the north wall of the portico.
Sources: Wikipedia, Khonsu (pointer, verify); Digital Karnak, Opet Temple (the myth); Epigraphic Survey, OIP 100 (1979), preface; Wikimedia Commons caption, "Karnak Tempel Chons 09.jpg" (Olaf Tausch); general knowledge for the sidelock.

## Herihor and the late Ramesside work at the Khonsu temple
Kind: person
About: herihor, khonsu-temple
Herihor was High Priest of Amun at the end of the New Kingdom, under Ramesses XI, the last Ramesside king (Shaw: about 1099 to 1069 BC). The Epigraphic Survey says he carved most of the scenes in the first hypostyle hall as High Priest, and that he claimed to do so on the "instruction of His Majesty" Ramesses XI. Later he took royal titles, and the court of the Khonsu temple is where that is best attested. The only other monument that formally names him as king is the Hypostyle Hall, where he added an inscription under scenes of Ramesses II.

In the court he copied the layout of the Hypostyle Hall: raised relief on the left (west) of the axis, sunk relief on the right (east). On the east wall of the court he had the façade of the Second Pylon shown. On the west wall of the portico is his family procession, which the Survey re-read: the first prince was not Paiankh, as had been assumed from Lepsius's old drawing, but Ankhefenmut. That weakens the old belief that Paiankh, who succeeded him as High Priest, was Herihor's son.

His wife Nuteme kept a respected place in Thebes after his death and was buried with papyri naming her King's Mother. His children seem to have faded from the record.

Herihor's famous oracle stela is on the façade of the portico, now badly worn, and cut through when a Thirtieth Dynasty doorway was installed. The Survey volumes (1979 and 1981) record all of this.

The era is a turning point: in the Twenty-first Dynasty (from about 1069 BC) kings ruled the north from Tanis while High Priests of Amun ran Thebes, and Pinedjem, also seen at Khonsu, became a king in Thebes.
Sources: Epigraphic Survey, The Temple of Khonsu, vol. 1 (OIP 100, 1979), preface by Wente; vol. 2 (OIP 103, 1981), preface; Digital Karnak, Khonsu Temple; Shaw (2000).

## The roof, the Taharqa porch and the conservation of the Khonsu temple
Kind: site
About: khonsu-temple, khonsu-conservation
Most of Karnak is open to the sky, but the Khonsu temple still has much of its stone roof over the columned hall and rooms. A visitor in June described its "intact ceiling and painted reliefs". The Epigraphic Survey mentions a solar chapel on the roof, with a scene carved under the High Priest Pinedjem I (the Survey spells the name Painutem). A stair leads up. ARCE says a mason installed sandstone roof-lights over the chapels, and a large replacement roofing block over the staircase to the roof. A safe viewing space on the roof, with a view over Karnak, was reported as nearly complete. I could not confirm that it is open.

Why does a stone roof matter? It spans the gaps between columns with stone beams, architraves, and slabs laid across them. That is a heavy load, which is why Egyptian halls have columns packed close together. Digital Karnak makes the point about Taharqa's porch, where the columns stood 7.2 to 8.2 metres apart: "a stone roof would have been too heavy to span", so it suggests a timber roof carried on stone architraves. The porch had four rows of five papyrus columns and a red granite floor, and it echoed one Taharqa built before the Ramesses II temple at the east end of Karnak.

The court of the Khonsu temple, by contrast, was unroofed, a portico of 28 columns round an open space.

ARCE, with USAID funding, worked here in phases from 2006 to 2018 and again from the 2020s. It cleaned and consolidated painted reliefs in six of the twelve chapels, laid a stone walkway, new flooring in the court and hall, and partly rebuilt the north façade. Chapel 12 was the model, with visitor-friendly flooring, solar lighting and removable guards. Some of the vivid reliefs had been hidden for centuries under smoke and grime.

Ancient Origins reported four chapels and two tombs opened to visitors, with a new walkway, but gave no date.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Khonsu Temple (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/khonsu-temple and ?p=3792); ARCE, Khonsu Temple (arce.org/?p=3600) and later ARCE update (via search summary, not fully read); Epigraphic Survey, OIP 100 (solar chapel, footnote); visitor account at thediscoveriesof.com.

## The Temple of Opet
Kind: site
About: opet-temple
The small Temple of Opet lies just west of the Khonsu temple. Opet was the mother goddess of Thebes, shown as a hippopotamus in some forms, and the temple is tied to the idea of rebirth (the hippopotamus link is general knowledge, verify). Digital Karnak says the temple was originally built by Nectanebo I (380 to 362 BC), modified by Ptolemy III (246 to 221 BC), who inscribed the crypt, and left unfinished until Ptolemy VIII. Most of the interior decoration is his, and the outside was finished under the Roman emperor Augustus. The earliest part may be Taharqa's porch and small pylon, so an earlier Opet temple probably stood here. The building's sequence is still unclear, and the CFEETK is studying it.

The plan is small. A small pylon and columned porch face west, with a columned kiosk in the court. A ramp leads up to the raised temple, a podium 19.6 by 22.7 metres and 1.9 metres high. Inside is a little hall with two Hathor-headed columns, then a square sanctuary with a niche for the god's statue.

The crypts are the special feature. They run down into the platform, and include a "tomb of Osiris" and a "birth chamber". They fit the myth that Amun dies as Osiris and is reborn through Opet. A small shrine to Osiris's cult statue is on the east rear wall, reached from outside.

The temple stands directly opposite a door of the Khonsu temple, which points to a close cult link.

Access: one visitor account (written 2020, updated 2024) found it locked and saw it only from the side. Commons has eight photographs taken inside on 10 March 2024, so it can be open. The crypts are almost certainly not open to the public (my inference).
Sources: Digital Karnak, Opet Temple (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/opet-temple/), citing Azim 1987, Golvin and Goyon 1987, Arnold 1999, Wilkinson 2000; Wikimedia Commons photographs dated 10 March 2024; sailingstonetravel.com visitor account.

## The Bab el-Amara gate (Ptolemy III's gateway)
Kind: site
About: bab-el-amara
The Bab el-Amara is the great stone gateway south of the Khonsu temple, in the enclosure wall of Nectanebo I. It is 21 metres high, and the opening is 14 to 14.4 metres high and 5.6 metres wide. Digital Karnak says Nectanebo I, or his successor Teos, began two pylon towers, each planned to be 28.2 metres long, to front the Khonsu temple with a new gate. They were never finished. Ptolemy III Euergetes (246 to 221 BC) then put a stone gateway between the planned towers and carved it with his name. A later Ptolemaic king built a mud brick wall against it, which covered the unfinished towers.

It is sometimes called the Gate of Euergetes. Digital Karnak's page on enclosures calls it a Ptolemy VIII gate, but its gate page and other sources give Ptolemy III, which is the better supported.

Its design: a winged sun disk at the centre of the lintel, flying vultures on the ceiling, and the hieroglyphs for "all life, prosperity and health" on the inner walls. The north face shows the king with divinities. A sphinx-lined alley ran south from it.

It gave the temple of Khonsu a direct way in from the south. Visitors reach the Khonsu temple through this southern side. The unfinished towers are a useful reminder that even late Egyptian building programmes were left half done.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Bab el-Amara Gate (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/bab-el-amara-gate/), citing Lauffray, Sauneron and Ramadan 1975, Zignani 2003, Laroche-Traunecker 1982; Digital Karnak, Khonsu Temple (the gate gave direct access); Shaw (2000).

## Gaston Maspero
Kind: person
About: gaston-maspero
Gaston Maspero (born 23 June 1846, died 30 June 1916; general knowledge, verify) was the French Egyptologist who led the Egyptian Antiquities Service, Legrain's employer. Legrain's letters about the Karnak dig were addressed to him. In 1905 Maspero wrote a newspaper article, "La pêche aux statues" ("fishing for statues"), for Le Temps, saying that after twenty months seven hundred stone monuments had been brought out of the water with more than ten thousand bronzes. When he reprinted the article in 1914 in his book Ruines et paysages d'Égypte he cut the mention of the bronzes, which Emmanuel Jambon found suspicious given that the bronzes have since disappeared from the record.
Sources: Jambon, BIFAO 109 (2010), pp. 239–279; general knowledge for his dates.

## Amun, Thebes and the origins of Karnak
Kind: reference
About: intef-column, intef-ii, senusret-temple, quay
Karnak is the temple of Amun-Ra, "king of the gods", in the city the Egyptians called Waset and the Greeks later called Thebes. Amun was at first a minor god. He rose as Thebes rose, first as the home town of a family of local rulers, the Intefs, in the Eleventh Dynasty, then as the royal city of the Middle and New Kingdoms.

The earliest firm evidence for a temple of Amun-Ra here comes from the reign of Intef II, who ruled about 2112 to 2063 BC (Shaw's chronology). It is a sandstone column, found reused at Karnak, carrying an inscription dedicated by that king. Sullivan reads it as part of a small mud-brick temple with a stone-columned portico. A stela from the Intef cemetery on the west bank that mentions a "Temple of Amun" supports this. Scholars have debated how to read the god's name on the column (one reading gives "Month-Re", the other "Amun-Ra"); Ullmann argues for Amun-Ra, and that is the reading Sullivan follows.

Egyptologists once thought the temple went back to the Old Kingdom, because a list of ancestor kings in Thutmose III's Festival Hall begins with Sneferu, and because Old Kingdom statues were found in the Karnak Cachette. Sullivan explains why that is weak: later priests collected old statues, and the CFEETK's excavations in the Middle Kingdom court found no Old Kingdom pottery. So the best-supported answer is that no Amun temple stood here before about 2100 BC. The ground itself was occupied earlier: Pennington and colleagues (2025) date the first permanent occupation of the river island to the Old Kingdom.

The CFEETK's Luc Gabolde found small sandstone platforms, none bigger than about 10 by 10 metres, below the Middle Kingdom court. Some reused blocks date to the early Eleventh Dynasty. Sullivan reports this as probably the site of Intef II's temple, taken down soon after his reign and rebuilt by later Eleventh Dynasty kings and then Amenemhat I. Senusret I (sole king about 1956 to 1911 BC in Shaw's chronology) then built a far grander limestone temple on the spot.

The river mattered too: Karnak began on a sandy terrace between Nile channels, and the temple grew westward as the river moved west (see "The Nile at Karnak"). Charloux and colleagues (2021) link the temple's beginnings, about 2200 to 2000 BC, to the retreating riverbank.
Sources: Sullivan, UEE "Karnak: Development of the Temple of Amun-Ra" (2010), pp. 1 to 4; Pennington and others, Antiquity 99 (2025); Charloux and others, Antiquity 95 (2021); Shaw (ed.), Oxford History of Ancient Egypt (2000) for reign dates; Ullmann (2007) as cited by Sullivan.

## The Estate of Amun: how rich the temple was
Kind: reference
About: papyrus-harris, intef-column
By the New Kingdom Karnak was not only a temple but one of the largest landowners and employers in Egypt. Its wealth paid for the building you see: the pylons, the obelisks and the vast hall all came from the king, but the temple's own estates fed, housed and equipped the priests, craftsmen and farmers who kept it running.

The best account is Papyrus Harris I, in the British Museum (EA 9999). It is a hieratic papyrus about 41 metres long, the longest known from Egypt, with roughly 1,500 lines. It was written for Ramesses III, who reigned 1184 to 1153 BC (Shaw), and its opening gives his thirty-second year. It was probably finished under his son Ramesses IV. Most of it lists gifts to the temples of Egypt, mainly those of Amun at Thebes, Ra at Heliopolis and Ptah at Memphis, and ends with a short history of the reign.

Summaries of the papyrus say the Theban temples of Amun held about 80 per cent of the land and about 86 per cent of the livestock belonging to the temples listed. I have taken those percentages from a Wikipedia summary of Grandet's edition and have not checked them against Grandet's own tables, so treat them as approximate. One museum page also gives 309,950 sacks of grain for the Theban list. I did not find reliable figures for workers, ships or acreage, so I give none.

What the evidence does show is a pattern. The more the god owned, the more the priests who ran his estates mattered. Akhenaten diverted the Amun temple's wealth to his new city at Amarna about 1348 BC (see the Akhenaten section). After Ramesses III the High Priests of Amun grew steadily stronger, until by 1069 BC they were ruling Upper Egypt from Thebes. Sullivan notes that a High Priest or a God's Wife of Amun sometimes gained enough power to sponsor building in the precinct in their own names, which was rare for anyone but a king.
Sources: Papyrus Harris I (British Museum EA 9999; edition: Grandet 1994), via Wikipedia "Papyrus Harris I" and Google Arts and Culture (secondary summaries, not checked against Grandet); Sullivan, UEE (2010); Shaw (2000).

## Akhenaten and the restoration
Kind: reference
About: akhenaten, tutankhamun, akhenaten-turn, gempaaten, restoration-stela
In his fifth year, about 1348 BC, King Amenhotep IV changed his name to Akhenaten and began a fervent attack on gods other than the sun-disc Aten. Amun was the special target. His name and image were cut out of temples across Egypt, Karnak included. Sullivan, following Redford, says the king then left Thebes for a new city, Akhetaten (modern Amarna), the wealth of the Amun temple was diverted to it, and the temple itself was closed.

At Karnak, Akhenaten's builders worked in east Karnak, outside the Amun precinct. They raised a huge temple called Gempaaten ("the Aten is found") from small, light sandstone blocks called talatat, small and easy to carry, which suited quick building (my inference, not stated by Sullivan). Its court had a colonnade with colossal statues of the king and Nefertiti against each pier. Sullivan lists at least four Akhenaten buildings known from inscribed talatat. Talatat blocks from his buildings were later found removed from pylons at Karnak and Luxor, where they had been reused as fill (Sullivan).

After Akhenaten died (1336 BC in Shaw), Tutankhamun reopened temples and restarted work at Thebes. His Restoration Stela, found by Legrain at Karnak in 1905, says the temples had fallen into ruin and that he restored them. Sources differ on its date: some read year 4 of his reign, others year 1 and treat it as recording a programme already under way. Horemheb later put his own name over many of Tutankhamun's cartouches on it.

Tutankhamun also set up ram-headed sphinxes on the road from Karnak to the Mut temple. Sullivan reports that study suggests they were originally human-headed figures of Akhenaten and Nefertiti, with ram heads substituted, and that Horemheb then usurped them. Horemheb took Gempaaten down block by block as fill for his new pylons, and the proscription of Akhenaten's memory soon extended to Tutankhamun.
Sources: Sullivan, UEE (2010) and Digital Karnak, "Development of the Temple of Karnak" (2010), pp. 6, 21 to 22; Digital Karnak, Restoration Stela of Tutankhamen (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/?p=6414); Redford (1984) as cited by Sullivan; Shaw (2000).

## Priest-kings, Libyans, Kushites and the Assyrian sack (1069 to 664 BC)
Kind: reference
About: assyrian-sack, gods-wives-of-amun, bubastite-portal, taharqa-kiosk
After Ramesses XI, about 1069 BC, Egypt split. Kings of the Twenty-first Dynasty ruled the north (their capital was in the Delta, general knowledge), while the High Priests of Amun, beginning with Herihor and Pinedjem I, ruled Upper Egypt from Thebes. Both put their names and sphinx avenues on the Karnak precinct. Sullivan notes that building then shifted largely to the north of Egypt for the Twenty-first to Twenty-fourth Dynasties.

The Libyan kings of the Twenty-second Dynasty put their own sons into the High Priesthood to keep Thebes loyal. Shoshenq I (945 to 924 BC) built the great court in front of the Second Pylon and carved his campaign on the Bubastite Portal, which Digital Karnak links to the biblical Shishak (1 Kings 14:25).

From about 750 BC the Kushite kings of the south took control of Thebes, in large part through a priestess, the God's Wife of Amun (see that section). Karnak was in the spotlight again. Shabaqo added gateways and porches, and Taharqa (690 to 664 BC) built his ten-column kiosk in the first court, of which one column still stands.

The Kushite rule ended when the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal invaded. Egypt was sacked and, as Sullivan says, the temples of Thebes were reportedly plundered. Sullivan adds that damage to the Amun-Ra temple itself seems to have been minimal, as little evidence of the event has been found. Assyrian sources and Egyptian dates vary: Shaw's chronology gives 664 BC, and some books give 663. Sullivan also suggests that the red granite bark shrine of Thutmose III may have been damaged by fires in the Assyrian or Persian invasions, which is why Philip Arrhidaeus later replaced it.

Montuemhat, a Theban official who served under Taharqa and the first Saite king, Psamtik I, became in effect the governor of a largely self-governing Thebes after the sack (secondary source, Wikipedia pointer). In 656 BC Psamtik brought Thebes under Saite control by having his daughter adopted as the next God's Wife.
Sources: Sullivan, UEE (2010), pp. 15 to 21; Digital Karnak, "Development of the Temple of Karnak" (2010), pp. 9 to 10, 23; Wikipedia "Montuemhat" (pointer only); Shaw (2000).

## The God's Wives of Amun
Kind: person
About: gods-wives-of-amun, adoption-of-nitocris
From the Twenty-fifth Dynasty on, a royal woman was set apart as the God's Wife of Amun, also called Divine Adorer of Amun. She served the god at Karnak, was supposed to remain unmarried, and passed her office to an adopted daughter. Sullivan notes that these priestesses appear in the decoration of small Osiris chapels built around the north and east of the Amun-Ra temple, an unusual honour for anyone but a king.

The office served politics. The Kushite kings placed their daughters in it, so that Thebes was held through the God's Wife. Shepenupet II, a daughter of the Kushite king Piye, was the serving God's Wife, and Amenirdis II, daughter of Taharqa, was named as heir. After Assyria had sacked Thebes, Psamtik I sent a fleet upriver and in 656 BC, his ninth year, had his own daughter Nitocris adopted as heir. The Adoption Stela, found at Karnak in 1897, is now in the Cairo Museum. Nitocris held the office about 655 to 585 BC, and the Saites ruled Upper Egypt through her.

The dates and relationships come from a UCL Digital Egypt page and Wikipedia pointers, whose dates differ slightly, so the years are approximate.
Sources: Sullivan, UEE (2010), p. 9 of Digital Karnak PDF; UCL Digital Egypt, "The God's Wife of Amun" (ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/thebes/late/godswife.html); Wikipedia "Nitocris I" and "Shepenupet II" (pointers only).

## Greeks, Romans and the end of the temple
Kind: reference
About: temple-closure, first-pylon, rams-avenue
The last native kings, Nectanebo I and II (380 to 343 BC), enclosed Karnak in huge mud-brick walls and began the First Pylon, the largest in Egypt. After Alexander (332 BC) the Ptolemies kept up the tradition. Philip Arrhidaeus replaced Thutmose III's granite bark shrine, Ptolemy III added the gate known as Bab el-Amara at the Khonsu temple, Ptolemy IV built an "Osiris catacomb" in the north-east, and Ptolemy VIII completed the Opet temple. Under Rome (30 BC to AD 395) the western entrance was reorganised and the sphinxes before the First Pylon were rearranged into their present places.

Building then stopped. Sullivan writes that by the end of the Roman period Karnak would be closed, and that by the fourth century AD monuments were being torn down rather than built. The "unique" obelisk of Thutmose IV and a western obelisk of Thutmose III were sent to Alexandria and later to Constantinople and Rome. The other fallen obelisks were broken up for door thresholds and millstones.

When exactly did worship end? No source I found gives a date for Karnak. Imperial bans on pagan cult came in the fourth century: Constantius II closed temples in 356 and Theodosius I issued bans in 391 and 392 (general knowledge; the Wikipedia history page names Constantine for 356, which I believe is an error). The last dated hieroglyphic inscription, at Philae, is from 394. Philae itself was closed by Justinian between 535 and 537, according to a secondary source, which shows that edicts did not shut every temple at once. The best-supported statement is that Karnak stopped working as a state temple during the fourth century and no later than the fifth, and that its exact closure is unknown.
Sources: Sullivan, UEE (2010) pp. 20 to 22 and Digital Karnak PDF (2010) pp. 12 to 13; Wikipedia "History of the Karnak Temple complex" and "Graffito of Esmet-Akhom" (pointers); general knowledge for the edicts (verify).

## Churches, a village and the travellers (AD 400 to 1858)
Kind: reference
About: christian-karnak, commission-des-sciences, akhmenu
After the temple fell silent, Christians founded churches among the ruins. The best known is in the central hall of Thutmose III's Akhmenu, where painted saints and Coptic inscriptions survive. Wikipedia cites Blyth (2006, page 234) for this. A local heritage site gives the fourth century, but I found no scholarly source for the century, so the date is uncertain, and the AD 400 on the timeline is a round figure.

For the long period from the Arab conquest of Egypt (about 641) to the Mamluks, the notes from my sources are thin. I found no source for medieval Arab accounts of Karnak. The village of Karnak grew among the ruins, and its name comes from the Arabic, which one source gives as meaning "fortified village". Etymology is contested. The name "Carnac" is first recorded in 1668 when two Capuchin missionaries, Protais and Charles François d'Orléans, travelled through the area.

European travellers followed. An unknown Venetian wrote the first known European description in 1589. Paul Lucas made the first drawing in 1704. Norden visited in 1737 and 1738, Pococke in 1738, Bruce in 1769 and Browne in 1792 and 1793 (Wikipedia list, without inline sources).

In 1798 Napoleon invaded Egypt with a Commission des Sciences et des Arts of scholars and artists. The artist Dominique Vivant Denon was the only artist with the French army in Upper Egypt from December 1798 to February 1799, and his Karnak drawings were among the first accurate European records. Scholars of the commission also surveyed it. Their results appeared as the Description de l'Égypte in stages from 1809 (some sources say 1808) to 1828. Through all of this, travellers and collectors also removed objects.
Sources: Wikipedia "History of the Karnak Temple complex" (pointer, citing Blyth 2006); Luxor Living Lab (heritage site); Linda Hall Library, "Napoleon and the Scientific Expedition to Egypt"; Bibliothèque nationale de France, "The Description of Egypt"; Sullivan, UEE (2010).

## Auguste Mariette
Kind: person
About: auguste-mariette, antiquities-service
Auguste Mariette (1821 to 1881) was a French Egyptologist. In 1858 the viceroy of Egypt, Said Pasha, appointed him head of a new antiquities service, based first in Bulaq in Cairo. Sources disagree slightly on the formal date (1858 in most; Wikipedia's article on the Supreme Council also gives 1859 once). The Egyptian Museum's own history page says 1858 and describes the post as director of a new museum at Bulaq.

The service's job was to stop the sale and destruction of antiquities and to control digging, and Mariette's excavations ran at many sites, Karnak among them. I did not find a source describing his Karnak work beyond a bibliographic mention. From general knowledge (verify), he found the Poetical Stela of Thutmose III at Karnak in 1858.

Mariette built a French-led tradition. French directors, Gaston Maspero among them, led the service until 1952. The Karnak works were then run by a dedicated commission set up in 1895, the "Direction des Travaux de Karnak", whose heads were Georges Legrain and later Henri Chevrier.
Sources: Egyptian Museum Cairo, "History of the Egyptian Museum" (egyptianmuseumcairo.eg); Wikipedia "Supreme Council of Antiquities" (pointer); Sullivan, UEE (2010), bibliographic note on the 1895 commission; general knowledge for the Poetical Stela and French directors (verify).

## Who has run Karnak: the custody chronology
Kind: reference
About: antiquities-service, cfeetk
Karnak has never had a private owner in the Western sense: it has always belonged to the state, the god's estate or the crown. In ancient times the king was its patron and the High Priest of Amun its manager, and for long periods the High Priests or the God's Wives held real control (see the Estate and priest-king sections). After the temple closed it was abandoned, then used as a church and a village, then visited and robbed by travellers.

Since 1858 it has been in the care of the Egyptian state's antiquities authority, whose name has changed. The Department or Service of Antiquities began in 1858 under Mariette. It became the Egyptian Antiquities Organisation in 1971, and in 1994 the Supreme Council of Antiquities, under the Ministry of Culture. In 2011 it passed to an independent Ministry of State for Antiquities (Affairs). The ministry was merged into tourism in 2019 as the Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities, though the Supreme Council of Antiquities still handles the sites.

Sources disagree about the last step. A search result describing the ministry says it was merged with tourism in December 2019. Wikipedia's page on the Supreme Council instead says 2022 for a Ministry of Antiquities and Tourism. I use 2019 and mark it as not confirmed from an official page, as the official site was unavailable. The Franco-Egyptian centre (CFEETK) has been the research and restoration partner since 1967.
Sources: Wikipedia "Supreme Council of Antiquities" (pointer; 1858, 1971, 1994, 2011); egymonuments.gov.eg (not retrievable); search summary of MoTA history for 2019; Sullivan, UEE (2010).

## Excavation and conservation since 1858
Kind: reference
About: antiquities-service, cfeetk, unesco-listing, cachette, white-chapel-rebuilt, hypostyle-collapse
Mariette's service took charge of Karnak in 1858 (I found no source detailing his clearance there). In 1895 a permanent commission, the Direction des Travaux de Karnak, was created for its study and conservation, with Georges Legrain in charge from 1895. He is remembered for rebuilding the Hypostyle Hall columns after the collapse of 3 October 1899 (finished in May 1902), for finding the Cachette of buried statues between 1903 and 1905, and for finding Tutankhamun's Restoration Stela in 1905. His 1929 book "Les temples de Karnak" summarises the work.

Henri Chevrier, an architect, directed the works from 1926. He rebuilt the White Chapel of Senusret I from reused blocks, between 1927 and 1938, and his excavation reports appeared in the Annales du Service des Antiquités in the 1940s and 1950s.

Since 1967 the work has been run jointly by Egypt and France through the Centre Franco-Égyptien d'Étude des Temples de Karnak (CFEETK, "Centre franco-égyptien"), under the CNRS and Egypt's antiquities authority. It records, studies and restores the temples and publishes the "Cahiers de Karnak". Its excavations since the late 1990s, by Luc Gabolde, refocused interest on Karnak's earliest periods. The Egyptian authority's own teams have also dug at Karnak, notably the western revetment wall and quay.

In 1979 UNESCO inscribed "Ancient Thebes with its Necropolis" on the World Heritage List (number 87), under criteria (i), (iii) and (vi). It covers Karnak and Luxor on the east bank and the necropolis on the west. UNESCO's committee has since asked for a management plan and impact assessments for new visitor developments.
Sources: Sullivan, UEE (2010), bibliographic note; Digital Karnak PDF (2010); UNESCO World Heritage Centre, list 87 (via search summaries; the page itself returned an error); CFEETK website (not retrievable); dates for the White Chapel from the site's existing dates file.
