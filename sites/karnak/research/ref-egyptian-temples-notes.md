## The plan of an Egyptian temple
Kind: reference
About: temple-plan, temple-orientation
A New Kingdom temple was built as a sequence, one space after another along a single axis. You entered through a pylon gateway, crossed an open sunlit court, passed into a hypostyle hall (a hall whose roof is held up by many columns), then through smaller offering halls to the sanctuary, where the god's statue lived. Later temples added a great many side rooms, storerooms and chapels, but the central line stayed the same.

Two tricks made the journey feel like a descent into mystery. The floor rose slightly towards the back, and the roof dropped, so each room was lower and narrower than the one before. Windows were tiny slits high in the walls, so the light faded step by step. The sanctuary itself was small and nearly dark, with a stone or wooden shrine for the image.

The Egyptians read the whole building as a model of creation. Their myths said the world began as a mound of earth rising from the dark waters of Nun, and the sanctuary stood for that first mound. The columns of the halls were stone marsh plants, papyrus and lotus, with ceilings painted as the star-filled sky and floors and wall-bases decorated with water and plants. Walking inwards, you went back towards the moment of creation (Wilkinson).

Orientation was usually practical and religious at once. Most temples were laid out in relation to the Nile, so that the god faced the river, which is why some run east to west and others north to south depending on the local river bend. There are also claims of astronomical alignment, such as to the midwinter sunrise or to particular stars. A few are well supported, but many are disputed or rest on small samples, and a long axis can line up with something by chance. Treat any single claimed alignment as a possibility rather than a proven design intention.
Sources: Wilkinson, The Complete Temples of Ancient Egypt (2000), introduction and ch. 1; Shafer (ed.), Temples of Ancient Egypt (1997), esp. the chapter by Arnold and Shafer; Met Museum, Heilbrunn Timeline, "Temples of Ancient Egypt"; UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology, "Temples, Egyptian" (not rechecked online).

## Pylons, flagpoles and gateways
Kind: reference
About: pylon-gateway
A pylon is the monumental gateway at the front of an Egyptian temple: two massive towers with sloping sides, linked by a lower doorway between them, topped by a hollow-curved cornice. "Pylon" is the Greek word for a gateway. The towers were built of stone faces over a core of rubble or brick, and the sloping sides (called a batter) helped them stand. The huge doors were of timber, often cedar from Lebanon, and covered in metal.

The shape is symbolic. The hieroglyph for "horizon" (akhet) shows two hills with the sun rising between them, and a pylon is the same picture in stone. The temple was where the sun god's daily rebirth was acted out, so entering through the pylon meant passing through the horizon.

On the front of the towers there are usually tall vertical slots, the flagstaff niches. Tall timber masts were fixed in them, held steady by clamps through openings higher up in the masonry, and flew long pennants. The masts were often of cedar and were said in inscriptions to be tipped with metal, such as electrum. Reconstruction drawings show them as strikingly tall, but no mast survives, and the pennants themselves are only known from carvings.

The pylon faces were also advertising hoardings. The usual image is the king grasping a bunch of enemies by the hair and about to strike them down in front of a god. It is an ancient symbol of royal power maintaining order (ma'at) against chaos rather than a record of one battle. The best preserved large examples are of the Ptolemaic period, such as Edfu, where the towers stand about 36 metres high. Some pylons were never finished: stepped ramps of mud brick built up against them for the builders were sometimes left in place.
Sources: Wilkinson (2000), ch. 1; Shafer (ed.) (1997); Met Museum, "Temples of Ancient Egypt" essay; Edfu height from Wilkinson (check).

## Columns and capitals
Kind: reference
About: temple-columns
Egyptian columns were stone versions of plants. A papyrus-bundle column has a shaft made of stems tied together, ringed with bands near the top, and a capital shaped either as a closed bud (a "bundle" or closed papyrus capital) or as the opened flower, the flaring open papyrus or "campaniform" capital. Lotus capitals were similar, and palm columns used a crown of leaves, a form known from the Old Kingdom onwards. Hathor columns are topped by the face of the goddess, with cow ears, usually above a sistrum-shaped block. They are common in temples of Hathor and in shrines in her honour. Osirid pillars have a statue of the king as Osiris standing against the front.

Ptolemaic and Roman builders developed the composite capital, in which many different plants, such as palm, lotus, papyrus and vines, are combined in one crown, and no two capitals in the same hall need be alike.

Why plants? Partly because of the creation myth: a temple was the first marshy island at the start of time, so its columns were the first reeds. Partly because early builders really did bundle reeds and logs and plaster them with mud, and the stone forms kept the memory of them. Painted details such as bands and leaf-tips were meant to look like living plants.

For an engineer, the interesting thing is the spacing. Stone is excellent under squeezing but weak when bent, so long beams snap. The roof slabs of an Egyptian temple could only span a few metres, which is why the columns stand so close together, forming a forest. Shafts were built up from stacked drums (cylindrical sections) and decorated after they were in place. The central columns of a hypostyle hall were often taller than the side ones, so the roof stepped up, leaving space for clerestory windows to let in a little light.
Sources: Wilkinson (2000), ch. 1 and glossary; Arnold, Building in Egypt: Pharaonic Stone Masonry (1991); Met Museum, Heilbrunn Timeline; UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology, "Architecture (Temples)" (check).

## The daily temple ritual
Kind: reference
About: daily-ritual
The god in an Egyptian temple was treated like a living ruler who needed care three times a day, morning, noon and evening. The main rite was in the morning. Before dawn the priests purified themselves, and the officiant went to the shrine bearing a torch and incense. He broke a clay seal, drew back the bolt and opened the doors, and so came face to face with the image of the god. Texts compare this with the sun rising.

The ritual then followed the stages of a human's morning. The god was greeted with hymns and incense, and given water and fresh clothing. The old garments were removed. The statue was washed, anointed with oil, and dressed in strips of linen in several colours. It had make-up applied, and collars and ornaments were put on. Food and drink were then presented: bread, beer, meat, fowl, vegetables, fruit and wine. The god was believed to take in the essence of the food, as the offerings were not eaten. After the rite the offerings were re-used. They were presented at statues of other gods, then at the temple's chapels for the dead, and finally shared among the priests and staff as part of their pay. The sanctuary was swept clean, the priest walking backwards and brushing out his footprints, and the shrine was sealed again.

At night there was a closing rite, and the temple was shut until the morning. Fuller versions of the ritual, as in the Abydos temple of Sety I (reigned 1294 to 1279 BC in Shaw's chronology), are carved on walls, and ritual papyri survive from Thebes, such as Papyrus Berlin 3055. The surviving versions differ in detail from place to place.

Most people never saw this. The sanctuary was open only to the priests on duty and the king, and the king was in theory the one who performed every ritual.
Sources: Wilkinson (2000), ch. 2; Sauneron, The Priests of Ancient Egypt (rev. ed. 2000); David, A Guide to Religious Ritual at Abydos (1981); Shaw (ed.), The Oxford History of Ancient Egypt (2000) for dates.

## Priests and temple staff
Kind: reference
About: temple-priesthood
The head of a major temple was called the first prophet or high priest, literally "first servant of the god". Under him were second, third and fourth prophets, and a large staff. Most priests were wab priests ("pure ones"), who handled sacred objects, carried the barks, made offerings and did everyday tasks. Lector priests ("carrying the ritual book") read out the spoken parts of rituals and magic texts. Other staff included scribes, astronomers or "hour-priests" who watched the sky to fix the times of ritual, singers, musicians, doorkeepers, butchers, bakers, and craftsmen.

For much of Egyptian history, most priests were not full-time. They were divided into four groups called phyles (the Greek word for tribes), each serving a month in turn and then returning to ordinary life for three. Under Ptolemy III, a decree issued in 238 BC at Canopus added a fifth phyle. Many part-time priests were local officials, scribes or craftsmen, and the title was often inherited. A smaller body of full-time priests ran the temples daily.

Purity was essential. Priests washed in the sacred lake or a pool, two or three times a day and sometimes at night too. They shaved their heads and bodies, and later Greek writers say they avoided wool, and some foods such as fish, and were circumcised. Greek writers are not always trustworthy on these details. Entry to the inner areas required ritual cleanliness, and those who were unclean stayed away.

The role of "God's Wife of Amun" became important from the New Kingdom. In the Third Intermediate and Late Periods it was held by a royal woman who in practice was one of the most powerful priests in Thebes, and she was sometimes shown performing temple rites herself, a role usually reserved for the king.
Sources: Sauneron, The Priests of Ancient Egypt (2000); Wilkinson (2000), ch. 2; UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology, "Priests" and "Temple Personnel"; Canopus decree from Wikipedia/Wilkinson (check); Shaw (2000).

## Bark processions and festivals
Kind: reference
About: bark-processions, opet-festival, valley-festival, oracle-processions
Egyptian gods did not usually leave their temples, except at festivals. On those days the image was lifted out and placed in a bark shrine: a gilded wooden boat with a canopy, carried by priests on poles. A great god might have a river barge as well. The route was marked by bark stations, small chapels or kiosks where the priests could rest the bark, make offerings and recite texts. Chapels beside avenues of sphinxes are best seen as bark stations.

The Opet Festival at Thebes was held in the second month of the flood season, when the river was high. The barks of Amun, Mut and Khonsu journeyed from the Karnak temple to Luxor Temple, about 2.5 kilometres, in a procession with troops, dancers, musicians and crowds, and at Luxor the king's divine nature was renewed. Records suggest it lasted about 11 days in the early New Kingdom, 24 days in the reign of Ramesses III and then 27 days later. These figures come from Wilkinson and others, and calendars are not complete.

In the Beautiful Festival of the Valley, held in the second month of summer at the new moon, Amun's bark crossed the Nile to the west bank, visiting the mortuary temples of the kings and the cemetery. Families went to their relatives' tombs, with food, flowers and drink, and feasted through the night.

Festivals were also when ordinary people met the god. They could watch the procession, and the bark could be asked questions: yes or no oracles were given by the bark moving or seeming to press down on the bearers. Some temples had a "hearing ear" place, a chapel or niche on the outer wall where petitions could be heard by a god in a way the public could use. Villagers at Deir el-Medina asked oracle questions about everyday disputes.
Sources: Wilkinson (2000), ch. 2; Bell, "Luxor Temple and the Cult of the Royal Ka", Journal of Near Eastern Studies 44 (1985); Schott, Das schöne Fest vom Wüstentale (1952); Kruchten and others on oracles, Teeter, Religion and Ritual in Ancient Egypt (2011); UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology, "Festivals" (check durations).

## Temple estates and the economy
Kind: reference
About: temple-estates
A big Egyptian temple was an economic engine as well as a place of worship. Kings gave land, livestock, workshops and people to the gods to pay for their cult, often tax-free. Temple lands, scattered along the Nile Valley and in the Delta, produced grain, flax, vegetables, wine, honey, cattle and fowl. Produce was stored in granaries and magazines (storerooms) around the temple, many of mud brick and some vaulted. The temple also ran bakeries, breweries, butchers, weaving workshops, scribal offices and metalworking, and it controlled mines and quarries and ships.

Priests were paid in part from the leftover offerings, which were distributed according to rank. Temples employed priests, farmers, herdsmen, fishermen, scribes, builders and craftsmen, and some gave loans of grain.

Two papyri are central. Papyrus Harris I (British Museum EA 9999), from the end of the reign of Ramesses III (1184 to 1153 BC in Shaw's chronology), lists gifts to the temples. The Wilbour Papyrus (Brooklyn Museum), from year 4 of Ramesses V, is a land survey of part of Middle Egypt that lists the holdings of temples and of the people who farmed them.

Numbers need care. Writers often quote that the temples of Amun held about four-fifths of the temple land listed in Papyrus Harris, and around 80,000 dependent people, but the figures differ between editions and scholars, and I have not checked them against the primary publications. Estimates of the temples' share of all cultivable land in Egypt range widely, from about a tenth to a third depending on the period and the method. The honest summary is that temples were very large landowners and employers, and the largest of them, Amun at Thebes, was a major power in the state, but exact percentages are uncertain.
Sources: Wilkinson (2000), ch. 2; Haring, Divine Households (1997); Gardiner, The Wilbour Papyrus (1941-1952); Grandet, Le Papyrus Harris I (1994); Janssen on economy, in UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology (check); Shaw (2000). Figures flagged as unchecked.

## Reading the walls: relief, registers and colour
Kind: reference
About: temple-decoration
Almost every surface of a temple was carved and painted. There are two main kinds of relief. In raised relief the background is cut away, leaving the figures standing out from the wall. This takes a lot of work, but gives fine detail, so it was preferred indoors. In sunk (incised) relief the outline is cut into the surface and the figures are modelled within it. It is quicker and in strong sunlight on exterior walls it casts a strong shadow that makes the figure legible, so it is common outside.

Subjects follow the building's logic. Outer walls and pylons show the king as victorious warrior, smiting enemies, making the military campaigns and defending order. Inner walls show rituals: the king offering to the gods, who give him life and power in return. The king is shown as the main actor everywhere even though priests did the work. Scenes are laid out in horizontal bands called registers, usually read from the bottom up or following the direction in which the figures face. Hieroglyphs face the same way, towards the start of each line.

Names of kings appear in cartouches, an oval loop of rope that encloses the royal name. From the Middle Kingdom a king had two, the throne name and the birth name. Cartouches were often re-carved by later kings, which is one reason why dating a wall can be difficult.

The walls were once bright. Pigments such as Egyptian blue (a synthetic copper-silicate), ochres for red and yellow, malachite or later copper-based greens, white gypsum or huntite and black carbon were applied to plaster. Colour survives best where it was sheltered, such as under roofs, in hall ceilings and in rooms sealed up later, which can be strikingly fresh. Carvers laid out figures on a grid of squares, in red ink, and corrections were marked in black.
Sources: Wilkinson (2000), ch. 1 to 2; Robins, Proportion and Style in Ancient Egyptian Art (1994); Lucas and Harris, Ancient Egyptian Materials and Industries (1962); Met Museum, "Egyptian Art" essays.

## Sacred lakes, nilometers and birth houses
Kind: reference
About: temple-lake, nilometer, mammisi
Many temples had a sacred lake, a rectangular pool lined with stone, often with steps going down to the water. It stood for the primeval waters of Nun from which the world was born. Priests used it to purify themselves before duties, water for libations was drawn from it, and some temples kept sacred geese there. At festivals it was used for ritual boat processions. The lake was usually fed by groundwater, which rose and fell with the Nile.

A nilometer measured the height of the annual flood. It could be a staircase going down into the river or a well with marked steps, and the reading helped to predict the harvest and so set the tax. Nilometers were maintained at temples including Elephantine, Edfu and Philae, and the Nile's rise was linked to the gods. Temple priests often recorded the levels.

A mammisi, a Greek-derived modern term, means a birth house. It was a small separate building in a temple precinct, common from the Thirtieth Dynasty and above all in Ptolemaic and Roman times. It celebrated the divine birth of the child-god of the local triad, such as Horus the Child at Edfu or Dendera. Its walls show the goddess giving birth, with attendants and other gods, and it also affirmed the king's divine status by suggesting that he, too, was divinely born. Examples survive at Dendera, Edfu, Philae and elsewhere. A mammisi at Armant built for Cleopatra VII, apparently to celebrate Caesarion, was demolished in the 19th century and survives only in drawings.
Sources: Wilkinson (2000), ch. 1 and 4; Daumas, Les mammisis des temples égyptiens (1958); UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology, "Nilometer" and "Mammisi" (check); Shafer (ed.) (1997).

## Temples after the pharaohs: Ptolemies, Romans and churches
Kind: reference
About: ptolemaic-temples, theodosian-decrees, philae-closure, temples-as-churches
After Alexander the Great conquered Egypt in 332 BC, the Ptolemaic kings (305 to 30 BC in Shaw's scheme) were Greek-speaking in court life, but presented themselves as pharaohs and built on a huge scale. Edfu was begun in 237 BC and finished in 57 BC, and is the best preserved large temple in Egypt. At Dendera the great temple of Hathor was begun in the first century BC and decorated under the Roman emperors, and Philae's Isis temple was built over centuries. After Augustus took Egypt in 30 BC, emperors continued to be carved as pharaohs, though they seldom visited.

Temples were not shut at a single moment. Pagan sacrifice was banned by Christian emperors in stages, and Theodosius I issued laws in 391 and 392 forbidding sacrifice and the worship of the old gods, including in Egypt. The Serapeum in Alexandria was destroyed about 391. But in the south, temples such as Philae continued to receive worship. The last dated hieroglyphic inscription is at Philae, in 394 AD, and the last in Demotic, in 452 AD. The historian Procopius says that Justinian had the Philae temples closed by his general Narses, which is usually dated between 535 and 537, though the date is not fixed.

Many temples became churches or monasteries, with crosses cut into the walls and scenes plastered over, which helped to preserve some paintings. Philae's Isis temple was converted to a church of St Stephen. At Luxor, a mosque still stands in the temple, in a continuous tradition of reuse. Elsewhere temples were quarried for stone and lime.
Sources: Wilkinson (2000); Frankfurter, Religion in Roman Egypt (1998); Bagnall, Egypt in Late Antiquity (1993); Procopius, Wars 1.19; Dijkstra, Philae and the End of Ancient Egyptian Religion (2008); Shaw (2000).
