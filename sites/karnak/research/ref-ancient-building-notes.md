## Stone types and quarries of ancient Egypt
Kind: reference
About: gebel-silsila-quarries, colossi-of-memnon
Egyptian builders used the stone that lay nearest, and they knew their rocks well. Limestone was the stone of the Old and Middle Kingdoms. The fine white limestone of Tura, across the Nile from modern Cairo, cased the Giza pyramids, while the hills beside Thebes gave a poorer, flintier limestone for local work. Sandstone (Nubian sandstone) is the stone of most New Kingdom and later temples in the south. The great source was Gebel el-Silsila, where the river squeezes between sandstone cliffs about 65 kilometres north of Aswan. Granite and granodiorite, the red, pink and grey stones of obelisks, doorways and statues, came from the Aswan area at the First Cataract. Quartzite, a very hard sandstone, came from Gebel el-Ahmar near modern Cairo and from other desert sites; Klemm and Klemm trace the two Colossi of Memnon, each around 700 tonnes, to Gebel el-Ahmar, and so about 675 kilometres of river travel to Thebes. Egyptian alabaster (really banded calcite or travertine) came from the Hatnub quarry in the Eastern Desert, and was used for vessels, shrines and sacred barque chapels.

Why sandstone? The sources infer rather than state it. Sandstone is softer than granite and splits cleanly along its beds into big, even blocks. It was also available all along a stretch of cliff that came right down to the river, so blocks could be slid straight onto barges. The scale of New Kingdom temples was far greater than anything cut from the local Theban limestone, which is flawed and cracked. Gebel el-Silsila was worked on a large scale from about 1500 BC and was still quarried in Roman times.

River transport mattered more than anything else. Blocks went by barge, and the Nile's flood (roughly July to October) lifted boats close to building sites by canal.
Sources: Klemm and Klemm, Stones and Quarries in Ancient Egypt (2008); Nicholson and Shaw (eds), Ancient Egyptian Materials and Technology (2000), chapter on stone (Aston, Harrell and Shaw); Arnold, Building in Egypt (1991); UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology, "Quarrying and Mining" and "Stone".

## How Egyptian quarrymen cut stone
Kind: reference
About: gebel-silsila-quarries, unfinished-obelisk
Soft stone (limestone, sandstone, alabaster) was cut with copper chisels, picks and wooden mallets. At Gebel el-Silsila and similar quarries the workmen first cut narrow trenches round the block, wide enough for a crouching man, then undercut it from the side and levered or wedged it free. They left blocks roughly dressed at the quarry, often with only the top and bottom faces smoothed, so the outside could be finished once the stone was in place. Visitors can spot the marks: parallel diagonal chisel strokes on quarry walls, rows of rectangular scars where blocks were removed, and sometimes the roughed-out shapes of blocks still part-attached.

Hard stone (granite, granodiorite, quartzite, basalt) was beyond copper and bronze tools. Workers pounded it with balls of harder rock, usually dolerite, a dark, tough stone. This crushed the granite surface into powder bit by bit. Engelbach, who studied Aswan in the early twentieth century, described the pounding trenches round the Unfinished Obelisk. It was slow, noisy work and the dust was heavy.

Wedges are debated. Rows of small slots along the edge of a cut block are often said to have held wooden wedges, soaked with water so that they swelled and split the rock. It is a neat idea but unproven. Arnold notes that wedge slots in Aswan granite become common only in the Late and Ptolemaic periods, when metal wedges are likely, and that the swelling-wood method has not been demonstrated for earlier periods. Treat it as a theory.
Sources: Arnold, Building in Egypt (1991), chapter on quarrying; Clarke and Engelbach, Ancient Egyptian Construction and Architecture (1930); Engelbach, The Aswan Obelisk (1922); Klemm and Klemm (2008); Nicholson and Shaw (2000).

## Moving heavy blocks: sledges, wet sand and barges
Kind: reference
About: wet-sand-study, djehutihotep-painting, merer-diary
Egyptians had no wheeled carts for heavy loads (the wheel was used for chariots from about 1600 BC). Heavy stone went on wooden sledges, dragged by teams on ropes over prepared tracks of clay, timber or sand.

A famous painting in the tomb of Djehutihotep at Deir el-Bersha (Middle Kingdom, about 1870 BC) shows a colossal statue of him, about 6.5 metres tall and estimated at about 58 tonnes, on a sledge hauled by 172 men. A man at the front pours liquid from a vessel onto the sand in front of the sledge. In 2014 a team led by Daniel Bonn at the University of Amsterdam (Fall and others, Physical Review Letters 112) tested this. Dry sand piles up in a heap in front of a sledge, but sand wetted to the right amount, a few per cent of water by volume, becomes firmer, so the sledge rides on a stiff surface. Pulling force fell by about half, as the friction coefficient dropped from roughly 0.5 to 0.3. On my arithmetic, 58 tonnes at 0.3 needs about 170 kilonewtons, or roughly 1 kilonewton (100 kilograms of pull) per man for 172 men. The study shows the method works. It does not prove it was the only method.

Water carried most of the load. Papyri found at Wadi al-Jarf in 2013 include the diary of an official, Merer, who recorded his crew shipping Tura limestone for Khufu's pyramid by boat about 2560 BC. Giza's workmanship, taken as a whole, implies an enormous workforce. A common figure of 2.3 million blocks in about 20 years means roughly 300 blocks laid each working day (Lehner). Estimates of workforce size differ widely, from a few thousand to tens of thousands.
Sources: Fall and others, "Sliding Friction on Wet and Dry Sand", Physical Review Letters 112 (2014); Newberry, El Bersheh (1895) for the statue estimate; Tallet, "The Red Sea Scrolls" and Lehner, The Complete Pyramids (1997); Arnold (1991); Clarke and Engelbach (1930).

## Construction ramps and how pyramids and pylons rose
Kind: reference
About: builders-ramp, hatnub-ramp-find, internal-ramp-theory
Without cranes or pulleys, Egyptians lifted stone by dragging it up slopes. Ramps of mud brick, rubble and earth were the standard tool, and they are plain in the record: unfinished temples, tomb paintings and quarries all show them.

For pyramids, four theories compete. A single straight ramp is simple, but one reaching 146 metres, the Great Pyramid's original height, would need a volume close to that of the pyramid itself. A zigzag ramp on one face and a spiral ramp winding round the sides both cut the volume but obscure the corners, which must be checked as they rise. A fourth idea, by the architect Jean-Pierre Houdin (2007), puts a ramp inside the pyramid; it is debated and has not been demonstrated. Probably several methods were combined.

The best direct evidence is rare. In 2018 a French and British team led by Yannis Gourdon (IFAO) announced a ramp system at the Hatnub alabaster quarry, dated to about the reign of Khufu. A central ramp rises at about 20 per cent (a slope near one in five) between two staircases lined with post holes. The usual reading is that ropes round posts let teams drag sledges up a steeper slope than friction normally allows, and gave them a way to hold the load. At Karnak, a surviving mud-brick ramp behind the First Pylon shows the temple method in use; a site section covers it.

Ramps could be dismantled, which is why few remain. This is also why finishing the walls "from the top down" was normal.
Sources: Arnold (1991), chapter on ramps; Lehner, The Complete Pyramids (1997); Clarke and Engelbach (1930); IFAO and Hatnub project reports on Hatnub 2018 (Gourdon, Enmarch); Houdin, "Khufu: The Secrets Behind the Building of the Great Pyramid" (2006); UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology.

## Foundations: why temples fall down
Kind: reference
About: hypostyle-collapse
Egyptian temples sit on the Nile floodplain. The ground is silt and sand over a water table that rises and falls with the river. Where the builders could, they cut to bedrock. In many temples they did not: they dug a trench, packed it with sand or rubble and laid shallow courses of stone on top. Sand spreads the load and absorbs shocks, so it is a reasonable foundation for a moderately heavy building, but it needs to stay put.

Trouble starts with water. Groundwater carries salt up through the lowest stones. As it evaporates, salt crystals grow in the pores and split the stone, a process called salt weathering. This is the main reason many temple bases crumble. Reused small blocks, packed in as filler, are weak. Over time the foundation sinks unevenly and columns lean. In 1899 eleven columns of the Great Hypostyle Hall at Karnak fell in a chain reaction. Explanations combine weak reused blocks, salt-laden groundwater and, in one view, disturbance from earlier trenches. The detail is disputed; site sections cover it.

The modern cure is to control water. Karnak and other Theban temples have had drainage and dewatering schemes in recent decades, using pumps and drains to lower and manage the water table (exact dates and layouts are not given here). The Aswan High Dam (completed 1970) ended the annual flood, so the water table now stays high all year round, which worsens the salt problem.
Sources: Nicholson and Shaw (2000); Arnold (1991), chapter on foundations; UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology, "Temple Foundations"; general knowledge for the Aswan High Dam; knowledge/karnak/notes.md for the 1899 collapse.

## Columns, architraves and roofs: why stone limits spans
Kind: reference
About: dieter-arnold
Egyptian temples are post-and-lintel buildings: columns carry horizontal beams (architraves), and roof slabs rest across the beams. There are no true arches in stone temples. The reason is that stone is strong when squeezed but weak when pulled. A beam on two supports sags slightly, which puts the underside in tension. Stone fails there, and cracks begin from the underside.

Bending stress grows with the square of the span, and falls with the depth of the beam. Double the span and the stress quadruples. For sandstone, with a tensile strength of only a few megapascals, a span of about 4 to 6 metres is comfortable. The largest monolithic stone beams, mostly granite, run a little longer, such as the ceiling beams of the King's Chamber in the Great Pyramid, which span about 5.2 metres. This is why Egyptian halls are packed with columns: the "forest" at Karnak is structural, not decorative. Where the nave needed more width, builders used several blocks side by side, even stacked in two layers.

Columns were built in drums or half-drums, usually carved to the final profile after assembly. A plumb line centred each drum. The roof was laid on top and the whole structure was then dressed from the top down once the earth packing and ramps were removed. That the earth was heaped round the columns and used as a working platform is a theory, supported by evidence at Karnak but not shown everywhere. Taller central columns allowed a raised roof with stone window grilles, the clerestory, that let in a little light.
Sources: Arnold (1991), chapters on columns and roofs; Clarke and Engelbach (1930); Nicholson and Shaw (2000); Lehner, The Complete Pyramids (1997) for the King's Chamber; Digital Karnak, Sullivan, "Construction Methods and Building Materials".

## Walls and pylons: stone skin, rubble heart
Kind: reference
About: dieter-arnold
A temple pylon, the great gateway of two sloping towers, looks like a solid mass of stone. It is mostly not. The outer faces were built of dressed blocks with carefully bonded courses, but the interior was often filled with rubble, sand, chips or reused blocks. This saved quarrying and carving, and the heavy core held the thinner face in place.

The faces were battered, meaning they lean inward as they rise, usually by a few degrees. A battered face is much more stable than a vertical one, and it lowers the centre of gravity of the whole tower. It also gives the Egyptian look, with a torus roll at the corners and a cavetto cornice at the top. The pylon faces carried deep recesses for flagpoles, held by timber masts. Joints were often tied with wooden dovetail cramps set across them, though their holes are all that survive in most places.

Reused fill explains why Karnak has given up so many treasures. When Amenhotep III built the Third Pylon, about 1380 BC, he dismantled earlier chapels and used the blocks as filler. Archaeologists such as Legrain and Chevrier took them out in the early twentieth century and the White Chapel and the Red Chapel were then reassembled for the Open Air Museum. Small blocks called talatat, carved for Akhenaten's temples, were reused in the same way in later pylons. Sources describe this as demolition and reuse; they do not generally call it deliberate hiding.
Sources: Arnold (1991), chapters on walls and pylons; Clarke and Engelbach (1930); Nicholson and Shaw (2000); knowledge/karnak/notes.md for the Karnak chapels (Digital Karnak, Open Air Museum sections).

## Obelisks and the physics of a very long stone
Kind: reference
About: unfinished-obelisk, engelbach-obelisk-study
An obelisk is a single tapering granite shaft, usually with a pointed tip, the pyramidion. Standing ones run from a few metres to about 30 metres, and the largest known would have been far bigger: the Unfinished Obelisk at Aswan is about 42 metres long and about 1,100 to 1,200 tonnes, which is more than any block Egyptians are known to have moved. Engelbach measured it in 1922 (41.75 metres). The shaft cracked while still being cut out, and the work was abandoned. A site section covers quarrying and raising, and the theories.

The physics is unforgiving. Granite weighs about 2.7 tonnes per cubic metre. A shaft of about 30 metres with a base about 2.5 metres across weighs several hundred tonnes. Its centre of gravity lies about 40 per cent of the way up from the base, which is the point at which it balances. If a shaft this long is supported only at the ends, it behaves like a beam. My rough calculation for a 30 metre shaft about 2.5 metres wide gives a peak bending stress of about 7 megapascals from its own weight alone. Granite's tensile strength is only about 5 to 15 megapascals, and a hidden flaw lowers it. So a lifted obelisk has to be supported along its length, on sledges or cradles, and any hidden crack is a danger.

Cracks are the reason for the Unfinished Obelisk's failure. The quarrymen cut a trench around it and found flaws as they went. Ancient workers, like modern ones, would test the stone, but this one failed too late.
Sources: Engelbach, The Aswan Obelisk (1922); Klemm and Klemm (2008); Arnold (1991); knowledge/karnak/notes.md, "Quarrying, moving and raising an obelisk"; stress figure is my own beam calculation from standard material properties.

## Mud brick: the everyday building material
Kind: reference
About: rekhmire-brickmaking
Most ancient Egyptian buildings were mud brick. Houses, palaces, town walls, fortresses and the huge enclosure walls of temples were brick; only temples and tombs were stone. Bricks were made from Nile silt mixed with water and often chopped straw or sand, pressed in a wooden mould and left to dry in the sun for several days. A tomb painting of the vizier Rekhmire (about 1450 BC) shows the whole process: men carrying water, mixing the mud, moulding bricks and stacking them.

Sizes changed with time and place. Typical bricks were about 35 to 40 centimetres long and 15 to 20 centimetres wide, with a thickness of 10 to 15 centimetres. On my estimate a brick this size weighs 15 to 20 kilograms, a good load for one worker. Many New Kingdom bricks were stamped with a royal name, especially those from temples; the stamp helped record who ordered them.

The most striking feature appears in huge enclosure walls of the Late Period, such as those of Nectanebo I at Karnak (380 to 362 BC). Courses were laid in alternating curves, concave then convex, a style called pan-bedding or wavy bedding. Why is debated. Suggested reasons include resistance to settlement or earthquakes, saving mortar and bricks, giving flexibility when walls were long, and a symbolic link to the primeval waters. No source settles it.

Mud brick lasts well in dry air, but not in damp ground or rain. This is why surviving brick ramps, such as the one behind Karnak's First Pylon, are rare and fragile.
Sources: Nicholson and Shaw (2000), chapter on mud brick (Spencer); Arnold (1991); Clarke and Engelbach (1930); Spencer, Brick Architecture in Ancient Egypt (1979); Davies, The Tomb of Rekh-mi-Re (1943).

## Surveying and setting out
Kind: reference
About: turin-plan-ramesses-iv, flinders-petrie
Egyptians measured in cubits. The royal cubit was about 52.3 centimetres, divided into 7 palms, each of 4 fingers (28 fingers in all). A shorter common cubit was about 45 centimetres (6 palms). Rods of wood and stone marked with these units survive.

The foundation ceremony was called "stretching the cord". A king and the goddess Seshat are shown driving pegs and stretching a cord to mark the corners, as in reliefs at Edfu and Dendera. Cords and set squares laid out right angles. A popular idea is that rope stretchers made right angles with a 3, 4, 5 triangle. No Egyptian text says so, so it is a modern theory.

Alignment was astronomical. The Great Pyramid's sides face the cardinal points within a few minutes of arc. Egyptians used the merkhet, a plumb-bob sighting tool, and the bay, a palm rib with a sighting slit, to line up on stars. Theories for how north was found include sighting on two circumpolar stars and using shadows of the sun; none is proved. The level base is also remarkable, flat within a couple of centimetres. Lehner suggests that water-filled trenches round the base worked as a level. It is a theory.

Planning evidence is real. Unfinished walls show guide lines and squared grids in red ochre, used for drawing figures to proportion. Ostraca (pot and limestone flakes) carry plans and sketches. The Turin papyrus shows the plan of the tomb of Ramesses IV (about 1150 BC), with measurements.
Sources: Arnold (1991); Clarke and Engelbach (1930); Lehner, The Complete Pyramids (1997); Nicholson and Shaw (2000); UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology, "Weights and Measures" and "Architecture".

## Egyptian building tools: copper, bronze and sand
Kind: reference
About: iron-in-egypt, flinders-petrie
Egyptian masons were limited to a short list of metals. Copper, often with a little arsenic, was the working metal for chisels, adzes, axes and saws from early times. Bronze (copper with tin) became common in the Middle and New Kingdoms and gave a harder edge. Iron was known, mostly as a rarity, such as a meteoric-iron dagger from Tutankhamun's tomb, but working iron tools became common only in the Late Period, from about 650 BC. Most great temples, including nearly all at Karnak, were built with copper and bronze.

None of these can cut granite. Granite was pounded with dolerite balls (about the size of a fist or larger) and ground. For sawing and drilling, workers used abrasive sand. A copper saw or tubular drill is pressed against the stone with a slurry of quartz sand; the sand does the cutting, as the copper holds the grains. Petrie proposed jewelled teeth in the blade. Experiments by Denys Stocks in the 1980s and 1990s showed sand and copper alone work, though slowly.

Other tools were simple. Wooden mallets, levers, sledges and rollers moved stone. Ropes were of papyrus, palm fibre or halfa grass. A set square checked corners and a plumb bob checked verticals, with stone or metal weights hung on a cord. The A-frame level had a plumb line hung from the apex. Examples survive, such as the tools in the Eighteenth Dynasty tomb of Kha at Turin. Egyptian builders could do astonishing work with these. What they lacked in tools they made up in organisation and labour.
Sources: Nicholson and Shaw (2000), chapters on metals and stone; Arnold (1991); Clarke and Engelbach (1930); Stocks, Experiments in Egyptian Archaeology (2003); UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology.
