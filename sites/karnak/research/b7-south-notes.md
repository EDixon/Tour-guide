## The Sacred Lake
Kind: site
About: sacred-lake
The Sacred Lake is the large rectangular pool in the south-east of the Amun-Ra precinct. Digital Karnak says it measures 128 metres long and 83 metres wide, and credits it to Thutmose III (reigned 1479 to 1425 BC), who "records digging a new sacred lake at the Amun-Ra temple". An engineers' survey of the lake gives about 130 metres by 80 metres and a depth of about 4 metres, so the two sources agree within a few metres. The tour's older figure of 120 by 77 metres is not supported by either.

Digital Karnak says the lake may have replaced an earlier sacred lake somewhere in the southern part of the temple. It was used during religious festivals for flotillas of sacred barks, and for the daily enjoyment of the geese of Amun, the goose being one of the god's sacred animals. The lake as we see it was further modified under Taharqa (about 690 to 664 BC), but Digital Karnak says what those changes were, and how much the lake changed, is unknown.

The sides were lined with stone. The engineers' survey says the remains are clear on the west, south and north sides, where stairs down to the water survive. Around the lake stood a "vast array of functions" in Digital Karnak's words: Taharqa's edifice and a well on the north, a small chapel of Thoth to the west, administrative buildings from the Twenty-second Dynasty to the Ptolemaic period on the east, and storerooms and an aviary on the south.

What the priests did here is partly tradition. The engineers' paper says priests washed in the lake water each day before ritual. Herodotus wrote that Egyptian priests washed twice a day and twice each night, but that is a general account and not about this lake (general knowledge, verify). In ritual terms the lake stood for the primeval water from which the world was said to have risen, a link Digital Karnak makes for the deep well beside it.

Visitors single the lake out more than almost anything else on the south side. Two guides call it one of the most atmospheric corners of Karnak, especially in early morning light. There is little shade beside it.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Sacred Lake (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/sacred-lake), citing Carlotti 2001, Lauffray and others (Karnak V, X; Kemi 19), Goyon and Traunecker 1982; Elfiky and Elsaeed, "Hydraulic study for the Sacred Lake pollution at Elkarnak Temple, Egypt" (Ain Shams and Banha universities); Digital Karnak, Taharqo Edifice and Nilometer; WeRoad Karnak guide (December 2025) for the atmosphere; Shaw (2000) for reign dates; general knowledge for Herodotus.

## Where the Sacred Lake's water comes from
Kind: site
About: sacred-lake, aswan-high-dam
The lake is not fed by a channel from the Nile. It is a pit cut about 4 metres down into the ground, and its water is groundwater. An engineering study by Abdel Fattah Elfiky (Ain Shams University) and Gamal Elsaeed (Banha University) describes how that worked before the Aswan High Dam.

Each year the Nile flood soaked the valley for several months. That raised the underground water table, and the lake filled to its highest level in November. Then the dry season lowered the water table, and by July the lake became almost dry. The study says the annual change was about 0.8 metres in the water table. Because the level rose and fell, the water was refreshed every year, like a basin with a slow tide.

The High Dam (built 1960 to 1970, general knowledge) holds the flood upstream and controls the river's flow. After it, the study says, the groundwater level became almost constant all year. The lake stopped refreshing. Luxor's heat and fast evaporation then concentrated the salts, and the water turned stagnant and unpleasant, which hurt tourism. The study, written for a World Bank funded scheme, recommended two linked pumping stations, one on the Nile bank and one at the lake, to feed in fresh Nile water and draw out the old at the same rate. It also warned that the water table must be kept steady to protect the temple foundations from settlement. I could not confirm whether this was built, or what the lake's water looks like now.

The same groundwater is a danger to the stonework. Salts carried up from the water table attack sandstone, and a rising water table was part of the story of the 1899 collapse in the Hypostyle Hall (see that section). The Cachette Court nearby shows the same effect: statues were buried in a pit that filled with water, and Legrain had to lift water by hand to dig them out.

Some popular guides say the lake is linked to the Nile by underground channels. I found no evidence for that in the technical sources. The groundwater explanation is the one the engineers give.
Sources: Elfiky and Elsaeed, "Hydraulic study for the Sacred Lake pollution at Elkarnak Temple, Egypt" (undated paper on the Banha University staff site; I could not find its year or journal); Digital Karnak, Sacred Lake (which says nothing about the water source); general knowledge for the High Dam dates.

## Looking across the Sacred Lake: Taharqa's edifice and the well
Kind: site
About: sacred-lake, taharqa-edifice
From the south-east corner the lake spreads out in front of you, and the temple rises behind it to the north-west. The water is usually still, so the buildings reflect in it, and early in the day the light is soft. The view is a favourite with photographers, and the lake is the place reviewers most often call atmospheric.

On the far (north) side of the lake stood the Edifice of Taharqa, built by the Kushite king Taharqa (about 690 to 664 BC). Digital Karnak gives it as 25 metres wide and 29 metres deep, and says only 1 to 2.5 metres of its walls survive, so there is little to see except low foundations. In front of it was a mud brick courtyard, and a deep stone well cut through that courtyard. Digital Karnak calls the well a "Nilometer", the usual name for a stair or shaft used to measure the river's level, but says this one "seems to have instead served a ritual purpose": its deep shaft linked the temple to the primeval waters, and the rituals there helped the king reaffirm his power. Parker, Leclant and Goyon published the edifice in 1979, and Kathlyn Cooney wrote about its ritual role in 2000.

The Egyptians held that the world began as a dark, limitless water, Nun, from which the first land rose. A temple's sacred lake, and a well reaching the water table, brought that water into the temple. It is a good example of ritual and practical water supply being the same thing: the pit is only possible because the water table lies close below the surface.

The café is on the north-west side of the lake. There is little shade at the viewpoint.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Taharqo Edifice and Nilometer (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/taharqo-edifice), citing Parker, Leclant and Goyon (1979), Cooney (2000), Leclant (1965); Digital Karnak, Sacred Lake; WeRoad Karnak guide (December 2025); general knowledge for Nun.

## The Seventh Pylon
Kind: site
About: seventh-pylon
The Seventh Pylon is the gateway at the south end of the Cachette Court. Digital Karnak credits it to Thutmose III, who ruled from 1479 to 1425 BC. It is 26 metres high, 63.2 metres long and 10.6 metres thick, and built of sandstone. Inscriptions on one of his chapels say he replaced an earlier mud brick pylon on the same spot.

It stands between Hatshepsut's Eighth Pylon and the southern entrance of his festival court, and it opened the temple's southern processional route. That route ran south towards the Temple of Mut and, beyond, Luxor Temple. Most processions went east to west, but the god also travelled south, and each new pylon pushed the route further out.

The south face of both towers, the side away from the Cachette Court, shows Thutmose III smiting a group of cowering captives. This is the standard way of showing a king's victory over Egypt's enemies, and it appears on the front of nearly every pylon. A public-domain photograph shows him smiting Canaanite enemies on this pylon, and the scene is linked to the Megiddo campaign of his Year 23 (about 1457 BC) in the photograph's caption. Two colossal statues of the king flanked the doorway on the south side, and two rose granite obelisks stood just south of them. Digital Karnak does not give their sizes.

From the north, in the Cachette Court, you see the back of the pylon. The court takes its name from the great pit of statues found here in 1903 (see the Cachette sections).

How a pylon was built is explained in the First Pylon sections: a rubble core, casing blocks laid in courses, and mud brick ramps raised as the walls grew. The Seventh Pylon will have been built in the same way, though Digital Karnak records no construction details.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Seventh Pylon (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/7th-pylon), citing Azim and Traunecker 1982, Carlotti 1995, Laskowski, Cline and O'Connor 2006; Shaw (2000); Wikimedia Commons caption, "Thutmose III at Karnak.jpg" (public domain) for the Megiddo link.

## The Cachette Court and the Karnak Cachette
Kind: site
About: cachette, cachette-burial, georges-legrain
The Cachette Court lies between the Hypostyle Hall and the Seventh Pylon. "Cachette" is French for a hiding place. Here, in the Greek and Roman period, many stone and metal statues were buried in one large pit, and in 1903 the French Egyptologist Georges Legrain began finding them. Emmanuel Jambon, who studied Legrain's records for the French Institute (IFAO), gives the discovery as 26 December 1903, with four seasons of work lasting until July 1907. Some sources say 1903 to 1905, but the IFAO's study supports 1907.

What was in it. The stone statues range from the Middle Kingdom to the Ptolemaic period: kings, priests, officials, gods, a great many block statues of private people. The totals vary by source and by date of counting. Legrain's first-season summaries give 457 statues and statuettes. The writer Gaston Maspero, who headed the Antiquities Service, wrote in 1905 that seven hundred stone monuments had been brought out of the water, with more than ten thousand bronzes. Popular accounts say more than 750, or nearly 800, stone statues and 17,000 or 18,000 bronzes. Legrain himself reached about 17,000 bronzes by 1906. The IFAO database, a modern catalogue, holds well over a thousand records, but that counts fragments and other objects too. Safe wording: several hundred stone statues, in the range 750 to 800 by the usual count, and about 17,000 bronzes.

Why it was buried. Nobody recorded the reason. Legrain thought the pit was filled in one go, and Jambon found no link between an object's age and its depth, which supports that. Scholars usually explain such deposits as the respectful burial of statues that were crowded out of the temple or no longer wanted, since sacred objects could not simply be thrown away (general knowledge, verify).

Legrain's other job was the Hypostyle Hall. He was rebuilding it after the collapse of 1899, and digging in this court was part of his wider work around the Seventh Pylon. He died in 1917.

Most statues went to the Egyptian Museum in Cairo, and the French database catalogues them with Legrain's own "K" numbers. Legrain's own excavation diaries are lost.
Sources: Jambon, "Les fouilles de Georges Legrain dans la Cachette de Karnak (1903–1907)", BIFAO 109 (2010), pp. 239–279; IFAO, Base Cachette de Karnak (Coulon and Jambon); Digital Karnak, Seventh Pylon (Greco-Roman burial); general knowledge for why such deposits were made.

## Digging the Cachette: water, shadufs and numbers
Kind: site
About: cachette, georges-legrain
The pit was deep and wet. The Nile flood pushes the water table up in autumn, and Legrain's season began on 28 September 1903 with the groundwater still high. Jambon says Legrain wrote repeatedly to the Antiquities Service director that October about the seepage. The pit lay below the water table, so it flooded as fast as it was dug.

Legrain solved it with the simplest local technology. He reported that "rustic apparatus", shadufs (hand-swung bucket-and-counterweight lifts, still used on the Nile), let the dig be taken down to about 10 metres in 1904 to 1905. A year later he reported reaching nearly 14 metres, and the work stopped on 14 June 1906. Jambon estimates the maximum between 10 and 15 metres. Maspero put it in a famous phrase: "twenty months we have been fishing for statues in the temple of Karnak". In May 1904 the pit was so flooded and foul that Legrain wrote it "stinks marvellously".

Not everything survived the water. In May and June 1905, at nine to ten metres, Legrain found a bank of wooden furniture and wooden statues, about ten cubic metres. Within hours of leaving the damp they dried, split and crumbled; he tried to keep the best in a railway wagon full of water, and sent a few examples to the museum, but they did not last. He also found many animal mummies.

The bronzes are a mystery. Legrain's counts rose to about 17,000 by 1906, yet Jambon says they seem to have vanished entirely since. He also notes that when Maspero reprinted his article in 1914 he removed its mention of the bronzes. Jambon suggests Maspero may have known more than we do about what happened to them. I found no explanation. So "about 17,000 bronzes" is what Legrain counted, not what a museum holds.

For the family: the Hypostyle Hall's collapse and this dig happened in the same few years, with the same man, and both were fights with the Nile's groundwater.
Sources: Jambon, BIFAO 109 (2010), pp. 239–279 (reports and letters of Legrain, Archaeological Report 1903 to 1906, and Maspero's articles of 1905 and 1914); general knowledge for the shaduf.

## The Hittite peace treaty on the Cachette Court wall
Kind: site
About: hittite-treaty, ramesses-ii
On the wall at the west side of the Cachette Court is a long text in hieroglyphs: the treaty between Ramesses II of Egypt and the Hittite king Hattusili III. The Biblical Archaeology Society describes it as inscribed in the centre of the wall between two engaged pillars, next to the Hypostyle Hall, and partly destroyed. Four carved scenes flank it, two on each side. These were once attributed to Ramesses II, but are now identified as episodes from the Canaanite campaign of his son Merneptah.

When. The treaty was agreed in the 21st year of Ramesses II, which is about 1259 BC in Shaw's chronology (some sources give 1258 BC). It ended roughly two decades of hostility after the battle of Kadesh, in about 1274 BC. I could not find out when the Karnak copy was carved, only that it is later than the treaty.

What it says. Neither state would invade the other, and each would help the other if a third party attacked. The treaty was written in both languages: each side wrote a version in its own language, and each was also set down in Akkadian cuneiform on metal, perhaps silver, tablets for exchange.

The two surviving versions. The Egyptian text survives in two places in Thebes, here at Karnak and at the Ramesseum, Ramesses II's mortuary temple. Wikipedia gives the Karnak text as 30 lines and the Ramesseum text as 10 lines, preserved only as fragments, but I could not confirm those numbers in a Karnak-specific source. Champollion copied the Karnak text in 1828. The Hittite side comes from clay tablets in Akkadian dug up at Hattusa (Boğazkale, Turkey) by Hugo Winckler's expedition, starting in 1906. Two are in the Museum of the Ancient Orient, part of the Istanbul Archaeology Museums, and one is in Berlin. The texts differ in wording, because each side framed its own.

The "oldest surviving peace treaty" is a common description. It is certainly the best-known early treaty with both sides' text surviving, but I did not check the claim against older treaties.

A copy is said to hang at the United Nations headquarters in New York. That comes from Wikipedia, citing two 1970 newspaper pieces. I could not confirm it from the United Nations, so treat it as reported but unverified.
Sources: Biblical Archaeology Society, "Understanding the Wall of Reliefs" and "A Good Peace: the Hittite-Egyptian Treaty" (library.biblicalarchaeology.org); Wikipedia, Egyptian–Hittite peace treaty (pointer only: line counts, tablets' locations, Champollion, UN claim, Winckler); Shaw (2000) for the regnal year.

## Hugo Winckler and the Hittite tablets
Kind: person
About: hugo-winckler, hattusa-tablets-found
Hugo Winckler (born 4 July 1863, died 19 April 1913) was a German Assyriologist. He excavated Hattusa, the Hittite capital in Turkey, from 1906 to 1908 with Theodore Makridi, and found the royal archives, thousands of clay tablets. Among them was the Akkadian version of the treaty with Ramesses II. The Egyptian side of the treaty was known long before, from the walls at Karnak that Champollion copied in 1828, so for the first time both sides' wording could be compared. Two tablets are in Istanbul and one in Berlin. He found them only three years after Legrain began the Cachette at Karnak, in the same decade.
Sources: Wikipedia, Egyptian–Hittite peace treaty (pointer; dates of the excavation and tablets' locations); Wikipedia, Hugo Winckler (birth and death dates, general knowledge, verify).

## The southern pylons (Eighth, Ninth and Tenth)
Kind: site
About: eighth-pylon, ninth-pylon, tenth-pylon
South of the Seventh Pylon the old southern road of Karnak runs on through three more pylons, each built a little further south, towards the Temple of Mut and on to Luxor.

The Eighth Pylon was begun under Hatshepsut (reigned 1473 to 1458 BC, Shaw; Digital Karnak dates the work 1479 to 1458 BC). It is 21 metres high, 47.7 metres long, 9.3 metres thick, and sandstone. It was the first of the surviving pylons on the southern route and may have replaced a mud brick one. Statues of Thutmose II, Amenhotep I with Ahmose-Nefertari, and Amenhotep II stood along its south face. Her own decoration is mostly lost, because later kings recarved it: Thutmose III, Amenhotep II, Tutankhamun and Sety I. The north face shows the king with the gods and a procession of the sacred bark, possibly the Opet festival. It is the oldest pylon on the southern route, but not the oldest at Karnak, since Thutmose I built the Fourth and Fifth Pylons earlier. The tour's older wording was wrong on that.

The Ninth Pylon was built by Horemheb (reigned 1323 to 1295 BC, as Digital Karnak gives it), 26.1 metres high and 65.6 metres long. Horemheb dismantled Akhenaten's temples at east Karnak and used their small sandstone blocks, the talatat, as fill inside the pylon. Ramesses II added reliefs between the Eighth and Ninth Pylons showing the Opet procession of the sacred barks.

The Tenth Pylon, the furthest south, was begun by Amenhotep III, who finished only its lowest courses. Horemheb completed it, 66.6 metres long and 21 metres high, with a rose granite doorway 15.62 metres high. The upper towers contain more of Akhenaten's blocks. Its court shows Horemheb's voyage to the land of Punt and the treasures brought back. A doorway on the east side led to offices, stores and priests' quarters.

Access is uncertain. Two visitor accounts (one undated, one written in 2020 and updated in 2024) say much of the area beyond the Eighth Pylon is closed, but Commons photographs show people at the Eighth, Ninth and Tenth Pylons on 10 March 2024 and on 4 December 2025.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Eighth Pylon (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/8th-pylon), Ninth Pylon, Tenth Pylon; Shaw (2000); Wikimedia Commons photographs dated 10 March 2024 and 4 December 2025 (access evidence); visitor accounts at thediscoveriesof.com and sailingstonetravel.com.

## The Temple of Khonsu
Kind: site
About: khonsu-temple
The Temple of Khonsu stands in the south-west corner of the precinct, facing south. The American Research Center in Egypt, which conserved it, calls it "an excellent example of a small but complete New Kingdom temple". It has a pylon, an open court with a portico of 28 papyrus-bud columns, a columned hall, and a bark chamber with the sanctuary behind. Digital Karnak gives the whole temple as about 70 metres long and 27 metres wide, with a pylon 34.5 metres long and 18 metres high.

Ramesses III (reigned 1184 to 1153 BC) began it, but not as a first temple. The Epigraphic Survey of the Oriental Institute says he used both newly quarried stone and old inscribed blocks, mostly Eighteenth Dynasty, taken from earlier buildings and recut. One reused block comes from the reign of Thutmose IV, and a column drum in the court still carries the double cartouche of Horemheb. Digital Karnak says reused blocks may even point to an earlier temple here, with some linked to Amenhotep III and possibly from his mortuary temple across the river.

Who finished it is argued. Epigraphic Survey: Ramesses XI and the High Priest Herihor built the front (the pylon, court and first hall), and "additions and alterations continued for over a millennium". Digital Karnak lists inscriptions of Ramesses III, Ramesses IV, Ramesses XI, Ramesses XII, Herihor and Pinedjem. About 690 to 664 BC Taharqa added a porch of four rows of five papyrus columns in front of the pylon. Later the Ptolemies added the great gateway south of the temple (the Bab el-Amara).

The temple was heavily used for oracles. The Epigraphic Survey says the god Khonsu gave oracular appearances here and that texts record land purchases and royal restorations.

The tour's date of about 1170 BC for the start is a mid-reign marker only. Ramesses III's reign gives the limits, and no source I found gives a start year.

The temple has been conserved by ARCE from 2006 to 2018, and further work began later.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Khonsu Temple (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/khonsu-temple); Epigraphic Survey, The Temple of Khonsu, vol. 1 (OIP 100, 1979) and vol. 2 (OIP 103, 1981), prefaces; ARCE, Khonsu Temple (arce.org/?p=3600); Shaw (2000).

## Khonsu the moon god
Kind: reference
About: khonsu-temple, opet-temple
Khonsu was the Egyptian moon god, and in Thebes the child of Amun and Mut, so the third member of the Theban triad. His name is usually translated "traveller", for the moon's journey across the night sky. He was also seen as a healer and protector of people travelling at night. The Bentresh stela tells of a princess of a far country cured when an image of him was sent there. His main cult centre was Karnak, and much Ramesside building here was done for him. (General knowledge, from Wikipedia, verify.)

At the Khonsu temple he is shown in the usual Egyptian way for a child god: a boy with a plaited sidelock of youth (general knowledge, verify). A Commons photograph of a carving in the columned hall is captioned as Khonsu in the form of a baboon, an animal linked with the moon and with Thoth, the god of writing.

Digital Karnak adds a Theban myth about rebirth. In one version Amun dies as Osiris, and is reborn through the mother goddess Opet. Khonsu, as son of Amun-Ra, was seen as the newly reborn sun god. That ties his temple to the Opet temple beside it, which has a doorway directly opposite one in the Khonsu temple.

The Epigraphic Survey's volumes show how real this was for the temple's builders. Herihor is shown several times in a scene of the Opet festival, with the barks of Amun, Mut and Khonsu being towed on the Nile, in the Khonsu temple. A procession of the portable barks of the Theban triad is carved on the north wall of the portico.
Sources: Wikipedia, Khonsu (pointer, verify); Digital Karnak, Opet Temple (the myth); Epigraphic Survey, OIP 100 (1979), preface; Wikimedia Commons caption, "Karnak Tempel Chons 09.jpg" (Olaf Tausch); general knowledge for the sidelock.

## Herihor and the late Ramesside work at the Khonsu temple
Kind: person
About: herihor, khonsu-temple
Herihor was High Priest of Amun at the end of the New Kingdom, under Ramesses XI, the last Ramesside king (Shaw: about 1099 to 1069 BC). The Epigraphic Survey says he carved most of the scenes in the first hypostyle hall as High Priest, and that he claimed to do so on the "instruction of His Majesty" Ramesses XI. Later he took royal titles, and the court of the Khonsu temple is where that is best attested. The only other monument that formally names him as king is the Hypostyle Hall, where he added an inscription under scenes of Ramesses II.

In the court he copied the layout of the Hypostyle Hall: raised relief on the left (west) of the axis, sunk relief on the right (east). On the east wall of the court he had the façade of the Second Pylon shown. On the west wall of the portico is his family procession, which the Survey re-read: the first prince was not Paiankh, as had been assumed from Lepsius's old drawing, but Ankhefenmut. That weakens the old belief that Paiankh, who succeeded him as High Priest, was Herihor's son.

His wife Nuteme kept a respected place in Thebes after his death and was buried with papyri naming her King's Mother. His children seem to have faded from the record.

Herihor's famous oracle stela is on the façade of the portico, now badly worn, and cut through when a Thirtieth Dynasty doorway was installed. The Survey volumes (1979 and 1981) record all of this.

The era is a turning point: in the Twenty-first Dynasty (from about 1069 BC) kings ruled the north from Tanis while High Priests of Amun ran Thebes, and Pinedjem, also seen at Khonsu, became a king in Thebes.
Sources: Epigraphic Survey, The Temple of Khonsu, vol. 1 (OIP 100, 1979), preface by Wente; vol. 2 (OIP 103, 1981), preface; Digital Karnak, Khonsu Temple; Shaw (2000).

## The roof, the Taharqa porch and the conservation of the Khonsu temple
Kind: site
About: khonsu-temple, khonsu-conservation
Most of Karnak is open to the sky, but the Khonsu temple still has much of its stone roof over the columned hall and rooms. A visitor in June described its "intact ceiling and painted reliefs". The Epigraphic Survey mentions a solar chapel on the roof, with a scene carved under the High Priest Pinedjem I (the Survey spells the name Painutem). A stair leads up. ARCE says a mason installed sandstone roof-lights over the chapels, and a large replacement roofing block over the staircase to the roof. A safe viewing space on the roof, with a view over Karnak, was reported as nearly complete. I could not confirm that it is open.

Why does a stone roof matter? It spans the gaps between columns with stone beams, architraves, and slabs laid across them. That is a heavy load, which is why Egyptian halls have columns packed close together. Digital Karnak makes the point about Taharqa's porch, where the columns stood 7.2 to 8.2 metres apart: "a stone roof would have been too heavy to span", so it suggests a timber roof carried on stone architraves. The porch had four rows of five papyrus columns and a red granite floor, and it echoed one Taharqa built before the Ramesses II temple at the east end of Karnak.

The court of the Khonsu temple, by contrast, was unroofed, a portico of 28 columns round an open space.

ARCE, with USAID funding, worked here in phases from 2006 to 2018 and again from the 2020s. It cleaned and consolidated painted reliefs in six of the twelve chapels, laid a stone walkway, new flooring in the court and hall, and partly rebuilt the north façade. Chapel 12 was the model, with visitor-friendly flooring, solar lighting and removable guards. Some of the vivid reliefs had been hidden for centuries under smoke and grime.

Ancient Origins reported four chapels and two tombs opened to visitors, with a new walkway, but gave no date.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Khonsu Temple (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/khonsu-temple and ?p=3792); ARCE, Khonsu Temple (arce.org/?p=3600) and later ARCE update (via search summary, not fully read); Epigraphic Survey, OIP 100 (solar chapel, footnote); visitor account at thediscoveriesof.com.

## The Temple of Opet
Kind: site
About: opet-temple
The small Temple of Opet lies just west of the Khonsu temple. Opet was the mother goddess of Thebes, shown as a hippopotamus in some forms, and the temple is tied to the idea of rebirth (the hippopotamus link is general knowledge, verify). Digital Karnak says the temple was originally built by Nectanebo I (380 to 362 BC), modified by Ptolemy III (246 to 221 BC), who inscribed the crypt, and left unfinished until Ptolemy VIII. Most of the interior decoration is his, and the outside was finished under the Roman emperor Augustus. The earliest part may be Taharqa's porch and small pylon, so an earlier Opet temple probably stood here. The building's sequence is still unclear, and the CFEETK is studying it.

The plan is small. A small pylon and columned porch face west, with a columned kiosk in the court. A ramp leads up to the raised temple, a podium 19.6 by 22.7 metres and 1.9 metres high. Inside is a little hall with two Hathor-headed columns, then a square sanctuary with a niche for the god's statue.

The crypts are the special feature. They run down into the platform, and include a "tomb of Osiris" and a "birth chamber". They fit the myth that Amun dies as Osiris and is reborn through Opet. A small shrine to Osiris's cult statue is on the east rear wall, reached from outside.

The temple stands directly opposite a door of the Khonsu temple, which points to a close cult link.

Access: one visitor account (written 2020, updated 2024) found it locked and saw it only from the side. Commons has eight photographs taken inside on 10 March 2024, so it can be open. The crypts are almost certainly not open to the public (my inference).
Sources: Digital Karnak, Opet Temple (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/opet-temple/), citing Azim 1987, Golvin and Goyon 1987, Arnold 1999, Wilkinson 2000; Wikimedia Commons photographs dated 10 March 2024; sailingstonetravel.com visitor account.

## The Bab el-Amara gate (Ptolemy III's gateway)
Kind: site
About: bab-el-amara
The Bab el-Amara is the great stone gateway south of the Khonsu temple, in the enclosure wall of Nectanebo I. It is 21 metres high, and the opening is 14 to 14.4 metres high and 5.6 metres wide. Digital Karnak says Nectanebo I, or his successor Teos, began two pylon towers, each planned to be 28.2 metres long, to front the Khonsu temple with a new gate. They were never finished. Ptolemy III Euergetes (246 to 221 BC) then put a stone gateway between the planned towers and carved it with his name. A later Ptolemaic king built a mud brick wall against it, which covered the unfinished towers.

It is sometimes called the Gate of Euergetes. Digital Karnak's page on enclosures calls it a Ptolemy VIII gate, but its gate page and other sources give Ptolemy III, which is the better supported.

Its design: a winged sun disk at the centre of the lintel, flying vultures on the ceiling, and the hieroglyphs for "all life, prosperity and health" on the inner walls. The north face shows the king with divinities. A sphinx-lined alley ran south from it.

It gave the temple of Khonsu a direct way in from the south. Visitors reach the Khonsu temple through this southern side. The unfinished towers are a useful reminder that even late Egyptian building programmes were left half done.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Bab el-Amara Gate (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/bab-el-amara-gate/), citing Lauffray, Sauneron and Ramadan 1975, Zignani 2003, Laroche-Traunecker 1982; Digital Karnak, Khonsu Temple (the gate gave direct access); Shaw (2000).

## Gaston Maspero
Kind: person
About: gaston-maspero
Gaston Maspero (born 23 June 1846, died 30 June 1916; general knowledge, verify) was the French Egyptologist who led the Egyptian Antiquities Service, Legrain's employer. Legrain's letters about the Karnak dig were addressed to him. In 1905 Maspero wrote a newspaper article, "La pêche aux statues" ("fishing for statues"), for Le Temps, saying that after twenty months seven hundred stone monuments had been brought out of the water with more than ten thousand bronzes. When he reprinted the article in 1914 in his book Ruines et paysages d'Égypte he cut the mention of the bronzes, which Emmanuel Jambon found suspicious given that the bronzes have since disappeared from the record.
Sources: Jambon, BIFAO 109 (2010), pp. 239–279; general knowledge for his dates.
