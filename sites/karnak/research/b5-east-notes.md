## The Middle Kingdom court (Senusret I's temple)
Kind: site
About: senusret-temple, senusret-i
The Middle Kingdom court is the open, empty-looking space between the granite sanctuary and the Akhmenu. It is the oldest part of Karnak that visitors can stand on. The temple of Amun-Ra that stood here was built by Senusret I, who reigned from 1971 to 1926 BC in Shaw's chronology. The tour uses about 1950 BC; Digital Karnak gives only his whole reign.

What it looked like depends on the reconstruction. Luc Gabolde of the Franco-Egyptian centre (CFEETK) rebuilt it on paper from blocks found at Karnak since the early 1900s and from his excavations in this court. His version is a limestone temple about 38 metres square and about 6 metres high, with four doorways whose thresholds were red granite. In front was a portico 3 metres deep with twelve square pillars, each carrying a statue of the king as Osiris. Some of those pillars are now in the Cairo Museum. The sanctuary was off the central line, so a priest had to turn ninety degrees left to reach it. Inside stood a calcite altar holding the shrine of the Amun-Ra statue. The materials were limestone, rose granite, sandstone and calcite, inside a mud-brick enclosure wall.

It was not the first building here. Below the level of the thresholds Gabolde found small sandstone platforms, none larger than 10 metres by 10 metres, which he dated to the early Eleventh Dynasty. He reads them as the first temple and portico of Intef II (about 2100 to 2050 BC), taken down soon after his reign and rebuilt by later Eleventh Dynasty kings and Amenhotep's predecessor Amenemhat I. So Senusret I enlarged an existing cult rather than founding it. His was the first large stone temple, which is why the app's timeline says "first large stone temple".

Later kings built over it. Amenhotep I (1525 to 1504 BC) added lines of stone chapels and store rooms along the court. Hatshepsut removed the Osiris portico and added her "Palace of Maat" in front of the temple, with the Red Chapel inside it. Thutmose III's Akhmenu, just east of here, echoes the layout of Senusret I's temple, with a sanctuary reached by a turn.

Sources disagree on three points. Size and direction: Gabolde's temple faces west; François Larché argues for a smaller temple with a double portico facing east, towards a Nile channel in east Karnak that has since gone. Demolition: Sullivan's traditional account has the temple razed, perhaps about 323 BC, when the granite shrine of Philip Arrhidaeus was installed and the ground raised; Larché says Amenhotep I took it down because the stone was decaying; Digital Karnak's court page says part may have stood until the fourth century AD, when local people carried off the blocks. Sullivan's account is the best supported but is not certain. Orientation: some scholars say the axis was set on the winter solstice sunrise from the start, and others doubt that for so early a date (see the section on the main axis).

What to look for: a low mud-brick wall, large flat stone slabs inside it, an information board, and Hatshepsut's standing obelisk to the west. There is no shade.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Middle Kingdom Court; Sullivan, "Karnak: Development of the Temple of Amun-Ra", UEE (2010); Sullivan, The Development of the Temple of Karnak (Digital Karnak, 2010); Shaw (ed.), Oxford History of Ancient Egypt (2000).

## The Akhmenu (Festival Hall of Thutmose III)
Kind: site
About: akhmenu, thutmose-iii
The Akhmenu, "the most glorious of monuments", is the large temple Thutmose III built behind what was then Karnak's east wall. Digital Karnak and Sullivan both say it was begun after his twenty-third year. Thutmose III reigned from 1479 to 1425 BC (Shaw), so that is about 1457 to 1456 BC. Building took about seven years, the botanical room was decorated two years after building began, and the great hall probably marks his jubilee (heb-sed), likely celebrated in his thirtieth year, about 1450 BC. Its exact religious purpose is still unclear; Sullivan suggests ceremonies for the renewal of the king.

It is 40 metres deep and 77.5 metres wide. The main door is in the south-west corner. Sources disagree about a second door found by CFEETK: Digital Karnak puts it in the middle of the west wall and Sullivan in the east wall. Thutmose III also built a new sandstone wall around the Akhmenu and a small contra-temple on its east side.

The great hall is the tent. Two rows of unusual columns, painted in bands of colour, represent the poles of a portable tent, and the ceiling was painted blue with yellow stars. North of it are three chapels, the largest holding a quartzite group of Thutmose III with Amun and Mut. The south-east holds a suite of rooms for Sokar, the falcon god of the dead at Memphis, around a hall of eight fluted columns. There is a main sanctuary with eight niches and a larger niche at the back for the god's shrine, and a stair in the north-east corner of the hall climbs to a solar shrine on the roof.

Beside the entrance corridor is the small Hall of Ancestors, which showed Thutmose III honouring sixty-one earlier kings, the earliest names being from the Fourth Dynasty. The reliefs were taken to the Louvre in 1843.

The building changed later. The upper solar shrine was altered in the Ramesside period. Decoration in the rooms for the king and for Amun was altered under Alexander the Great, and inscriptions credit him with restoring one of the twin sanctuaries. The central hall later became a church (see the section on the church).

Access: visitors have reported that few rooms are open. The Southern Chapels, to the right of the main entrance (seven linked shrines and two columned rooms), were restored by the CFEETK and reported complete in June 2025, with new signs and ramps. The Past says the public could see them for the first time. Whether the inner rooms are open in March 2027 is not known.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Akhmenu; Sullivan, UEE (2010); Sullivan, The Development of the Temple of Karnak (Digital Karnak, 2010); Ancient Pages, 17 June 2025 (citing Ahram Online); The Past, 21 August 2025; Luxor Living Lab, Layers of Heritage; Shaw (2000).

## The botanical room of the Akhmenu ("botanical garden")
Kind: site
About: botanical-garden, akhmenu
The botanical room is in the north-east part of the Akhmenu. Its walls are carved with plants and animals that Thutmose III and his men saw on their military campaigns abroad, according to Digital Karnak and Sullivan. Four papyrus columns stood in it, and its decoration was begun about two years after building started, so about 1454 BC.

The carving is in two bands: plants above and animals below. Wikipedia counts about 275 different kinds of plant. That figure is not from a source I could open, so treat it as approximate. Early French scholars called it the "botanical garden" because of the plants. Nathalie Beaux's 1990 book on the room is titled "Le cabinet de curiosités de Thoutmosis III", the cabinet of curiosities, which is closer to how it is now understood: a showcase of exotic things from Syria and Palestine, not a garden.

What it was for is argued. Popular accounts say Thutmose III brought the plants home and introduced species such as the iris, but that is not established. A study by Dimitri Laboury (2007) links the room's function to ritual. Treat both as ideas, not facts.

The campaigns themselves are recorded elsewhere in Karnak. Thutmose III's "Annals", carved near the sanctuary, describe campaigns from his twenty-second to his forty-second year, which is about 1458 to 1438 BC. This room is the picture book to go with that written record.

What to look for: low, shallow carvings of leaves, flowers, fruit, birds and animals. Photographs suggest the carved wall can be seen from a roped walkway on the outside of the room, which may help if the inner door is closed. The wall is faint in strong light, so look from an angle.

For the family: this is the best place in the east end to find animals and flowers for a two-year-old.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Akhmenu; Sullivan, UEE (2010); Wikipedia, "Botanical garden of Thutmosis III" (pointer only, citing Beaux 1990 and Laboury 2007); Shaw (2000).

## The Akhmenu as a church: painted saints on the columns
Kind: site
About: karnak-churches, akhmenu
After about AD 300 the great temples of Egypt lost their purpose. Pagan worship was banned across the Roman empire in the late fourth century, with Constantius II closing temples in 356 and Theodosius I issuing bans in 391 and 392 (general knowledge, to be verified). By the fourth century Karnak was being dismantled rather than built: Sullivan reports that two great obelisks were taken away, and the other fallen obelisks were broken up for door thresholds and millstones.

Christians then founded churches among the ruins. The best known is in the central hall of Thutmose III's Akhmenu, where painted saints and Coptic inscriptions survive. Wikipedia gives this and cites Blyth (2006, page 234), which I could not open. A local heritage site, Luxor Living Lab, says the church dates from the fourth century and that saints are painted on six columns. I found no scholarly source that confirms the century, so it is uncertain. The label on the app's timeline, AD 400, is a round figure.

What you can see: faint painted figures with halos on some of the columns, put over the older painted decoration. Photographs show them high on the column shaft. Visitor accounts mention them (one blogger noticed "several paintings of saints on the columns"). They are faint and easy to miss; look about two thirds of the way up the shaft, where the column is widest.

Why a temple hall? The Akhmenu's central hall had two rows of tall columns and was easy to make into a covered space, but no source says why it was chosen, so do not take that as fact.

The reuse shows how Karnak's life continued after the pharaohs. The same hall was a tent of stone for a king's festival, then the nave of a church. After the church was abandoned, a village grew up among the ruins of Karnak.
Sources: Sullivan, UEE (2010); Wikipedia, History of the Karnak Temple complex (pointer only, citing Blyth 2006, page 234); Luxor Living Lab, Layers of Heritage (heritage site, not scholarly); a travel blog, November 2018; general knowledge for the closure edicts.

## The Temple of Ptah and the Sekhmet statue
Kind: site
About: ptah-temple, ptah-gates
The Temple of Ptah is a small sandstone temple in the northern part of the Amun precinct. Thutmose III built it, possibly on the site of an earlier mud-brick temple (Sullivan). The CFEETK's work agrees: Thutmose III's foundation stele (Cairo CG 34013) says he found an older temple of brick, with wooden columns and doors, falling to ruin, and rebuilt it. That puts the building at about Year 23 to Year 24 of his reign, about 1457 to 1456 BC. I could read this only through summaries of the CFEETK and Egyptian Archaeology reports, so treat the exact year as approximate.

It was a temple for Ptah, the god of craftsmen and builders, with Sekhmet his lioness-headed wife and Nefertem their son. A hall with two columns fronted a triple sanctuary. Excavations since 2008 by a Franco-Egyptian team found that the earlier brick sanctuary may go back to the Seventeenth Dynasty. The full survey is Biston-Moulin and Thiers, Le temple de Ptah à Karnak (2016).

The gateways to the west were added later, over about 700 years. Shabaqo, a Kushite king, added two about 700 BC. The Ptolemaic kings Ptolemy VI and Ptolemy XII extended the entrance with more. Reliefs of the Roman emperor Tiberius (AD 14 to 37) were added to one gate. Nectanebo I's great wall (about 380 to 362 BC) had a gate in the north wall beside this temple.

The Sekhmet statue is in the southern side chapel, the right-hand room as you go in. The statue is about 2 metres tall and is lit only by a hole in the roof. According to the CFEETK, it was re-erected and is not in its original place, and the zenithal opening gives sun, and on some nights moonlight, to the figure. An Egyptian Archaeology report says the missing roof slabs over the chapels were removed by Georges Legrain to let light in, so the effect is modern (reported, not checked). The northern chapel keeps its original roof.

Sekhmet is the fierce side of the goddess: a lioness of war and plague who could also heal (general knowledge, to be verified). Ptah is the Memphite creator who made the world by thought and word.

Access: in a 2020 blog (updated in 2024), a room in the temple was locked until a guard opened it, and the Sekhmet statue was seen in the second sanctuary. Carry small change for a tip. If it is shut, the row of gateways can be seen from outside.
Sources: Sullivan, UEE (2010); Digital Karnak, Ptah Temple (location and bibliography only); CFEETK and Egyptian Archaeology 38 (2011), Thiers and Zignani (read only through summaries); Wikimedia Commons file description of the Sekhmet statue; a travel blog, updated September 2024.

## The Temple of the Hearing Ear and the eastern end
Kind: site
About: hearing-ear-temple, nectanebo-east-gate
At the east end of Karnak is a small temple that Ramesses II built to "Amun-Ra, Ramesses II, who hears prayers", usually called the Temple of the Hearing Ear. Digital Karnak says it lay at the far east side of the Amun precinct, built around a single obelisk (see the section on the Lateran obelisk). It is about 32 metres wide and 25.5 metres long, in sandstone with a mud-brick gateway, and had a pillared hall with a central false door. Two side doors led out to the obelisk. Column drums of Thutmose III were reused in it, so Ramesses II probably rebuilt an earlier shrine of Thutmose III.

Its purpose was public. The temple stood outside the closed inner temple, behind the Akhmenu and Thutmose III's contra-temple, so people who were not allowed inside could come to petition the god. Sullivan says it seems to have worked like a contra-temple, and that people came for oracular judgements, though Digital Karnak does not use the word "oracle". Ramesses II reigned from 1279 to 1213 BC; the tour uses about 1250 BC. One specialist study queries a date of Years 40 to 46 of his reign (about 1240 to 1234 BC), so it is uncertain.

Taharqa, the Kushite king, added a porch of twenty papyrus columns in four rows of five, 9.5 metres high and about 1 metre across, joined by low screen walls (690 to 664 BC). It has fallen. Ptolemy VIII (about 170 to 116 BC) enclosed the rear colonnade and the base of the obelisk and cut a new doorway through the false door.

Thutmose III's contra-temple just west of it was a calcite shrine with a statue group of the king and Amun, between two obelisks of Hatshepsut. Her obelisks are destroyed; the CFEETK has been documenting their pieces.

The eastern gate beyond is Nectanebo I's. Digital Karnak says it was begun by Nectanebo I and was about 20 metres high. His wall was 12 metres thick and 21 metres high. He planned twelve gates and finished four. Ramesses II had earlier marked the east entrance with two red granite obelisks and two granite sphinxes.

Beyond the gate lay Akhenaten's temples to the Aten, built about 1350 BC and later pulled apart by Horemheb for his own building fill.

Access: the end is quiet. One blog (2020, updated 2024) found the eastern gate locked. If it is closed, it can still be seen from the temple side.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Ramesses II Eastern Temple; Digital Karnak, Enclosures and Gates; Sullivan, UEE (2010); Sullivan, The Development of the Temple of Karnak (Digital Karnak, 2010); a travel blog, updated September 2024; Shaw (2000).

## The Lateran obelisk (the "unique" obelisk of east Karnak)
Kind: site
About: lateran-obelisk, lateran-obelisk-to-rome, lateran-obelisk-reerected, constantius-ii, domenico-fontana, thutmose-iv
The obelisk that stood at the east end of Karnak now stands in front of St John Lateran in Rome. It is the tallest ancient Egyptian obelisk still standing, about 32 metres for the shaft and 45.7 metres with the base and cross (NOVA, Turismo Roma). Red granite from Aswan.

It was begun for Thutmose III, who reigned from 1479 to 1425 BC. He did not finish it. Thutmose IV added his own lines of text around his grandfather's and raised it about 1400 BC. A text of his says it had lain on its side for thirty-five years (NOVA and Wikipedia; I did not see the inscription). Unlike the other obelisks at Karnak, which stood in pairs, this one stood alone, in the part of the temple given to sun worship (Sullivan). Ramesses II later built the Temple of the Hearing Ear at its foot.

Estimates of its weight vary. NOVA gives 455 tons, which is about 413 tonnes if short tons. Wikipedia gives about 300 tonnes for it as it stands now. It was re-erected about 4 metres shorter than at Karnak, because the base was damaged, according to Wikipedia.

In the fourth century it was taken down and shipped to Alexandria (Sullivan; Habachi 1984). Sources disagree on who ordered it. NOVA says Constantine I wanted it for Constantinople but died in 337 before it left Egypt; his son Constantius II sent it to Rome instead. Wikipedia credits Constantius II in one place and Constantine in another. Constantius II had it set up in the Circus Maximus in 357 (the date is from the ancient historian Ammianus Marcellinus; general knowledge, to be verified). It reached Ostia by ship and was towed up the Tiber.

It fell some time after the fifth century and was buried in mud. Pope Sixtus V ordered a search. In 1587 three pieces were found about 7 metres down in the old Circus Maximus. The architect Domenico Fontana joined them and raised the obelisk on 3 August 1588, with a cross on top. It was consecrated on 9 August. The base inscription gets some history wrong, for instance Constantine's baptism.

Shaltout and Belmonte suggest this obelisk stood exactly on Karnak's main axis, so someone at the quay might have seen it tip at the winter solstice sunrise. They say this is speculation.

Nothing of the obelisk is left at Karnak to see. The place is at the rear of the Hearing Ear temple.
Sources: Sullivan, UEE (2010); Shaltout and Belmonte, Journal for the History of Astronomy 36 (2005); NOVA Online, "A World of Obelisks: Rome"; Turismo Roma, The Lateran Obelisk; Wikipedia, Lateran Obelisk (pointer only); general knowledge for Ammianus.

## The main axis and the winter solstice sunrise
Kind: reference
About: karnak-axis, senusret-temple, akhmenu, lateran-obelisk
Karnak's main axis runs from the river in the west through the great temple to the Akhmenu and the east gate. Sparavigna measured it from satellite maps at 27.2 degrees from due east-west. At Luxor, the winter solstice sunrise is about 26 to 27 degrees south of east (my own calculation from the sunrise formula in her paper, about 26.2 degrees today and about 26.7 degrees around 1950 BC; not a published figure). So the axis points to the winter solstice sunrise, to within about a degree. The sun's disc is half a degree wide, so that is a close match but not exact. Sparavigna's paper is a preprint, not peer reviewed.

Who says what. Norman Lockyer (1894) thought the axis aimed at the summer solstice sunset. Shaltout and Belmonte (2005) report that when he asked for a check, the hills of western Thebes blocked the sunset and it never reached the inner rooms. Gerald Hawkins and others then favoured the winter solstice sunrise, and drew attention to a "high room of the sun" at the Akhmenu, perhaps built by Thutmose III, though inscriptions honouring the sunrise are Ramesses III's. Shaltout and Belmonte think its east opening was a window. Claude Traunecker (1991) argues it was a niche.

Hatshepsut's temple of Amun-Ra who hears prayers sat on the same axis but opened east, which on Belmonte's view made it the first building at Karnak to face the sunrise. Between her two obelisks, they suggest, the solstice sun would light the statues. Thutmose III then put a huge obelisk in front of it. The UNESCO and International Astronomical Union portal says the temple was aligned on the winter solstice sunrise since Senusret I "if not before". Belmonte himself, in 2005, put Gabolde's argument for a Middle Kingdom foundation date "in quarantine". So the early date is disputed.

The site may have been chosen for the river. Shaltout and Belmonte note that Thebes is the only place above the First Cataract where the average direction of the Nile is perpendicular to the solstice line. Modern studies show that the river channels near Karnak moved, so this works only for the broad course.

Each year about 21 December the rising sun is reported to shine along the axis and crowds gather. The family visit is in March, when the sun rises about due east, so the alignment cannot be seen directly.
Sources: Sparavigna, "The sunrise amplitude equation applied to an Egyptian temple" (arXiv 1207.6942, 2012); Shaltout and Belmonte, "On the orientation of ancient Egyptian temples: (1) Upper Egypt and Lower Nubia", Journal for the History of Astronomy 36 (2005); UNESCO and IAU Astronomy and World Heritage portal, The Temple of Amun at Karnak; Sullivan, UEE (2010); Digital Karnak, Akhmenu.

## Senusret I
Kind: person
About: senusret-i, senusret-temple
Senusret I (also spelled Senwosret, or Sesostris in Greek) was the second king of the Twelfth Dynasty and reigned from 1971 to 1926 BC (Shaw). At Karnak he turned a small earlier temple of Amun-Ra into a large limestone building about 38 metres square, with a portico of Osiris pillars. He also built the White Chapel, now rebuilt in the Open Air Museum, and other small shrines along processional routes.

Almost nothing of his temple stands where it was. Hatshepsut removed its portico, and most of the rest was dismantled. Its blocks were reused for fill in later pylons, which is how scholars have rebuilt it on paper.

Some scholars think the axis of the temple was set to the winter solstice sunrise. That is disputed.
Sources: Sullivan, UEE (2010); Digital Karnak, Middle Kingdom Court; Shaw (2000).

## Thutmose III
Kind: person
About: thutmose-iii, akhmenu, botanical-garden, lateran-obelisk
Thutmose III reigned from 1479 to 1425 BC (Shaw). He came to the throne as a child, and for about twenty years Hatshepsut ruled beside him. He then fought a series of campaigns into Syria and Palestine, the first ending at Megiddo in his Year 23 (about 1457 BC). His "Annals" at Karnak record the campaigns of his Years 22 to 42.

At Karnak he built the Akhmenu, the Seventh Pylon, the Sacred Lake, the Temple of Ptah and a new enclosure wall, and he remodelled the core. He also began the great obelisk now in Rome, which his grandson Thutmose IV raised. Hatshepsut's name and images were attacked late in his reign.
Sources: Sullivan, UEE (2010); Shaw (2000); Digital Karnak, Akhmenu.

## Ramesses II
Kind: person
About: ramesses-ii, hearing-ear-temple
Ramesses II ("the Great") reigned from 1279 to 1213 BC (Shaw). At Karnak he decorated the south half of the Hypostyle Hall, added an eastern entrance with two red granite obelisks and sphinxes, and built the Temple of the Hearing Ear at the foot of the obelisk of Thutmose IV. He is also known for his temples at Abu Simbel and for the treaty with the Hittites.
Sources: Sullivan, UEE (2010); Digital Karnak, Ramesses II Eastern Temple; Shaw (2000).

## Constantius II and Domenico Fontana (the obelisk's movers)
Kind: person
About: constantius-ii, domenico-fontana, lateran-obelisk-to-rome, lateran-obelisk-reerected
Constantius II (born 317, emperor from 337, died 361) was the son of Constantine I. He visited Rome once, in 357, and had the Karnak obelisk set up in the Circus Maximus there. He also ordered temples closed in 356 (general knowledge, to be verified).

Domenico Fontana (born 1543, died 1607) was the architect of Pope Sixtus V. In 1586 he raised the obelisk in front of St Peter's, and in 1588 he joined the three broken pieces of the Karnak obelisk and raised it in front of St John Lateran. He set a cross on top.
Sources: NOVA Online, "A World of Obelisks: Rome"; Turismo Roma, The Lateran Obelisk; general knowledge, to be verified.
