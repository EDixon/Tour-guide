## Stone types and quarries of ancient Egypt
Kind: reference
About: gebel-silsila-quarries, colossi-of-memnon
Egyptian builders mostly used the stone nearest the site. Harrell (UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology, "Building Stones") notes that most building stone was local, with notable exceptions. Limestone was the stone of the Old and Middle Kingdom Memphite pyramids and tombs, and the high-quality white limestone of Tura and Masara, near modern Cairo, was brought in for casing and burial chambers. Sandstone from Gebel el-Silsila was, in Harrell's words, the principal building material for temples in the Theban region. The quarries lie about 65 kilometres north of Aswan, where the river narrows between sandstone cliffs, and over 100 kilometres upstream of Thebes. Granite and granodiorite, the red, pink and grey stones of obelisks, doorways and statues, came from Aswan at the First Cataract. Quartzite, a very hard sandstone, came from Gebel el-Ahmar near Cairo. A Geological Survey of Norway study points to that quarry for the two Colossi of Memnon, each estimated at about 700 to 720 tonnes with its base and about 18 metres tall, though one source argues for Aswan. Egyptian alabaster (banded calcite, or travertine) came from the Hatnub quarry in the Eastern Desert.

Why sandstone? The sources imply, rather than state, the reasons. Gebel el-Silsila yielded large, fracture-free blocks of even colour and texture, and it is among the largest quarries in Egypt. Harrell lists it as worked from the Middle Kingdom through the New Kingdom and again in Ptolemaic and Roman times, so the shift to sandstone in the New Kingdom was about scale, not a first discovery.

Water transport mattered most. Harrell notes it was easier to float a heavy load down the Nile than to sail it up, so stone was taken from upstream quarries. The flood (roughly July to October) let barges get close to building sites by canal.
Sources checked: Harrell, "Building Stones", UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology (2012, escholarship.org/uc/item/3fd124g0); Geological Survey of Norway Special Publication 12 (heavy-mineral study of the Colossi); Wikipedia and worldhistory.org summaries for the Colossi's weight and height (about 720 tonnes, about 18 metres, secondary); several web summaries for Silsila's distance from Aswan (about 65 kilometres, one gives 64); Klemm and Klemm (2008) and Arnold (1991) from the brief but not opened.

## How Egyptian quarrymen cut stone
Kind: reference
About: gebel-silsila-quarries, engelbach-obelisk-study
Soft stone (limestone, sandstone, alabaster) was cut with copper, later bronze, chisels and picks, driven by wooden mallets. Harrell describes the basic method as unchanged from the Old Kingdom to Roman times: vertical trenches were cut along the back and two sides of a block, then the open front was undercut to free it, and the loose block was often trimmed at the quarry. At Gebel el-Silsila, and in some limestone quarries, workers cut galleries and painted lines on the ceilings to track progress, a practice first seen under Amenhotep III. Visitors can spot the marks: parallel diagonal chisel strokes on quarry walls, rows of rectangular scars where blocks were removed, and sometimes blocks left part-attached. The Amarna talatat blocks (about 52 by 26 by 26 centimetres) were cut small enough for one man to carry.

Hard stone (granite, quartzite, hard sandstone) was too tough for copper and bronze. Harrell says stone pounders or mauls were far better, and workers also set fires against granite faces to crack them (fire-setting is documented at Aswan by Bloxam). Dolerite pounders are the classic image: Engelbach described the pounding trenches round the Unfinished Obelisk.

Wedges are debated. Rows of slots along cut blocks are often said to have held wooden wedges, soaked so that they swelled and split the rock. Engelbach (1922) doubted it for Aswan granite: tapering slots would tend to push a wetted wedge out, and he had seen the method work only in an experiment on limestone with parallel-sided slots. What is well attested is later: Harrell says hammered iron wedges set in lines of pre-cut slots were used in the Ptolemaic and Roman periods, especially for the harder limestone and sandstone. Treat swelling wooden wedges as unproven.
Sources checked: Harrell, "Building Stones", UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology (2012); Engelbach, The Aswan Obelisk (1922, Project Gutenberg text); Bloxam, "Quarrying and Mining (Stone)", UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology (read only via a search summary); Arnold (1991) and Clarke and Engelbach (1930) not opened.

## Moving heavy blocks: sledges, wet sand and barges
Kind: reference
About: wet-sand-study, djehutihotep-painting, merer-diary
Heavy stone went on wooden sledges, dragged by teams on ropes over prepared tracks. Harrell notes sledges were the norm in the Dynastic Period, with wagons used only later, and boats whenever the site was far from the quarry.

A famous painting in the tomb of Djehutihotep at Deir el-Bersha (Middle Kingdom, about 1900 to 1850 BC) shows a colossal statue of him on a sledge hauled by 172 men. A man at the front pours liquid on the sand. A commonly quoted figure is about 58 tonnes for the statue, and the accompanying inscription gives its height as 13 cubits, about 6.8 metres; I found the 58 tonnes only in secondary sources and could not trace it to Newberry's 1895 publication. In 2014 a team at the University of Amsterdam led by Daniel Bonn (Fall and others, Physical Review Letters 112, 175502) showed that sand with the right amount of water is about twice as stiff as dry sand, and that the pulling force needed fell in proportion to that stiffness. The university says the right dampness halves the pulling force. Press reports give the best amount as roughly 2 to 5 per cent water by volume, and too much water makes it worse. I could not confirm the exact friction coefficients from the paper itself, so I give none. The study shows the method works; it does not prove it was the only one.

Water carried most of the load. Papyri found at Wadi al-Jarf in 2013 include the diary of an official, Merer, whose crew shipped Tura limestone for Khufu's pyramid by boat about 2560 BC. A commonly quoted estimate of 2.3 million blocks laid in about 20 years works out, on my arithmetic, at about 300 blocks a working day. Estimates of the workforce vary widely.
Sources checked: University of Amsterdam Institute of Physics news release, "prl-egyptian-pyramids" (iop.uva.nl, 2014); Archaeology magazine, "Wet Sand May Have Helped Egyptians Move Pyramid Stones" (2 May 2014); publisher listing for Fall and others, Physical Review Letters 112, 175502 (doi 10.1103/PhysRevLett.112.175502; abstract only via a repository listing); web summaries of Djehutihotep's colossus (58 tonnes, 172 men, 13 cubits; secondary); Harrell (2012); Tallet on Wadi al-Jarf and Lehner (1997) not opened.

## Construction ramps and how pyramids and pylons rose
Kind: reference
About: hatnub-ramp-find, internal-ramp-theory
Without cranes, Egyptians lifted stone by dragging it up slopes. Ramps of mud brick, rubble and earth were the standard tool, and the unfinished state of some temples, tomb paintings and quarries all show them. Emery's UCLA entry on mud-brick architecture notes that construction ramps are described in archaeology, and that a ramp of a given size was planned along with the building.

For pyramids, several theories compete: a single straight ramp (one reaching the Great Pyramid's height of about 146 metres would need a volume close to the pyramid's own), a zigzag ramp up one face, a spiral ramp round the sides, or Jean-Pierre Houdin's proposal (2007) of a ramp inside the pyramid, which is debated and untested. Probably several methods were combined.

The best direct evidence is rare. In 2018 a joint mission of the Institut Francais d'Archeologie Orientale and the University of Liverpool announced an extremely well preserved ramp at the Hatnub alabaster quarry. Two staircases with numerous post holes flank a central ramp. Yannis Gourdon said ropes tied to posts and attached to a sledge let workers pull alabaster blocks out of the quarry on slopes of 20 per cent or more (about one in five). Roland Enmarch dated the system to the reign of Khufu, on tool marks and two inscriptions naming him. Some experts are cautious about drawing a link to the pyramids themselves. A surviving mud-brick ramp at a temple pylon shows the temple method in use; a site section covers it.

Ramps could be dismantled, which is why few remain.
Sources checked: University of Liverpool press release (5 November 2018, via phys.org) quoting Gourdon and Enmarch; University of Liverpool Egyptology blog, "Hatnub" (2018); Archaeology magazine, 31 October 2018; Emery, "Mud-Brick Architecture", UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology (2011); Houdin (2006), Arnold (1991) and Lehner (1997) not opened.

## Foundations: why temples fall down
Kind: reference
About: dieter-arnold
Egyptian temples sit on the Nile floodplain. The ground is silt and sand over a water table that rises and falls with the river. Where the builders could, they cut to bedrock. In many temples they did not: they dug a trench, packed it with sand or rubble and laid shallow courses of stone on top. Sand spreads the load and absorbs shocks, so it is a reasonable foundation for a moderately heavy building, but it needs to stay put.

Trouble starts with water. Groundwater carries salt up through the lowest stones. As it evaporates, salt crystals grow in the pores and split the stone, a process called salt weathering. This is the main reason many temple bases crumble. Reused small blocks, packed in as filler, are weak. Over time the foundation sinks unevenly and columns lean. In 1899 eleven columns of the Great Hypostyle Hall at Karnak fell in a chain reaction. Explanations combine weak reused blocks, salt-laden groundwater and, in one view, disturbance from earlier trenches. The detail is disputed; site sections cover it.

The modern cure is to control water. Karnak and other Theban temples have had drainage and dewatering schemes in recent decades, using pumps and drains to lower and manage the water table (exact dates and layouts are not given here). The Aswan High Dam (completed 1970) ended the annual flood, so the water table now stays high all year round, which worsens the salt problem.
Sources: general knowledge for the Aswan High Dam and for salt weathering; Arnold (1991), Nicholson and Shaw (2000) and the UCLA Encyclopedia entry on temple foundations were NOT opened in this pass, so foundation details are general and unverified; the 1899 collapse is covered by knowledge/karnak/notes.md.

## Columns, architraves and roofs: why stone limits spans
Kind: reference
About: dieter-arnold
Egyptian temples are post-and-lintel buildings: columns carry horizontal beams (architraves), and roof slabs rest across them. There are no true stone arches in temples. Stone is strong when squeezed but weak when pulled. A beam on two supports sags slightly, which puts its underside in tension, and stone fails there.

Bending stress grows with the square of the span and falls with the depth of the beam: double the span and the stress quadruples. Sandstone's tensile strength is only a few megapascals and granite's roughly 5 to 15 (textbook ranges, general knowledge), so spans stay short. A firm example is the King's Chamber in the Great Pyramid: it is 5.2 metres wide, and nine granite beams run across that width, projecting beyond the walls. Estimates of their weights differ, from a few tens of tonnes to about 80 tonnes. I found no source giving a general maximum for Egyptian stone beams, so none is given here. This is why Egyptian halls are crowded with columns: the "forest" is structural. Where more width was needed, builders built architraves from several blocks, even stacked two layers deep.

Columns were built in drums or half-drums and were usually carved to the final profile after assembly, with a plumb line centring each drum. That earth was heaped round the columns and used as a working platform is a theory, supported at Karnak but not shown everywhere. Taller central columns allowed a raised roof with stone window grilles, the clerestory, which let in some light.
Sources checked: NOVA Online and ancientegyptonline.co.uk for the King's Chamber (width 5.20 metres, nine granite beams; weights from the University of South Florida image archive, not authoritative); Arnold (1991) and Clarke and Engelbach (1930) not opened; granite and sandstone strengths are general knowledge.

## Walls and pylons: stone skin, rubble heart
Kind: reference
About: dieter-arnold
A temple pylon, the great gateway of two sloping towers, looks like a solid mass of stone. It is mostly not. The outer faces were built of dressed blocks with carefully bonded courses, but the interior was often filled with rubble, sand, chips or reused blocks. This saved quarrying and carving, and the heavy core held the thinner face in place.

The faces were battered, meaning they lean inward as they rise, usually by a few degrees. A battered face is much more stable than a vertical one, and it lowers the centre of gravity of the whole tower. It also gives the Egyptian look, with a torus roll at the corners and a cavetto cornice at the top. The pylon faces carried deep recesses for flagpoles, held by timber masts. Joints were often tied with wooden dovetail cramps set across them, though their holes are all that survive in most places.

Reused fill explains why Karnak has given up so many treasures. When Amenhotep III built the Third Pylon, about 1370 BC, he dismantled earlier chapels and used the blocks as filler. Archaeologists such as Legrain and Chevrier took them out in the early twentieth century and the White Chapel and the Red Chapel were then reassembled for the Open Air Museum. Small blocks called talatat, carved for Akhenaten's temples, were reused in the same way in later pylons. Sources describe this as demolition and reuse; they do not generally call it deliberate hiding.
Sources: Harrell, "Building Stones", UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology (2012) for talatat dimensions; knowledge/karnak/notes.md for the Karnak chapels; Arnold (1991) and Clarke and Engelbach (1930) not opened, so the descriptions of battered faces, rubble cores and cramps are general knowledge.

## Obelisks and the physics of a very long stone
Kind: reference
About: engelbach-obelisk-study
An obelisk is a single tapering granite shaft with a pointed tip, the pyramidion. The Unfinished Obelisk at Aswan would have been about 42 metres long (Engelbach measured 41.75 metres in 1922) and about 1,100 to 1,200 tonnes (his 1,168 English tons is about 1,190 tonnes; other sources say about 1,090). Cracks appeared while it was being cut out and it was abandoned. There is no firm date. Engelbach found nothing to date it and left the question open; the Egyptian Ministry's monument page says it is believed to have been commissioned by Hatshepsut, but that is conjecture. A site section covers quarrying and raising.

The physics is unforgiving. Granite weighs about 2.7 tonnes per cubic metre. As a worked estimate of my own, a 30-metre shaft about 2.5 metres across at the base has a mass of a few hundred tonnes. Hoisted as a beam supported only at its ends, its own weight gives a peak bending stress of about 7 megapascals (0.75 times density times gravity times span squared, divided by width, treating it as a uniform square bar). Granite's tensile strength is only about 5 to 15 megapascals (general knowledge), and a hidden flaw lowers it. So a heavy shaft must be supported along its length, and a hidden crack is dangerous. The pyramid-shaped tip and the taper also move the centre of gravity below mid-length, which matters when tipping it upright.

Cracks are why the Unfinished Obelisk failed: trenches pounded round it exposed flaws, and the stone could not be saved. Even the ministry page puts abandonment down to fissures and flaws.
Sources checked: Engelbach, The Aswan Obelisk (1922, Project Gutenberg text; length, weight, no firm date); Egyptian Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities, "The Unfinished Obelisk" (egymonuments.gov.eg); knowledge/karnak/notes.md for the site's wording; stress figure is my own worked estimate; Klemm and Klemm (2008) not opened.

## Mud brick: the everyday building material
Kind: reference
About: rekhmire-brickmaking
Most ancient Egyptian buildings were mud brick: houses, palaces, fortresses, town walls and the great temple enclosure walls. Only temples and tombs were stone. Emery's UCLA entry credits its easy and economical production and universal availability. Bricks were made from Nile silt, often with chopped straw as temper, moulded and left to dry in the sun. Mortar was similar mud, used in horizontal joints. A tomb painting of Rekhmire (about 1450 BC) shows brickmaking, with bricks moved from where they were made to the site.

Sizes varied by place and period. One summary gives a standardised Middle Kingdom brick of about 30 by 15 by 7.5 centimetres. I did not find a source for New Kingdom sizes, so none is given. As a worked estimate of mine, a Middle Kingdom brick of that size weighs about 6 kilograms. Royal names were stamped on many bricks in temples.

Late Period temple enclosure walls, as at Karnak and Elkab, were built in sections that alternate convex and concave (or concave and horizontal), not with straight horizontal courses; Emery gives Dendera's north wall as an example, citing Arnold, Spencer and others. This is called pan-bedding or wavy bedding. Why is debated; suggestions in the literature include flexibility against settling and earthquakes, saving material and a symbolic link to the primeval waters. I did not find a source that settles it.

Mud brick lasts well in dry air but not in damp ground or rain, which is why surviving brick ramps are rare.
Sources checked: Emery, "Mud-Brick Architecture", UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology (2011, escholarship.org/uc/item/4983w678); web summary for the Middle Kingdom brick size (secondary); Spencer, Brick Architecture in Ancient Egypt (1979) and Nicholson and Shaw (2000) not opened; explanations for wavy walls are general suggestions, not individually sourced.

## Surveying and setting out
Kind: reference
About: turin-plan-ramesses-iv, flinders-petrie
Egyptians measured in cubits. The royal cubit was about 52.3 centimetres, divided into 7 palms, each of 4 fingers (28 fingers in all). A shorter common cubit was about 45 centimetres (6 palms). Rods of wood and stone marked with these units survive.

The foundation ceremony was called "stretching the cord". A king and the goddess Seshat are shown driving pegs and stretching a cord to mark the corners, as in reliefs at Edfu and Dendera. Cords and set squares laid out right angles. A popular idea is that rope stretchers made right angles with a 3, 4, 5 triangle. No Egyptian text says so, so it is a modern theory.

Alignment was astronomical. The Great Pyramid's sides face the cardinal points within a few minutes of arc. Egyptians used the merkhet, a plumb-bob sighting tool, and the bay, a palm rib with a sighting slit, to line up on stars. Theories for how north was found include sighting on two circumpolar stars and using shadows of the sun; none is proved. The level base is also remarkable, flat within a couple of centimetres. Lehner suggests that water-filled trenches round the base worked as a level. It is a theory.

Planning evidence is real. Unfinished walls show guide lines and squared grids in red ochre, used for drawing figures to proportion. Ostraca (pot and limestone flakes) carry plans and sketches. The Turin papyrus shows the plan of the tomb of Ramesses IV (about 1150 BC), with measurements.
Sources: Harrell (2012) for the 52 centimetre cubit implied by talatat blocks; Arnold (1991), Lehner (1997), Nicholson and Shaw (2000) and the UCLA Encyclopedia entries on weights and measures NOT opened in this pass, so the royal cubit (about 52.3 centimetres), alignment theories, Giza levelness and Turin plan details are general knowledge and unverified.

## Egyptian building tools: copper, bronze and sand
Kind: reference
About: iron-in-egypt, flinders-petrie
Egyptian masons used copper, often with a little arsenic, for chisels, adzes and saws from early times. Bronze, which is harder, became common later. Harrell writes that throughout the Dynastic Period, until near the end of the Late Period, building stones were quarried with copper and later bronze chisels and picks. Iron was known, mostly as a rarity, from the end of the Eighteenth Dynasty. A museum glossary says iron objects gradually increased and by the Twenty-sixth Dynasty (664 to 525 BC) were as common as bronze. Quarry tools of "iron" (low-grade steel) replaced bronze and stone only toward the end of the Late Period, and iron wedges appear in the Ptolemaic and Roman periods. Most of Karnak was therefore built with copper, bronze and stone tools.

None of these cuts granite. It was pounded with stone mauls and ground. For sawing and drilling, workers used abrasive sand with a copper saw or tubular drill, so the sand does the cutting. Petrie suggested jewelled teeth in the blade; Denys Stocks's experiments are reported to have shown that sand alone works, though slowly.

Other tools were simple. Wooden mallets, levers and sledges moved stone, and ropes were of papyrus or fibre. A set square checked corners and a plumb bob checked verticals. Surviving examples include tools from the tomb of Kha at Deir el-Medina, now in Turin.
Sources checked: Harrell, "Building Stones", UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology (2012); Global Egyptian Museum glossary entry on iron (globalegyptianmuseum.org); Napierala (2023) abstract on Late Period iron working (via search summary); Stocks (2003), Nicholson and Shaw (2000) not opened.
