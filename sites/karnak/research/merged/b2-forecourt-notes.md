## The Triple Shrine of Sety II (the "Chapels of Sety I")
Kind: site
About: sety-ii-shrine, sety-ii
The small three-roomed building on the north (left) side of the great court is the triple bark shrine of Sety II, a Nineteenth Dynasty king who reigned from about 1200 to 1194 BC (Shaw's chronology). The on-site board calls it the "Chapels of Sety I". Scholarly sources disagree with the board: Digital Karnak and Henri Chevrier, who published the shrine in 1940 as "Le temple reposoir de Séti II", both give it to Sety II. Sety I (reigned about 1294 to 1279 BC) built the Hypostyle Hall just beyond, which may explain the mix-up, but that is a guess.

It is a bark shrine: a resting place for the portable boats (barks) of the gods when they were carried out of the temple on festival days. Its three sanctuary rooms were for Amun in the middle, Mut on the west and Khonsu on the east, the Theban triad. Sullivan writes that the boats "would have paused here during festival journeys before crossing the Nile". Digital Karnak adds that it may also have been a place where ordinary Egyptians could gather within the temple complex and pray.

When Sety II built it, it stood outside the temple's western entrance, in front of Horemheb's Second Pylon, on the east-west processional route. Ramesses III later built his own bark shrine opposite it. About 250 years later, Shoshenq I (945 to 924 BC) built a huge court around both shrines, so what was outside is now inside.

The building is small and survives almost complete. Digital Karnak gives its size as 21 metres across, 16.5 metres deep and 7.1 metres high, with a central doorway 4.5 metres high and side doorways 3.79 metres. The foundations, door sills and first course of blocks are quartzite, a very hard stone, and the rest is sandstone. The north-east corner and the west wall were left undressed, which suggests the work was never finished. The walls carry relief scenes of the king, and on the south face and entrance Sety II is shown offering to the Theban gods.

Digital Karnak notes that the shrine may have been partly dismantled when Nectanebo I (380 to 362 BC) built the First Pylon, then only partly rebuilt. It says "may", and the evidence is not given on the page.

What to look for: three doorways side by side, the king's figure in the carved scenes, and the plain, unfinished stonework on the less visible sides.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Sety II Shrine (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/Sety-II-Shrine); Sullivan, "The Development of the Temple of Karnak" (Digital Karnak, 2010), phase summaries for Sety II and Shoshenq I; Shaw (ed.), Oxford History of Ancient Egypt (2000), reign dates; site board (source 9); Chevrier 1940 cited in the Digital Karnak bibliography (not read).

## The Kiosk of Taharqa (the single column)
Kind: site
About: taharqa-kiosk, taharqa
The tall single column in the middle of the great court is the only one still standing of ten that made up the Kiosk of Taharqa, built between 690 and 664 BC (Shaw's chronology). The site board calls it the "Pavilion of Taharqa". Taharqa was a Kushite king of the Twenty-fifth Dynasty, from Nubia in modern Sudan.

Digital Karnak describes a free-standing structure of two rows of five large open-papyrus columns, joined by low screen walls, with doorways on all four sides. The overall footprint was about 20.5 metres by 29 metres. Each column was 2.99 metres across and about 21 metres high. The materials were limestone and rose granite, with a calcite (Egyptian alabaster) shrine at the centre which supported the bark of the god. Sullivan notes that the width of the kiosk, over 16 metres, "could not have been bridged with a roof", so scholars generally agree it was left open to the sky. It is possible that stone beams linked the tops of the columns. This is the clearest evidence about roofing in the court (see the section on the great court).

Excavations suggest Taharqa's kiosk replaced an earlier wooden kiosk on the same spot, and the alabaster altar is thought to belong to that earlier building. Much later, Ptolemy IV added decoration to the screen walls showing nome-gods (the gods of Egypt's regions) in procession, and the rose granite paving may have been added then too.

What it was for is unclear. Some scholars think it was used in the "union of the disc", a yearly rite involving the cult statue of Amun-Ra. Jean Lauffray, who studied it in 1970 and 1975, titled his work on it a "colonnade-propylée", a columned entrance porch, but Digital Karnak does not adopt a single explanation.

Taharqa also appears in the Bible as "Tirhakah king of Ethiopia" (2 Kings 19:9), who set out against the Assyrian king Sennacherib. That story is set in 701 BC, but Taharqa did not become king until about 690 BC (Shaw). Scholars explain this in different ways: that he was then a young prince leading an army for an older king, that the biblical writer used the title he later held, or that the text refers to a different campaign. The question is debated and is not settled (secondary summary only, verify).

What to look for: the huge papyrus-flower capital, the drums of the shaft (built up of stone courses), the stumps of the other nine columns and the calcite altar.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Taharqo Kiosk (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/Taharqo-Kiosk); Sullivan, "The Development of the Temple of Karnak" (2010), Taharqo entry; Digital Karnak, Shoshenq I Court; Shaw (2000); Wikipedia, Taharqa, as a pointer to Kitchen (1996) and Pope (2022) on 701 BC (secondary, not checked against those works).

## The Temple of Ramesses III in the forecourt
Kind: site
About: ramesses-iii-temple, ramesses-iii
On the south (right) side of the great court is a complete small temple built by Ramesses III, who reigned about 1184 to 1153 BC (Shaw's chronology). The site board lists it as "Temple of Ramesses III". Digital Karnak calls it a bark shrine: it was built to house the sacred barks for the gods' festival day journeys. It sits at right angles to the traditional east-west processional route and was set opposite the shrine of Sety II.

The temple takes the shape and size of a small temple. Digital Karnak gives its overall size as 27.5 metres across, 60.5 metres deep and 14 metres high. From the court you reach it through a small pylon carved with scenes of the king smiting his enemies, with a granite gateway and no flagstaffs. Beyond lies an open court with pillars carrying statues of the king, a small vestibule, a hypostyle hall 21.25 metres wide and 9 metres deep, and a sanctuary. Instead of a single holy of holies, the sanctuary has three bark shrines, for Amun, Mut and Khonsu.

The statues in the court show the king in the form of the god Osiris. Digital Karnak says "sixteen engaged statues" stood on the pillars. Other descriptions give eight, which may count one side only. I could not settle the count from sources I could open, so treat it as uncertain and let the family count them.

It is a good model for the ruined parts of Karnak. Almost all the other temples here survive as plans or fragments. Here a pylon, a court, a hall and sanctuary rooms stand together at small scale, so you can see how the standard pieces of an Egyptian temple fitted.

About 240 years after it was built, Shoshenq I built his great court around the front part of the temple, so the pylon end is now inside the court. The Oriental Institute's Epigraphic Survey published the temple's walls in "Reliefs and Inscriptions at Karnak" (1936 and later), and Chevrier published the building in 1933. Ramesses III also began the Temple of Khonsu in the south of the precinct.

The inner rooms may be gated. If so, the court, pylon and sanctuary doors are all visible from the doorway.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Ramesses III Temple (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/Ramesses-III-Temple); Sullivan, "The Development of the Temple of Karnak" (2010); Epigraphic Survey, Reliefs and Inscriptions at Karnak I (OIP 25, 1936), landing page only; Shaw (2000); Madain Project and Smithsonian record for the differing statue counts (secondary).

## The Bubastite Portal and Shoshenq I's campaign relief
Kind: site
About: bubastite-portal, shoshenq-campaign, shoshenq-i
In the south-east corner of the great court a side doorway leads out to the south. It is called the Bubastite Portal, after Bubastis in the Nile Delta, home city of the king who built it, Shoshenq I (945 to 924 BC, Shaw). Digital Karnak says the doorway is just under 5 metres wide. The Epigraphic Survey (1954) describes it as the south entrance to the first court, designed as a uniform part of the court's wall and colonnade, built to fill the space between the Second Pylon and the Ramesses III temple. Two closed papyrus columns inside the court mark it.

The relief is carved on the outer (south) face of the wall, beside and over the doorway. It shows Shoshenq, the largest figure, striding forward to smite his enemies. Amun and the goddess of Thebes hold cords that bind the hands and throats of rows of captives. Each captive is a name ring, an oval shaped like a crenellated city wall, holding the name of a town or people. Digital Karnak counts 156 rings, while an introduction in Wikipedia gives 155 and notes many names are lost. The relief is dated to about 925 BC.

It matters because it is a rare Egyptian account of Canaan in the tenth century BC. Shoshenq is usually identified with Shishak, who took treasures from Jerusalem in 1 Kings 14:25 to 26 and 2 Chronicles 12. Jerusalem is not among the surviving names on the list. Scholars have proposed that it was erased, that Rehoboam's tribute saved the city, or that the list was partly copied from an older king. The identification is widely accepted but debated, and so is how historical the biblical account is: Israel Finkelstein treats the plunder story as a theological construct, and newer excavation dating at Tel Gezer fits the biblical date poorly (all via Wikipedia, verify).

A quarry stela at Gebel el-Silsila, dated to Shoshenq's year 21 and published by Caminos in 1952, records a new quarry for a pylon and festival hall at Karnak, for "the first campaign of victory". A fragment of a victory stela with his name was found at Megiddo in 1925, though some now suggest it may be architectural.

Digital Karnak records that no attempt was made to repair the broken or damaged areas of the relief, so what you see is the surviving original.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Bubastite Portal (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/bubastite-portal); Sullivan, "The Development of the Temple of Karnak" (2010); Epigraphic Survey, Reliefs and Inscriptions at Karnak III: The Bubastite Portal (OIP 74, 1954), introductory text; Wikipedia, Bubastite Portal and Shoshenq I, as pointers to Caminos (1952), Wilson (2005), Finkelstein (2002, 2006) and Webster and others (2023), not checked against those works; Shaw (2000).

## Shoshenq I's court (the great court or first court)
Kind: site
About: shoshenq-court, shoshenq-i
The great open court between the First and Second Pylons is the work of Shoshenq I (945 to 924 BC). The site board labels it the "Bubastite Courtyard". Digital Karnak gives it as 82 metres wide and 101 metres deep. It enclosed the shrine of Sety II and the front of the temple of Ramesses III, which had stood outside the earlier entrance. Its north and south sides were lined with sandstone columns with papyrus-bud capitals. The western side had a monumental gate with an opening of 17.7 metres and a total height of 27.5 metres. A stela of his year 21 describes the project as a "festival court for the house of his father Amun-Ra".

Nectanebo I (380 to 362 BC) removed the court's west wall to build the First Pylon. Parts of the side walls and columns on the far west were taken down for construction ramps, then rebuilt with new stone, and the work was left unfinished: the column drums there are undressed. Remnants of the mud-brick ramp still stand in the court. Only the west wall has gone.

The ram-headed sphinxes along the sides may have been moved here when the court was built or when Taharqa added his kiosk. Sullivan says that today's arrangement dates from the Roman reorganisation of the entrance.

Was the court ever roofed? I found no source that says so. The sources describe an open court. The only structure that is explicitly said to be unroofed is Taharqa's kiosk, whose 16 metre span was too wide to roof. A court 82 by 101 metres could not have been roofed in stone with Egyptian beams, but that is my inference. The side colonnades and the rooms of the Ramesses III temple and Sety II shrine would have had roofs, and Digital Karnak does not say which roof slabs survive. Treat the roofing of the colonnades as unconfirmed.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Shoshenq I Court (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/?p=4245); Sullivan, "The Development of the Temple of Karnak" (2010); Digital Karnak, Taharqo Kiosk; Shaw (2000); site board (source 9).

## Bark shrines and festival journeys
Kind: reference
About: sety-ii-shrine, ramesses-iii-temple
Egyptian gods were believed to live in their cult statues. On festival days the statue was placed in a small shrine on a model boat, called a bark, and carried on poles on the shoulders of priests. The most famous journeys at Karnak were to Luxor Temple at the Opet festival and across the Nile to the west bank for the Beautiful Festival of the Valley (general knowledge).

Along the route, small bark shrines gave the boats a place to rest. Digital Karnak says Sety II's shrine and Ramesses III's temple were each a bark station of this kind, placed on the western approach to the temple in front of Horemheb's Second Pylon. Sullivan's account of the Sety II shrine says the boats of Amun, Mut and Khonsu "would have paused here during festival journeys before crossing the Nile". Ramesses III's version grew into a small temple, with a court, hall and three sanctuaries.

Taharqa's kiosk had a calcite shrine at its centre which supported the bark of the god. It may have served the same purpose.
Sources: Sullivan, "The Development of the Temple of Karnak" (2010); Digital Karnak pages for Sety II Shrine, Ramesses III Temple and Taharqo Kiosk; general knowledge for the festival background.

## Shoshenq I (the biblical Shishak)
Kind: person
About: shoshenq-i, bubastite-portal
Shoshenq I founded the Twenty-second (Libyan) Dynasty. He reigned from about 945 to 924 BC in Shaw's chronology, which Kitchen also used. Other scholars give different dates: Krauss argued for 943 BC and Schneider for 962 to 941 BC (from Wikipedia's summary, verify). His family came from Bubastis in the Delta and descended from Libyans who had settled in Egypt (general knowledge).

At Karnak he built the great court and carved the Bubastite Portal relief. A quarry stela at Gebel el-Silsila dated to his year 21 records a new quarry for a pylon and festival hall at Karnak, overseen by his architect Horemakhet. Much of his Karnak work was left unfinished, apart from the campaign scenes.

The campaign is usually dated about 925 BC. He is generally identified with Shishak (1 Kings 14:25 to 26; 2 Chronicles 12:2 to 9), who attacked Judah in the fifth year of Rehoboam. The Karnak list names places from the Negev to Galilee but not Jerusalem. The identification has its critics, and so has the usual date: Finkelstein has argued that it rests on the biblical reference alone.
Sources: Digital Karnak, Bubastite Portal and Shoshenq I Court; Wikipedia, Shoshenq I, as a pointer to Caminos (1952), Krauss, Schneider, Finkelstein (2006) (not checked against the originals); Shaw (2000).

## Taharqa
Kind: person
About: taharqa, taharqa-kiosk
Taharqa was a king of the Kushite Twenty-fifth Dynasty, which came from Nubia and ruled Egypt as well as Kush. His reign is dated about 690 to 664 BC (Shaw). He is named in the Bible as Tirhakah (2 Kings 19:9, Isaiah 37:9). He built at Karnak, notably the kiosk in the great court, a porch of twenty columns at the Khonsu temple and another at the eastern temple of Ramesses II (Sullivan). He is also known for a large pyramid at Nuri in Sudan (Wikipedia, as a pointer; verify).

Assyria invaded Egypt during his reign: Esarhaddon took Memphis in 671 BC and Taharqa fled south, and Ashurbanipal's forces later reached Thebes. He died in 664 BC (Shaw), and the Assyrian sack of Thebes is dated 664 BC in the tour's timeline. The Kawa stelae, texts from his temple in Nubia, say a predecessor sent young Nubians north to Thebes and Taharqa was among them. One reading puts him at about twenty then, though scholars warn the text is not a reliable chronicle (Wikipedia's summary of Broekman 2015, verify).
Sources: Sullivan, "The Development of the Temple of Karnak" (2010); Shaw (2000); Wikipedia, Taharqa, as a pointer to Kitchen (1996), Pope (2022) and Welsby (1996) (not checked).

## Sety II and Ramesses III
Kind: person
About: sety-ii, ramesses-iii, sety-ii-shrine, ramesses-iii-temple
Sety II (reigned about 1200 to 1194 BC, Shaw) built the triple bark shrine, in front of the Second Pylon. Ramesses III (about 1184 to 1153 BC) built his bark temple opposite it, and the Temple of Khonsu in the south. Only about ten years separate the end of Sety II's reign from the start of Ramesses III's (Shaw), though Sullivan notes the two shrines were added roughly a century after Horemheb's Second Pylon. The two buildings show a difference of ambition: Sety II's is a plain three-roomed shrine, while Ramesses III's grew into a miniature temple with its own pylon, court and hall. Between them they gave the temple's western approach two bark stations facing each other, until Shoshenq I enclosed both in his court.

Ramesses III is the last great warrior king of the New Kingdom. His main monument is his mortuary temple at Medinet Habu on the west bank, and Papyrus Harris I records the wealth he gave to Amun's temples (general knowledge, verify). At Karnak, the Epigraphic Survey of the Oriental Institute published his bark temple in Reliefs and Inscriptions at Karnak, volumes I and II.
Sources: Sullivan, "The Development of the Temple of Karnak" (2010); Shaw (2000); Digital Karnak, Ramesses III Temple; general knowledge for Medinet Habu and Papyrus Harris I.
