## The Quay and Tribune (the temple's old harbour)
Kind: site
About: quay, sety-ii-obelisk, nile-level-texts, taharqa-quay-ramp, hakoris-bark-chapel
The quay is the stone landing stage at the western end of the temple, with a raised platform, the tribune, at its centre. On the on-site board it is feature 1, the Port of the Temple. Today it overlooks dry land, but for well over a thousand years the Nile flowed right up to it. Boats carrying the statue of Amun tied up here, and a road lined with ram-headed sphinxes led from the quay to the temple gate.

What you can see. On the tribune stands a small quartzite obelisk carved for Sety II, who reigned about 1200 to 1194 BC. Catalogue entries give 3.3 and 3.57 metres, so say "about 3.5 metres". Sety II's name is carved on all four sides, and it was probably one of a pair. The tribune was once dated to the Nineteenth Dynasty partly because of these obelisks, but its date is uncertain. A stele of Sety II is also recorded here.

When was the quay built? Elaine Sullivan, in the UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology, writes that a new quay and a huge revetment (a retaining wall faced with stone) began about the time of Shoshenq I, around 945 BC. The stone wall shows that the Nile then flowed right up to its edge, in front of Shoshenq's new entrance. Excavators led by Mansour Boraik found the wall about 50 metres south of the present quay, preserved to 3.5 metres high. Inscriptions suggest it was built in the Twenty-second Dynasty, with building and repairs continuing into the fourth century BC. Michel Ghilardi, a geoarchaeologist, also dates the quay to the early 900s BC, but calls this a hypothesis. A Wikipedia article says the terrace was built under Ramesses II; it is a pointer only and disagrees with the excavators.

The flood records. Kings from the Twenty-second Dynasty onwards had the height of the annual Nile flood cut into the quay, dated by regnal year. Georges Legrain copied the texts in 1896, and Jürgen von Beckerath and Gerardus Broekman later studied them. Many are worn or lost.

Later changes. In the Twenty-fifth Dynasty the Kushite king Taharqa built a paved stone ramp south of the quay, running down into the river. Texts inside it show it was used for water rituals, including the New Year festival. Ghilardi describes it as built over the quay to ease the arrival of the royal barque. Under Hakoris (reigned 393 to 380 BC), a small boat chapel stood outside the west gate, on the site of the later First Pylon. Its extra wide west door let the god's boat be lifted from the river and carried straight in, still parallel to the bank. The Romans repaved the quay and the road, reusing older blocks.

The river. A narrow channel linking the quay to the main Nile was still in use in late Roman times and was given up in the Byzantine and Islamic periods (Ghilardi 2015). See "The Nile at Karnak" for how the river moved.
Sources: Sullivan, UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology, "Karnak: Development of the Temple of Amun-Ra" (2010), pp. 14 to 22; Ghilardi, "The Nile Valley in Upper Egypt during the Dynastic period (ancient Karnak area)" (2015); Wikidata entry and Wikimedia Commons categories for the obelisk and stele of Seti II (pointers only; the Wikidata entry cites the Karnak database SITH, which could not be opened); Legrain 1896, von Beckerath 1966 and Broekman 2002 as listed on Wikipedia, "Nile Level Texts" (pointer only); on-site board (source 9).

## The Nile at Karnak (how the river moved)
Kind: reference
About: quay, first-pylon
Karnak was not always beside the river, and it was not always on dry land that stayed dry. Sediment cores show the temple began on a sandy river terrace that stood as an island between Nile channels. The island was about 10 hectares, and the terrace is about 72 metres above sea level. Pennington and colleagues (Antiquity, 2025) took 61 cores, up to 11.65 metres deep, and date the first permanent occupation to the Old Kingdom, after about 2520 BC, give or take 420 years.

In the early Middle Kingdom, about 2000 BC, a western channel 90 to 150 metres wide ran so close that its eastern bank lay near the present Third Pylon and within the courtyards of the Seventh to Tenth Pylons. A major eastern branch, 220 to 500 metres wide in the Third Intermediate Period, ran to the east. Minor channels crossed the terrace, one near the First Pylon. The western channel silted up in stages, its south first, and the main Nile migrated west to where it runs today. Each strip of new land let the temple grow west: the Seventh to Tenth Pylons and the First to Third Pylons stand on ground the river gave up. Charloux and colleagues (Antiquity, 2021), excavating in the Ptah temple, link the temple's formation about 2200 to 2000 BC to the retreating riverbank. Pennington and colleagues cite a shift away from river cutting at Luxor about 4,000 years ago.

Ghilardi drilled a 25-metre borehole in front of the First Pylon. About 18 metres of it is Nile sediment with pottery, brick fragments and wood, from about 1450 BC (a wood fragment gave 1494 to 1402 BC) to about AD 350. Nile deposits west of the temples are about 20 metres thick, against under 6 metres to the east.

Texts and pictures agree. A painting in the Theban tomb of Neferhotep (TT 49) shows a T-shaped basin joined to the Nile by a canal on the temple's west side, so the river was in the Eighteenth Dynasty close enough to dig a harbour. By the Twenty-second Dynasty the river was at the quay, and it stayed in reach until Roman times.

Disagreements. François Larché has suggested that the Nile ran east of the temples in the early second millennium BC; Ghilardi calls this an assumption to check. The main published reconstruction used here is Pennington and colleagues (2025). Eliot's own geoarchaeology plan, with its AS cores and P profiles, could not be identified, so none of its labels are used; the AS core names in Pennington and colleagues (for example AS012 and AS040) show the same project's naming, but that is not proof of a match.
Sources: Pennington and others, "Conceptual origins and geomorphic evolution of the temple of Amun-Ra at Karnak (Luxor, Egypt)", Antiquity 99(408), 2025, 1535 to 1549 (figures 1 to 4); Charloux and others, "Shifting Nile and the origins and development of ancient Karnak", Antiquity 95(382), 2021, 919 to 939; Ghilardi (2015), chapter 12 (figures 3 and 4); Sullivan, UEE (2010), pp. 14 to 15.

## Avenue of ram-headed sphinxes
Kind: site
About: rams-avenue
Forty ram-headed sphinxes line the approach to the First Pylon, and fifty-two more stand inside the great court beyond. Each is a lion's body with the head of a ram, the sacred animal of Amun. They are sandstone, about 3.6 to 3.8 metres long and 1.1 to 1.2 metres wide, on pedestals 1.35 by 1.70 metres. A few keep traces of original paint. Between the front paws of each stands a small figure of Ramesses II as Osiris, with his titles on it. The ram shelters the king; this is the usual reading of the pose, not a statement from a Karnak text.

Whose are they? The traditional answer is Ramesses II (reigned 1279 to 1213 BC), whose name is on the statuettes. Agnès Cabrol argues that the sphinxes were carved under Thutmose IV and Amenhotep III on style, stood at Luxor Temple, were usurped by Ramesses II when he altered that temple, and reached Karnak only in the Twenty-first Dynasty, when Pinedjem I added his own inscriptions to the bases. Sullivan reports both versions. Digital Karnak follows the second.

How the avenue changed. Pinedjem may have placed a line of 100 or more along a road of about 200 metres from the old temple gate, the Second Pylon, to the quay. About 945 BC Shoshenq I built the great court, which cut the line nearly in half, so some sphinxes were moved to line its north and south walls (a later move, perhaps when Taharqa's kiosk was added). Nectanebo I built the First Pylon across the road. In Roman times the sphinxes outside were regrouped as seen today, and a gap was left between the seventh and eighth for access to buildings south of the quay.

Do not confuse this avenue with two others. Tutankhamun's ram sphinxes on the road to the Mut precinct were recarved from earlier figures, probably of Akhenaten and Nefertiti. The Luxor avenue is separate: see "The Avenue of Sphinxes to Luxor".

Look for the tiny king under a ram's chin; the closest pair show it best.
Sources: Digital Karnak, "Western Processional Way" (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/?p=4300; cites Cabrol 2001, Lauffray 1971, Murnane 1979) and "Other Processional Ways" (?p=4531); "Shoshenq I Court" (?p=4245); Sullivan, UEE (2010), pp. 17 to 18 and 21 to 22; Wikimedia Commons file descriptions only for photographs.

## The First Pylon
Kind: site
About: first-pylon, nectanebo-enclosure
The First Pylon is the biggest pylon in Egypt and was never finished. Nectanebo I (reigned 380 to 362 BC, Thirtieth Dynasty) began it as the monumental western entrance to the temple. It is about 113 metres wide and 14.5 metres thick at the base. The south tower rises 45 courses of stone, about 31.65 metres; the north tower only 32 courses, about 21.7 metres. Finished, the towers would have been 38 to 40 metres high. Digital Karnak's wording for the 113 metres is ambiguous, so treat the width as that of the whole front.

Why is it unfinished? Egyptian builders raised the masonry first, using ramps, then dressed and carved the surface from the top down as the ramps came away. The work stopped before that stage, so the front is bare and the upper edge ragged. The sources used do not say why work stopped; Nectanebo I's reign ended in 362 BC.

What it replaced. Shoshenq I's court (about 945 BC) had a western wall and gateway. The sources say Nectanebo removed the wall but kept the unfinished central stone gateway, 27.5 metres high and 7.4 metres wide, and built the pylon around it. Digital Karnak thinks Shoshenq I probably built the gateway. A smaller doorway, 10.85 metres high, was fitted in later, with wooden doors.

The wall. Nectanebo also enclosed the Amun-Ra temple, and the Mut and Montu temples, in huge mud-brick walls. The Amun-Ra wall was about 21 metres high and 12 metres thick, of mud brick and sandstone; he planned twelve gates and only four were finished. Digital Karnak suggests the pylon and wall may have been meant to protect the temple from foreign invaders.

Details. Look for the tall vertical slots in the front. Great wooden flagstaffs with cloth banners stood in notches in the stone, held by clamps. The front faces west, so at 08:30 it is in shade; the afternoon sun lights it.

Dates. Digital Karnak and Sullivan give 380 to 362 BC. The Historical Marker Database says about 350 BC, and one Commons description says it "may have been built by Nectanebo I" without certainty. An unsourced blog says Shoshenq I planned it as a copy of the Second Pylon; Sullivan says only that he began a gateway.
Sources: Digital Karnak, "1st Pylon" (digitalkarnak.ucsc.edu/1st-pylon; cites Hölscher 1943, Carlotti 1995, Lauffray 1970) and "Enclosures and Gates" (?p=730); Sullivan, UEE (2010), pp. 17 and 21; Great Hypostyle Hall Project, "Constructing the Hall" (University of Memphis).

## Builders' ramp behind the First Pylon
Kind: site
About: builders-ramp, first-pylon
The sloping heap of mud brick against the inside of the First Pylon, in the great court, is what is left of the ramp used to build it. Digital Karnak says plainly that remains of the mud-brick ramps "can still be seen inside the court today". The pylon was never completed, and the ramps were never taken down. The Great Hypostyle Hall Project captions its photograph "Remains of the mud-brick ramp used in the construction of the First Pylon". The stop sits on the south side, behind the south tower (the position is from photographs and map placement, not stated by Digital Karnak).

How it worked. The Great Hypostyle Hall Project describes the Egyptian method: builders laid a course of stone, then buried the whole area with earth before laying the next, and hauled each layer up mud-brick ramps until the top was reached. Afterwards the earth and ramps were removed, and masons smoothed the walls on the way down. The method needed no cranes: the building rose inside a mound of earth. Dieter Arnold's Building in Egypt (1997) is the standard reference for the ramp types, but it has not been read for this note.

What the ramp shows. Where the ramp survives, the carving and smoothing, which normally came last, were never done. This is the best place at Karnak to see the method in progress.

The court. To make room for ramps like this, Nectanebo I dismantled part of the north and south walls and columns on the far west side of Shoshenq I's court. Digital Karnak says the work was not completed and column drums were left completely undressed, and that the unfinished state is easy to spot. Rams from the avenue stand along the colonnades inside the court.

3D model. Digital Karnak's model of the pylon shows the unbuilt upper parts as transparent blocks; Sullivan's Architectural Features guide has an image of the pylon with remnants of the mud-brick ramp.

Access. The ramp is a fragile mound and is usually fenced. It is clearly visible from the path within a few metres.
Sources: Digital Karnak, "1st Pylon" and "Shoshenq I Court" (?p=4245); Sullivan, Architectural Features (Digital Karnak, 2008), caption "Pylon I with remnants of mud brick ramp in the Shoshenq I Court", citing Arnold 2003; Great Hypostyle Hall Project, "Constructing the Hall"; Wikimedia Commons file description for the brick photograph (pointer only).

## The Avenue of Sphinxes to Luxor and its 2021 reopening
Kind: reference
About: sphinx-avenue-reopening, luxor-sphinx-avenue
A processional road about 2.7 kilometres long (1.7 miles) joins Karnak to Luxor Temple. Sullivan gives about two kilometres. It was the route of the Opet festival, when statues of Amun and his family travelled to Luxor in a procession with dancers, and boats on the river celebrated the flood.

The road was paved and lined with sphinxes. Mansour Boraik says Amenhotep III started it and Nectanebo I completed it; Sullivan says Nectanebo added the sandstone paving and a series of human-headed sphinxes along the whole route, about 700 in all by their spacing, enclosed by mud-brick walls and planted with trees. Most of the sphinxes seen today are Nectanebo's. Part of the avenue was found in front of Luxor Temple in 1949 (Zakaria Ghonaim), and Mohamed Abdel Kadir cleared the first stretch between 1958 and 1960. Work halted in 2005 and resumed in 2017. About 50 years of excavation, removing hundreds of homes and mosques, preceded the reopening.

On 25 November 2021 Egypt held a televised ceremony, attended by President Abdel Fatah el-Sisi, to reopen the restored avenue. The next day it opened to visitors.

The ram avenue at the First Pylon is a different, short road.
Sources: PBS NewsHour, "Egypt unveils renovated Avenue of the Sphinxes in Luxor" (26 November 2021); The Art Newspaper, 25 November 2021; Digital Karnak, "Other Processional Ways"; Sullivan, UEE (2010), p. 21.

## Nectanebo I
Kind: person
About: nectanebo-i, nectanebo-enclosure
Nectanebo I (Nakhtnebef) was the first king of the Thirtieth Dynasty and reigned from 380 to 362 BC in Shaw's chronology. He was one of the last native Egyptian kings before the Persians returned in 343 BC, after the reign of Nectanebo II (360 to 343 BC). He launched a huge building programme across greater Karnak: he enclosed the temples of Amun-Ra, Mut and Montu in mud-brick walls, began the First Pylon, laid a sandstone pavement and rows of human-headed sphinxes along the avenue to Luxor, and added a gateway to the temple of Opet.
Sources: Sullivan, UEE (2010), p. 21; Digital Karnak, "1st Pylon" and "Enclosures and Gates"; Shaw (ed.), Oxford History of Ancient Egypt (2000), chronology; general knowledge for the return of the Persians in 343 BC.

## Ramesses II
Kind: person
About: ramesses-ii, rams-avenue
Ramesses II reigned from 1279 to 1213 BC (Shaw). At Karnak the small statuettes between the ram sphinxes' paws carry his titles. Whether he commissioned the sphinxes or took over older ones, such as those of Thutmose IV and Amenhotep III, is debated. Other Karnak works include completing and altering Sety I's Hypostyle Hall and the small Temple of Amun-Ra Who Hears Prayers on the east side, a chapel open to the public for oracles. Some accounts credit him with the quay terrace, but the excavators date the quay wall later. He reigned for about 66 years, which is why his name appears across so many Egyptian monuments, and why later kings so often took over his statues, as Pinedjem may have taken over his ram sphinxes.
Sources: Sullivan, UEE (2010), p. 17; Digital Karnak, "Western Processional Way".

## Pinedjem I
Kind: person
About: pinedjem-i, rams-avenue
Pinedjem I was High Priest of Amun and de facto ruler of Thebes in the Twenty-first Dynasty, from about 1070 BC. On Cabrol's theory he moved Amenhotep III's ram sphinxes (which Ramesses II had usurped) to Karnak and added his own name to their pedestals. He also added decoration to the entrance of the Temple of Khonsu. After the Twentieth Dynasty collapsed, Egypt was split: kings ruled the north from Tanis while the High Priests of Amun governed the south from Thebes, so Pinedjem was effectively the king of Upper Egypt in all but name. The dates of his later royal titles vary between scholars and are not given in the sources used here, so the guide says only "from about 1070 BC".
Sources: Sullivan, UEE (2010), p. 17; Digital Karnak, "Western Processional Way"; Shaw (2000) for the starting date.

## Sety II
Kind: person
About: sety-ii, sety-ii-obelisk
Sety II was a Nineteenth Dynasty king who reigned about 1200 to 1194 BC (Shaw), a son of Merenptah and grandson of Ramesses II. At Karnak he put a three-roomed quartzite and sandstone bark shrine in front of the old western gate (the Second Pylon of his day), with sanctuaries for Amun, Mut and Khonsu, where their boats rested on festival processions. He also had a small quartzite obelisk set up on the quay, probably one of a pair. His reign was short, about six years. His shrine is described with the great forecourt. The on-site board calls the shrine the "Chapels of Sety I", but the scholarly sources give Sety II.
Sources: Sullivan, UEE (2010), p. 16; Wikidata entry for the Karnak obelisk of Seti II (pointer); on-site board (source 9).
