## The Mithraic Altar and the Hutton Monument
Kind: site
About: mithraic-altar, hutton-monument, athenian-letters
The Mithraic Altar stands in woodland west of the Archer Pavilion. It was put up for Jemima, Marchioness Grey, and her husband Philip Yorke, and Historic England says it was complete by September 1748. It is a block of flint and stone: a rough base of Ketton stone on uncut flint, a die faced with knapped flint, a frieze encrusted with pebbles, carved lion's paws at the corners, and acanthus-leaf scrolls under a cap carved with drapery. Two stone tablets, on the west and east faces, carry inscriptions, one in cuneiform-style characters and one in Greek, with traces of black paint. It is Grade II*, and still stands where it was put. Bedfordshire Archives says it is about ten feet high; the Historic England listing gives the pedestal's plan as roughly 2.3 by 2.55 metres.

The name is misleading. Mithras was a god of Persian origin, worshipped in a Roman mystery cult popular with soldiers, but this is not an ancient altar. Bedfordshire Archives calls it "a fanciful creation of 1748"; Historic England describes a "Persian" style design. English Heritage's account of the women of Wrest calls it "an intellectual joke": Jemima, her husband and friends built it with a cryptic inscription inspired by the Athenian Letters.

The Athenian Letters were written by a circle around Philip Yorke and his brother Charles, begun in 1739 and printed privately in two volumes in 1741 and 1743. They are framed as letters from an agent of the King of Persia living in Athens during the Peloponnesian War. Historic England records an 1831 account that the altar commemorated the book, and that the Greek inscription was written by Daniel Wray, one of the contributors. That a Persian agent is the narrator fits the altar's Persian look, though no source states that link outright.

The design is credited to Thomas Wright, who was Jemima's tutor: see the section on Thomas Wright of Durham. (The Historic England listing gives his dates as "1711–1776"; every other source gives his death as 1786.)

The Hutton Monument stands about 40 metres west of the altar, about 175 yards south of the West Half House. Bedfordshire Archives dates it to about 1730 and says it was put up for Henry, Duke of Kent: a brick wall with an inset ashlar plaque commemorating Thomas Hutton, Esquire, "of Somerset House", who enjoyed the place and was agreeable company to his friend the Duke. The archive's author finds the inscription "rather condescending". The wording could not be checked against the stone, so quote it only as a paraphrase.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1158808 (Mithraic Altar); Bedfordshire Archives, Monuments and Ornaments at Wrest Park; English Heritage, Women's Designs at Wrest Park (Google Arts & Culture); Wikipedia, Athenian Letters.

## Thomas Wright of Durham
Kind: person
About: thomas-wright, original-theory, kant-theory, mithraic-altar
Thomas Wright (born 22 September 1711 at Byers Green, County Durham; died 25 February 1786) was a clockmaker, navigation teacher, astronomer and garden designer. He was apprenticed to a clockmaker at Bishop Auckland in 1725, studied mathematics and navigation, and in 1730 opened a school in Sunderland, selling instruments he made himself. He published a large engraved diagram of the heavens as a teaching aid (Clavis coelestis, 1742). From about 1746 he taught mathematics and astronomy to aristocratic women and surveyed estates, planning at least 15 gardens and grottoes. In 1755 he bought the house where he was born, rebuilt it and lived there until his death.

His link to Wrest is genuine. English Heritage says Jemima was tutored in garden design, surveying, geometry and astronomy by Wright, and the Wrest Circle research project at Oxford says he tutored all the women in her coterie. Historic England credits him with the design of the Mithraic Altar. A Durham University catalogue says he taught Lady Anne Sophia Grey (later Egerton) in 1739, 1741, 1742 and 1745, and adds that Capability Brown later destroyed some of Wright's landscapes at Wrest. That last remark is not confirmed by any Wrest source found, so treat it with caution.

His famous book is An Original Theory or New Hypothesis of the Universe (1750), self-published, with fine engravings, in a very small printing (one account says 118 copies). Wright explained the Milky Way as an effect of our position: we are inside a thin layer of stars, so looking along it we see a dense band of light and looking out of it we see few. His own picture was more theological than the modern one. Stars moved round a divine centre, in a flat ring (his Plate XXV) or in nested spherical shells (Plate XXVII); he treated both as possible and later preferred the shells. Parks & Gardens UK describes it as a grindstone-shaped disc and a forerunner of William Herschel's work (1785); Linda Hall Library points out that Wright himself did not conclude the Milky Way is a disc.

The disc idea was taken up by Immanuel Kant. Kant learned of Wright's book from a review in a German periodical, not from the book itself, and in 1755 published his Universal Natural History and Theory of the Heavens. Wright also suggested that faint nebulae might be other galaxies, which Kant called "island universes". Most astronomers rejected the idea until Edwin Hubble used Cepheid variable stars to show in 1924 that spiral nebulae are distant galaxies.

## The Chinese Temple
Kind: site
About: chinese-temple
The Chinese Temple, listed Grade II as the Chinese Summerhouse, stands on the south side of the north Broad Water. It is a timber building with a lead-covered roof on a stone plinth, open on its sides and front. A two-stage pagoda roof with upturned corners is topped by a crouching wyvern, the Grey family emblem (a wyvern is a two-legged dragon), and ornamental bells hang from the corners. Fretwork decorates the painted panelling and eaves. Inside are a stone floor and a simple timber bench along the back.

Date and designer: Historic England and Bedfordshire Archives date it to about 1760, for Jemima, Marchioness Grey, and say it was possibly designed by Sir William Chambers, who also designed the Pagoda at Kew. English Heritage's Google Arts story puts Jemima's Chinese-inspired "set piece" in the late 1750s: a wooden Chinese bridge, a temple, a conch-shell water feature, a willow and a tulip tree. Wikipedia's list of Chambers's work gives "about 1766" and "attributed". No document linking Chambers to Wrest has been found, so describe the attribution as possible. Bedfordshire Archives records that Edward Stevens, who also built the Bath House, repaired the temple in 1769.

It was rebuilt in 1876 for Lady Anne Cowper (Bedfordshire Archives says "rebuilt"; Historic England and Wikipedia say "remodelled") and heavily restored in the twentieth century.

It stands in the setting made when Jemima had Capability Brown rework the canals into the Broad Water between 1758 and 1760. Historic England places the Chinese Bridge about 60 metres from the temple.

What to look for: the wyvern on the very top of the roof; the little bells hanging at the corners of both tiers; the lattice-style fretwork in the panels and under the eaves; the bench inside; and the upturned, curling ends of the roof, which are the feature that most says "Chinese" to a European eye. Because it is timber, it needs repainting and repair far more often than stone, which is why it has been restored more than once. Jemima wrote of her garden in 1749 that "nothing can surpass it" (English Heritage's Google Arts story), a few years before this corner was made.

Chinese buildings like this were fashionable in English gardens in the middle of the eighteenth century. The taste is called chinoiserie, a European fantasy based on imported goods and travellers' reports; see the section on William Chambers.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1321760; Bedfordshire Archives, Buildings at Wrest Park; English Heritage, Women's Designs at Wrest Park; Wikipedia, William Chambers (architect); Wikipedia, Wrest Park; Historic England list entry 1158909; general knowledge for the upkeep of timber buildings.

## The Chinese Bridge
Kind: site
About: chinese-bridge
The Chinese Bridge crosses the north end of the Broad Water, east of the temple. The present bridge dates from 1876. Historic England says it was designed by Anne Florence, Countess Cowper, and built by estate workmen. It is of yellow brick with limestone dressings and a single elliptical arch, with rusticated voussoirs along its lower edge. The parapet has a dentil course of brick at its base, recessed panels, and a corbelled brick pier at the centre; the central and end piers carry ball finials. Stones on either side bear the date 1876 and her monogram under a coronet. Bedfordshire Archives reads the initials as those of Anne Florence, Countess Cowper, also 7th Baroness Lucas of Crudwell.

It replaced a timber bridge of the 1760s, which had itself succeeded an earlier bridge of the 1750s, from the years when Lancelot Brown reshaped the canals. Bedfordshire Archives says its style copies the bridge on Willow Pattern china. The Willow Pattern is a design made in England in the late 1700s, not an old Chinese one (general knowledge).

Engineering: an elliptical arch is a flattened curve, wider than it is high. Compared with a semicircular arch it gives a lower roadway for the same span, so less climbing, but it pushes outwards more strongly at its feet. Those sideways thrusts must be resisted by the abutments, which here are part of the banks. The wedge-shaped stones (voussoirs) pass the load round the curve in compression, which brick and stone handle well. The rusticated voussoirs are the roughened, boldly outlined stones at the arch's edge. This is general engineering knowledge, not stated by any Wrest source. The bridge has steps.

The Countess's family, the Cowpers and Lucases, had inherited Wrest in 1859; see the section on Anne Florence, Countess Cowper. The meadow just north of the bridge is one of English Heritage's recommended spots for autumn colour.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1158909; Bedfordshire Archives, Buildings at Wrest Park; English Heritage, Seasonal garden highlights; general knowledge for the arch, the Willow Pattern and voussoirs.

## William Chambers and chinoiserie
Kind: person
About: william-chambers, kew-pagoda, chinese-designs-book
Sir William Chambers (1723–1796) was an architect. Between 1740 and 1749 he worked for the Swedish East India Company and made three voyages to China, where he studied Chinese buildings. In 1757 he published Designs of Chinese Buildings, Furniture, Dresses, Machines, and Utensils in London. In 1761–1762 he built the ten-storey Great Pagoda at Kew for Princess Augusta, which helped to spread the fashion for Chinese-style garden buildings in England.

Historic England says the Chinese Temple at Wrest was "possibly" to his design. No other source confirms it, so say "possibly". Some accounts of the Kew Pagoda give its inspiration as the Porcelain Tower at Nanjing and others a timber tower at Whampoa; sources differ.

Chinoiserie is the European fashion for Chinese-style objects and buildings, about 1700 to 1780. It drew on imported porcelain, lacquer and silk and on travellers' accounts. It gave light, playful, painted and fretworked buildings: pagodas, bridges, tea houses with bells and upturned eaves. Wrest's temple has all these: upturned roof corners, bells and fretwork. Jemima's Chinese set piece was in the late 1750s, so quite early in the fashion.
Sources: Wikipedia, William Chambers (architect); Historic England list entry 1321760; English Heritage, Women's Designs at Wrest Park; History Today, The Great Pagoda at Kew (search summary only, not opened); general knowledge for the dates and character of chinoiserie.

## Diana and the greyhound
Kind: site
About: diana-statue
By the Ladies' Lake, a short way west of the Chinese Temple, stands a lead statue of Diana, the Roman goddess of hunting. She is naked except for drapery about her waist, with a greyhound at her side. The statue is 2.34 metres tall and stands on a Portland stone plinth. The geograph caption and OpenStreetMap place it by the Ladies' Lake.

Art Fund records it as made about 1729–1730 and attributed to Andrew Carpenter, and says it was commissioned specifically for the Wrest gardens. Art UK says it is thought to be by Carpenter (about 1677–1737), a leading maker of lead garden statues with a yard near Hyde Park, and that Henry, Duke of Kent, bought it in the late 1720s. English Heritage calls it an eighteenth-century statue.

Why it was ever away: the Art Fund's record says most of the estate's lead statuary was melted down for financial reasons by the Greys in 1809, but this one survived. Its recorded provenance runs: John George Murray, 1917 (the buyer of Wrest, when the estate was sold); moved to Coles Park about 1939; moved to Idlicote House about 1970. It was bought back with a £30,000 Art Fund grant towards a cost of £143,200, recorded in 2012; the vendor was the auction house Summers Place.

Lead was a popular garden material in the eighteenth century: it could be cast in a mould, was far cheaper than carved marble, and weathers to a soft grey unless painted or gilded. That is also why it was vulnerable: lead was valuable to melt down, which is how most of Wrest's lead figures were lost. What to look for: the goddess leaning forward with one foot raised on a small block, the dog sitting up beside her with its head lifted to her hand, and the collar round the greyhound's neck.

Do not confuse it with the painted lead group of Diana and Endymion in the French Parterre, which Bedfordshire Archives dates to about 1730. OpenStreetMap labels this statue simply "Diana".
Sources: Art Fund, Figure of Diana (artwork 11864); Art UK, Diana (as reported in search results; page not opened); Geograph, Michael Garlick, Silsoe, Wrest Park, by the Ladies' Lake, 'Diana and Greyhound' by Andrew Carpenter; English Heritage, The top dogs at English Heritage sites; Bedfordshire Archives, Statues at Wrest Park; English Heritage on Google Arts & Culture, moving statues and monuments at Wrest Park; general knowledge for lead statuary and what to look for (from the photograph).

## The five ancient altars and the dogs' cemetery
Kind: site
About: ancient-altars, ancient-altars-carved
Five white marble altars stand on a circular lawn among trees south of the Ladies' Lake: four round the edge and one in the middle. Historic England dates them to the late Hellenistic period, the late second to first century BC, making them by far the oldest objects at Wrest. Each is cylindrical, about a metre tall (Bedfordshire Archives says about four feet), with a thin projecting cornice, a plain marble cap and a square stone base. Each is carved with four ox heads (bucrania) joined by festoons of flowers. The pattern recalls the ox sacrifices made at altars.

Provenance: in 1817 Amabel, Countess de Grey, bought them from T. Beauclere Esq. of Muswell Hill and placed them where they are. Where Mr Beauclere obtained them is not known. They have stood in this setting ever since. Historic England lists the group Grade II*; the lawn is also on OpenStreetMap as the "Graeco-Roman Altars".

The central altar carries a Greek inscription: "Hail Diogenes, son of Enon, honest citizen of Askalon", Askalon being the ancient city of Ashkelon, on the coast near Gaza. Bedfordshire Archives notes that this inscription is much less worn than the stone around it and thinks it probably an early nineteenth-century addition.

Amabel (1751–1833) inherited Wrest from her mother, Jemima, in 1797. English Heritage says she made few changes to the garden, bought the altars in 1817 and had many of the lead statues melted for roof repairs, which she later regretted. The Art Fund puts the melting at 1809 and gives "financial reasons". The two sources agree on the act, differ slightly on the reason.

The dogs' cemetery lies in woodland, about 90 metres south-east of the altar lawn on OpenStreetMap's positions. English Heritage says the Lucas family, who owned Wrest into the twentieth century, buried their favourite dogs there, with headstones arranged round a stone dog on a plinth. No source found gives the dates or the dogs' names; treat any detail beyond this as unconfirmed.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1311237 (Group of Five Altars); Bedfordshire Archives, Monuments and Ornaments at Wrest Park; English Heritage, Women's Designs at Wrest Park; Art Fund, Figure of Diana; English Heritage, The top dogs at English Heritage sites; OpenStreetMap (positions).

## The statue of Atlas
Kind: site
About: atlas
The Atlas statue stands in rough grass about 250 metres south-east of the site of the old house. Historic England lists it Grade II as a Portland stone figure of Atlas, stooping with the globe on his shoulders and holding it with both hands. Drapery hangs over his left shoulder and falls to a tree stump behind his leg. He is muscular, with a strong face, a large nose and thick beard and hair. A small hole on the body is thought to be left from a nineteenth-century fig leaf. The figure stands on a naturalistic base with a later sandstone base, and the pedestal is thought to be nineteenth century. The hands and feet have lost much of their detail.

Date and place: the statue is understood to date from the early eighteenth century, when Henry Grey, later Duke of Kent, was developing the gardens, and is probably one of his additions. Its original position is unknown; the earliest record of it here, east of the old house, is from 1900. Earthworks show it once stood in a pond, now silted up; Bedfordshire Archives noted in 2011 that the pond was dry, and that although it is called the Atlas Fountain, "it is not a fountain, just a statue".

Space link: in Greek myth the Titan Atlas was condemned to hold up the sky (the heavens), not the Earth. Classical art shows him holding the celestial spheres. The "Atlas carrying the world" picture is a later muddle, helped by Mercator's sixteenth-century dedication of his map collection to the Titan, which made "atlas" mean a book of maps.

A celestial globe shows the sky as if seen from outside, so constellations look the opposite way round to the view from Earth. The best-known ancient example is the Farnese Atlas in the National Archaeological Museum in Naples: a Roman marble of about 150 AD, probably copied from an earlier Greek work. Atlas carries a globe 65 centimetres across, carved with 41 or 42 of the 48 classical constellations. It is the oldest surviving celestial globe. In 2005 Bradley Schaefer argued that its constellations fit the lost star catalogue of Hipparchus, about 129 BC; this is contested. Nothing suggests the Wrest Atlas was modelled on the Farnese one.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1321795; Bedfordshire Archives, Statues at Wrest Park; Wikipedia, Atlas (mythology); Wikipedia, Farnese Atlas; general knowledge for the mirror-reversal of celestial globes.
