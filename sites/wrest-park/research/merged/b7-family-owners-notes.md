## The de Grey family and Wrest
Kind: site
About: grey-family, grey-first-record, kent-earldom, wrest-sale, edmund-grey, amabel-de-grey, lord-lucas
The Greys and their heirs held Wrest for more than 600 years, from the 1280s until the estate was sold in 1917. The Victoria County History of 1908 says the manor "has been in the possession of the De Greys, earls of Kent, and their descendants for over six hundred years". The first record is of Reginald de Grey, who held a fee in Flitton and Silsoe in 1284–1286. Edward IV made Edmund Grey Earl of Kent in 1465.

The family had a hard time in the early 1500s. Richard, 3rd Earl, gambled and borrowed, and Wrest was sold to Sir Henry Wyatt in 1507. His half-brother Henry bought it back in 1512 (Bedfordshire Archives also gives a deed of 1514) but was too poor to use the earldom. His grandson Reginald was recognised as Earl again in 1572.

In the early 1680s Anthony, 11th Earl, his wife Mary, Baroness Lucas, and his mother Amabel made the Long Water. Their son Henry became Duke of Kent in 1710 and made the great formal garden. When he died in 1740 his Kent titles died with him, and Wrest passed to his granddaughter Jemima, Marchioness Grey. Her elder daughter Amabel, made Countess de Grey in 1816, left it in 1833 to her nephew Thomas, 2nd Earl de Grey, who pulled down the old house and built the present one in 1834–1839.

After Thomas, Wrest went to his daughter Anne Florence, Countess Cowper (1859), then to her son Francis, 7th Earl Cowper (1880), then to Francis's nephew Auberon Herbert, 9th Baron Lucas (1905), who let it to the American ambassador Whitelaw Reid. Lucas, a Royal Flying Corps pilot, was killed in November 1916. His sister Nan, who had run the wartime hospital in the house, sold the estate in 1917 to John George Murray of Thirsk, a brewery and coal company chairman. Bedfordshire Archives says it was a private sale after an auction had been advertised.

Fuller sections follow the story in order: the medieval Greys, losing and regaining Wrest, the Earls of Kent, the heiresses, the Cowpers and Lucases, the hospital and fire, the 1917 sale, and after the family.

## The medieval Greys (1280s–1490)
Kind: site
About: grey-first-record, kent-earldom, edmund-grey, reginald-de-grey, old-house, grey-family
Wrest does not appear under its own name in Domesday Book. The Victoria County History takes the manor to be the larger of two in Silsoe in 1086. It was held by a man called Hugh from Walter, brother of Saher, and was assessed at four hides. A thegn called Leofnoth had held it in Edward the Confessor's time. Bedfordshire Archives adds a mill worth 26 shillings, woodland for 100 pigs, six villagers, eight smallholders and four slaves. That identification is the historians' and not Domesday's own. Between 1086 and 1284 no holder of Wrest is recorded. The VCH says the manor was held of the barony of Wahull, and J. H. Round suggested that the Greys got their Bedfordshire lands by marrying an heiress of the Wahull tenants. John de Grey was a sheriff of the county under Henry III.

The first record of the Greys here is the Feudal Aids of 1284–1286: Reginald de Grey held one fee in Flitton and Silsoe of John de Wahull. When he died in 1307–1308 the papers listed a "capital messuage with a dovecote", 100 acres of arable, meadow, pasture and a wood. The manor was worth £4. The capital messuage is probably the start of the old house. Reginald was summoned to Parliament in 1295 as Lord Grey de Wilton.

His son John, 2nd Baron, was a Lord Ordainer in 1310. In 1311 Wrest was settled on Roger, John's son by his second wife, and the rest of the Bedfordshire property came to Roger when John died in 1323. Roger was summoned as Lord Grey de Ruthyn in 1324 and in about 1344 claimed free warren and a park at Wrest, the first mention of the park. After Roger came Reginald (died 1388, when the manor was worth £13 a year) and a second Reginald, captured in 1402, who died in 1440–1441.

His grandson Edmund Grey, born in 1416, was at first a Lancastrian. At Northampton on 10 July 1460 he went over to the Yorkists. Edward IV made him Lord High Treasurer in 1463 and Earl of Kent on 30 May 1465. Sources disagree about his reward. The VCH says he received Ampthill. Bedfordshire Archives says he bought it in 1446, and the 2nd Earl de Grey doubted the story. The Inventories chapter says he bought Ampthill Castle in 1454, after which Wrest was not the main residence until 1508. He died on 22 May 1490 (the VCH says 1489).

His son George, 2nd Earl, fought at Stoke Field in 1487. The VCH says he was sent to France in 1491 to help "the Emperor Maximilian", the Habsburg ruler. The old house was moated by 1512.
Sources: Victoria County History, Bedford vol. 2, Flitton cum Silsoe; Bedfordshire Archives, Silsoe in 1086, The Manor of Wrest, Wrest Park Before the 17th Century, The Earls of Kent; Cambridge Core abstract of Inventories of Bedfordshire Country Houses, Wrest Park.

## Losing and regaining Wrest (1507–1572)
Kind: site
About: grey-family, kent-earldom
Richard Grey, 3rd Earl of Kent, succeeded in 1503 (the Victoria County History and the Complete Peerage say his father George died on 21 December 1503; Bedfordshire Archives and Wikipedia say 1505). He was a courtier, at the Field of the Cloth of Gold in 1520, and by every account a spendthrift. Bedfordshire Archives quotes the 2nd Earl de Grey's family history: he "wasted the Family property" and mortgaged or sold most of it, including Ruthin, Ampthill and Ashill in Norfolk. Gambling is the usual explanation. Bedfordshire Archives adds that his father George predicted on his deathbed that Richard "will not thrive and will be a waster".

The Inventories of Bedfordshire Country Houses says that in 1507 he was forced to sell Wrest to Sir Henry Wyatt. Wyatt was a Tudor royal councillor and (general knowledge, verify) the father of the poet Sir Thomas Wyatt. Richard died in London in 1524 with no children.

His half-brother Henry (born in 1490 by Bedfordshire Archives) could not afford to take the earldom, so he lived as Sir Henry Grey. The chapter on Wrest says he bought Wrest back in 1512, to stop it being pulled down for its materials. Bedfordshire Archives quotes a document of about 1524, in which Henry says he heard that Wyatt meant to pull down the manor place and sell the timber and tile. Having no house of his own and pitying the loss of the house of his ancestors, he bought "the manor off Wraste" and nearby lands for 450 marks (£300). Bedfordshire Archives gives two dates: 1512 in one page, and in another 28 April 1514, a deed that lists Wrest, Flitton, Pulloxhill, Upper and Lower Gravenhurst, Brobury and Cainhoe, and describes the manors as mortgaged and not sold. So Wrest and its neighbouring manors were recovered. The rest of Richard's lost property mostly was not, and Henry spent years in lawsuits. The VCH skips the Wyatt episode and says only that the manor passed to Henry on Richard's death in 1524.

Henry died in 1562 (Bedfordshire Archives gives 24 September; the Complete Peerage 21 September) and Wrest passed to his grandson Reginald. In 1571 Reginald petitioned Elizabeth I, and in 1572 she recognised him as Earl of Kent. William Camden says the title had "lain asleep" for fifty years. He failed to recover Richard's other lands. He died in 1573 with no children. The 1573 inventory of the house lists rooms from the Dining Chamber to the Clock Chamber.
Sources: Bedfordshire Archives, The Earls of Kent and Wrest Park Before the 17th Century; Cambridge Core abstract of Inventories of Bedfordshire Country Houses 1714–1830, Wrest Park; Wikisource, Complete Peerage vol. 4 p. 352; Wikipedia, Richard Grey, 3rd Earl of Kent and Reginald Grey, 5th Earl of Kent (quoting Camden); VCH Bedford vol. 2.

## The Earls of Kent, 1572–1702
Kind: site
About: grey-family, kent-earldom, anthony-11th-earl
After Reginald (died 1573) the earldom went to his brothers. Henry, 6th Earl (born in 1541, died 1615), was one of the commissioners who sentenced Mary, Queen of Scots, in 1587, and was present at her execution. He built the de Grey Mausoleum at Flitton church, where the family were buried until 1859. Charles, 7th Earl, followed in 1615 and died in 1623.

Henry, 8th Earl (born about 1583), was a friend of the lawyer John Selden. He married Elizabeth Talbot in 1601 and died on 21 November 1639 with no children. The VCH gives 1631, which is wrong. The title passed to a distant cousin, Anthony, 9th Earl, a country clergyman, rector of Aston Flamville, who was 82 when he succeeded and died in 1643.

His son Henry, 10th Earl (born 1594), backed Parliament in the Civil War. He was a Speaker of the House of Lords until it was abolished in February 1649. He died on 28 May 1651. His widow Amabel Benn, the "Good Countess", born in 1607 and died on 29 August 1698, ran the estate for her young son and helped pay for the old house's classical north front. A sculpture on the Flitton tomb shows her effigy higher than her husband's.

Anthony, 11th Earl (born 11 June 1645), became Earl at six. He married Mary Lucas on 2 March 1663; on 6 or 7 May 1663 she was made Baroness Lucas of Crudwell. Her father had bought the title with an unusual rule, so that it could descend through women. When her father died on 2 July 1671 she inherited a large estate in Wiltshire and Essex. Their son Henry was baptised on 28 September 1671. Their money paid for the north front, the Long Water and the first formal gardens. Anthony died in August 1702 (Bedfordshire Archives gives both 9 and 19 August) and Mary a few months later.

Numbering differs. This guide follows Bedfordshire Archives and Wikipedia, where Anthony is the 11th Earl and his son Henry the 12th. The VCH counts one fewer.
Sources: Bedfordshire Archives, The Earls of Kent, Wrest Park in the 17th Century and The Barons and Baronesses Lucas of Crudwell; Wikipedia, Henry Grey, 8th Earl of Kent; VCH Bedford vol. 2.

## Heiresses: Jemima, Amabel and the de Greys
Kind: site
About: jemima, amabel-de-grey, earl-de-grey, grey-family
Henry, Duke of Kent, died on 5 June 1740 with no surviving sons: nine of his eleven children died before him. Wrest went to his granddaughter Jemima Campbell. Her mother Amabel was the Duke's eldest daughter; her father John Campbell, later Earl of Breadalbane, was British envoy at Copenhagen, where Bedfordshire Archives says she was born on 9 October 1722. Wikipedia, The Peerage and the Early Modern Letters database say 1723. Sources disagree, and I could not settle it.

In 1740 the Duke was made Marquess de Grey with a special remainder to Jemima, so a woman could hold the title. She married Philip Yorke on 22 May 1740, two weeks before her grandfather died. One source suggests she inherited on condition of marrying him. I found nothing in the sources I read to support that, so treat it as unconfirmed. Yorke became 2nd Earl of Hardwicke in 1764 and died in 1790. They lived at Wimpole in Cambridgeshire and at Wrest. Jemima died on 10 or 11 January 1797 (sources differ).

The marquessate then died out, but the Lucas barony, which can pass through women, went to her elder daughter Amabel (born January 1751). She was widowed in 1781 and had no children. Created Countess de Grey on 25 October 1816, with a special remainder to her sister's male heirs, she left Wrest to her nephew in 1833. She died on 4 March 1833 (Wikipedia; Bedfordshire Archives says 4 May).

Thomas Philip, 2nd Earl de Grey, was the son of her younger sister Mary Jemima and Lord Grantham. He took the name Weddell in 1803 and de Grey in 1833. The foundation stone of his new house was laid on 12 February 1834 and the family moved in autumn 1839. The earldom de Grey itself could pass only in the male line, so it went to another nephew, George Robinson, later Marquess of Ripon. He did not inherit Wrest. The barony of Lucas, which can pass to a daughter, and Wrest went to the Earl's daughter Anne Florence.
Sources: Bedfordshire Archives, Jemima Marchioness Grey, The Earls and Countesses de Grey of Wrest, The Duke of Kent; VCH Bedford vol. 2; Wikipedia, Jemima Yorke, 2nd Marchioness Grey and Amabel Hume-Campbell; Early Modern Letters Online (Swansea), Jemima Campbell Yorke.

## The Cowpers and Lucases
Kind: site
About: anne-florence-cowper, francis-cowper, lord-lucas, whitelaw-reid, grey-family
Anne Florence (born 8 June 1806) inherited Wrest and the Lucas barony in 1859, aged 53. She had married George Cowper in 1833 and became Countess Cowper when he succeeded as 6th Earl in 1837; he died in 1856. Wrest was no longer the family's main home from then: the Cowpers lived at Panshanger in Hertfordshire. She died on 23 July 1880.

Her son Francis Thomas de Grey Cowper, 7th Earl Cowper (born 11 June 1834), was Lord Lieutenant of Bedfordshire from 1861 and of Ireland from 1880 to 1882. He died at Panshanger on 22 July 1905 with no children. He held 37,869 acres in ten counties, 9,105 of them in Bedfordshire, and his will was valued at £1,079,000.

The barony and Wrest went to his nephew Auberon Herbert, "Bron" (born 25 May 1876), 9th Baron Lucas. Whitelaw Reid had been appointed American ambassador in June 1905, and Lucas let him Wrest that year at £1,500 a year. Reid stayed for about eight years. Bedfordshire Archives says he died at Wrest; Wikipedia, citing the New York Times, says he died at Dorchester House in London on 15 December 1912.

King Edward VII spent the weekend of 24–25 July 1909 at Wrest as Reid's guest. The Bedfordshire Times called it the first visit by a sovereign. Among the guests invited to meet him was the Austrian ambassador. Crowds lined the road, and the King went to church at Silsoe and looked round Flitton church and mausoleum.

Lucas lost a foot as a Boer War correspondent. He was Under-Secretary for War from 1908 to 1911 and President of the Board of Agriculture from August 1914 to May 1915. He then joined the Royal Flying Corps and was killed on 3 November 1916. He was unmarried. His sister Nan inherited.

Numbering: Bedfordshire Archives and Wikipedia call Bron the 9th Baron and Nan the 10th. English Heritage often calls them 8th and 9th. The difference is whether Anthony, Earl of Harold (died 1723), who held the barony by writ in his father's lifetime, is counted.
Sources: Bedfordshire Archives, The Barons and Baronesses Lucas of Crudwell, Wrest Park in the 20th Century, King Edward VII at Wrest Park; Wikipedia, Whitelaw Reid; Parks & Gardens UK, Wrest Park (Historic England register 1000113); English Heritage, Auberon Herbert (members' magazine).

## Hospital, fire and sale (1914–1917)
Kind: site
About: wrest-sale, lord-lucas, nan-herbert, wrest-hospital, wrest-fire
Bron Lucas offered Wrest as a hospital in August 1914, just after he joined the Cabinet. It was ready for its first patients on 7 September 1914 (English Heritage). It began as a convalescent home and became a base hospital in November 1914. Unlike most auxiliary hospitals it was not run by the Red Cross or the Order of St John but privately, and Lucas paid for it. His sister Nan Herbert set it up, trained at the Metropolitan Hospital in London, and became matron in February 1915. English Heritage calls it "one of the best-run country house hospitals".

About 1,600 men passed through it. It could hold up to 200 patients, and had a medical officer who could call on three London surgeons and about 20–25 nurses (over 100 in all served there). A-Ward, for the worst cases, was in the ground-floor reception rooms, B-Ward in the first-floor bedrooms and C-Ward in the service wing. The operating theatre was in a first-floor bedroom over the north side of the entrance hall, with an X-ray room. Soldiers came by train to Ampthill and were bathed in the "louse house" before the wards.

The fire was on Thursday 14 September 1916. English Heritage says it badly damaged the mansion and that all 156 patients were got out safely, with beds wheeled onto the terrace and into the garden. A local-history account says it started in the east wing (the service wing), ten fire brigades attended and Royal Engineers came from Haynes Park. No-one was hurt, and Ampthill recruits helped save furniture and paintings. About 50 patients were moved at around midnight to Woburn Abbey hospital and huts at Ampthill Camp. The damage from fire, smoke and water was estimated at £20,000. The 1917 sale catalogue says the house needed a new roof because of "the recent fire". I found no source for the cause. The damage was too serious to reopen the hospital. Nurses stayed to help clean up.

Six weeks later, on 3 November 1916, Bron was killed. Nan succeeded him and married Lt-Col Howard Lister Cooper on 30 April 1917. Why the family sold is not stated outright. The sources give three reasons together: Bron's death, the fire damage and the burden of such a large house.
Sources: English Heritage, Wrest Park at War, First World War nurse identified, and the nurses page; Tommy's Footprints, Wrest Park Military Hospital (a local-history site, quoting the Bedfordshire Standard of 22 September 1916; not independently checked); Bedfordshire Archives, Wrest Park in the 19th Century (1917 sale catalogue) and Wrest Park in the 20th Century.

## The 1917 sale and what happened to the contents and statues
Kind: site
About: wrest-sale, contents-auction, lord-lucas
Who bought Wrest? Bedfordshire Archives says a sale by auction was put in hand in 1917 (the Silsoe local history says it was to be at the Mart, Tokenhouse Yard, London, on 17 July 1917), but in the event the house was sold privately to John George Murray of Thirsk. The Bedfordshire Archives catalogue says Wrest was sold in July 1917. Another Bedfordshire Archives page says the estate was sold off in 1918 and 1919, with Murray taking the bulk. Whether those were separate parcels is unclear.

Murray was born in 1864 at Consett, County Durham. By Bedfordshire Archives he was chairman of Associated Breweries and North Eastern Breweries, and of several north-eastern coal companies including Seaton Burn, North Walbottle and Redheugh Colliery. So the usual description, a brewing and coal businessman, is right. He was High Sheriff of Bedfordshire in 1923.

The contents were sold separately. English Heritage's "Lost and Found" says they went in a five-day auction in September 1917. The catalogue of "the valuable remaining contents of the mansion" by Messrs Foster, on the premises, began on 10 September 1917 and ran four more days. "Remaining" matters: Bron had sold some furnishings before the war, and the manuscripts had been dispersed. Nan gave the British Museum a collection of over 3,000 prints in memory of Bron, and later gave fossils to the Natural History Museum. Most furniture and porcelain was sold, so most rooms are now empty. The Huysmans portrait of the three Crew sisters left at the sale and was bought by English Heritage in 2015. The hall's eleven portraits were excluded from the sale and offered separately, and in 1939 the Sun Insurance bought them.

The statues mostly did not go in 1917. The sources date their loss to Murray's time in the 1930s. In 1933 he moved to Coles Park in Hertfordshire, taking many statues and ornaments, including the 1682 sundial. The Four Seasons and Four Elements went to Ditchley Park, and are still missing. The Duke of Kent's obelisk and the Duchess's column went to Trent Park in about 1934 (from a search summary of the Historic England entry; not opened). The Ministry of Works bought back Italian Garden edging in 1950 and the sundial in 1954 when Coles Park was pulled down. The Diana statue stayed with Murray's descendants and came back in 2011–2012 with Art Fund help. Murray also removed the Rose Garden, and many old trees were felled in the 1920s to 1940s. Earlier still, lead statues were melted down in 1809 or around then (Art Fund) or by Countess Amabel for roof repairs (English Heritage).
Sources: Bedfordshire Archives, Wrest Park in the 20th Century, The Manor of Wrest, catalogue entry for the Wrest Park [Lucas] Archive, Wrest Park in the 19th Century; Wikishire, Silsoe; English Heritage on Google Arts & Culture, Wrest Park: Lost and Found; Wikipedia, Nan Ino Cooper, 10th Baroness Lucas (citing the 1917 catalogue and The Times, 20 December 1917) and Wrest Park; Art Fund, Figure of Diana.

## After the family (1917–today)
Kind: site
About: grey-family, sun-insurance, ministry-purchase, niae, eh-takeover, gardens-reopened, archaeology-store
John George Murray lived at Wrest until the 1930s. A 1927 valuer found the house modernised, but the grounds "too big and not kept up well". In 1932 he advertised it as a school for at least 250 pupils, an institution or a country club, and failed to sell it. He had left for Coles Park by 1933 (English Heritage) though Kelly's Directory had him at Wrest in 1936.

In June 1939 the Sun Insurance Office bought the mansion and about 260 acres of the roughly 500-acre estate for £25,000, expecting war. Murray kept the rest. Sun built twelve huts and converted the stables for its staff, and finished the alterations on 4 September 1940. Bedfordshire Archives says Sun moved back to London in late 1945 and early 1946.

Then the state bought it. The date is disputed. Geograph descriptions echoing the Historic England entry say 1946; Bedfordshire Archives says October 1949, and the "c.1946" in its timeline. The National Institute of Agricultural Engineering moved in between June 1947 and July 1948, and a 1960 Commons answer says the Ministry of Works began restoring the garden buildings in September 1949. The buyer was the Ministry of Works (the register calls it the Ministry of Public Building and Works, a later name). In 1960 the house and gardens were leased to the British Society for Research in Agricultural Engineering and occupied by the institute. From 1954 the historic gardens opened to the public on summer weekends. The institute (renamed Silsoe Research Institute in 1991) closed at the end of March 2006.

English Heritage took over and began a 20-year plan to restore the gardens to how they were before 1917. The Wolfson Foundation pledged up to £400,000 in autumn 2007 and the Heritage Lottery Fund gave over £1 million in 2010. The restored gardens reopened on 4 August 2011. In 2014 a £2 million store for English Heritage's archaeological collections opened in the grounds, holding over 150,000 objects from sites such as Kirby Hall, Audley End and Hill Hall. English Heritage looks after the house and gardens for the state.

## Auberon Herbert, 9th Baron Lucas
Kind: person
About: lord-lucas
Auberon "Bron" Herbert (born 25 May 1876) was the son of Auberon Edward William Molyneux Herbert and Florence Amabel Cowper, and nephew of the 7th Earl Cowper. He was educated at Bedford School and Balliol College, Oxford, rowed for Oxford, and lost a foot in the Boer War as a war correspondent. As a Liberal minister he was Under-Secretary of State for War (1908–1911) and was President of the Board of Agriculture from 6 August 1914. He inherited Wrest in 1905 and let it to Whitelaw Reid. He established a Shorthorn herd at the Home Farm.

In August 1914 he offered Wrest as a hospital. He resigned in May 1915 to join the Royal Flying Corps, trained as an observer and got his pilot's certificate in September 1915. He was in France from October 1916. On 3 November 1916 he was flying near Haplincourt, shot down by a German fighter, and died of his wounds the same day aged 40. His observer, Anderson, was captured. He is buried at the HAC Cemetery, Ecoust-St Mein. He died unmarried and his sister Nan inherited the barony and Wrest.
Sources: Bedfordshire Archives, The Barons and Baronesses Lucas of Crudwell and Wrest Park in the 20th Century; English Heritage members' magazine, Auberon Herbert.

## Nan Herbert, Baroness Lucas
Kind: person
About: nan-herbert, wrest-hospital
Nan Ino Herbert (born 13 June 1880, died 1958) was Bron's sister. Before the First World War she had been involved in theosophy and a school in Cuba. In 1914 she set up Wrest as a hospital and recruited the nurses, which was hard in wartime. Her diary called some early recruits an unsuitable mix. She became matron in February 1915 after a crash course at the Metropolitan Hospital, and later recalled beds being wheeled onto the terrace in the 1916 fire.

She succeeded as 10th Baroness Lucas (9th by English Heritage's count) in November 1916 and married Lt-Col Howard Lister Cooper on 30 April 1917. She sold Wrest that year. She gave over 3,000 prints to the British Museum in Bron's memory and fossils to the Natural History Museum, and in 1936 deposited the Wrest papers with the Bedfordshire record office. Her daughter Anne Rosemary Palmer (born 1919) inherited the barony.
