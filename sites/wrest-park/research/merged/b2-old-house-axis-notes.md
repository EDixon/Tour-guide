## The old house of Wrest
Kind: site
About: old-house, north-front, old-house-demolished, carew-poem
The house the Greys lived in for about 550 years stood in the southern part of the South Lawns, close to the fountain and more than 200 metres south of today's mansion. Nothing stands above ground, but its walls lie just under the turf. They show as brown parchmarks in dry summers: archaeologists noticed them in 1988 and photographed them from the air in 1994.

It began as a manor house. When Reginald de Grey died in 1307–1308 the papers listed a "capital messuage with a dovecote" at Silsoe, meaning a manor house with its outbuildings, and by 1512 it was moated. Each generation then added rooms, so that the 1573 inventory lists a chapel, a "clock chamber" and many named bedchambers. Bedfordshire Archives says it grew "piecemeal with no attempt at symmetry".

In spring 1639 the poet Thomas Carew stayed here with Henry, 8th Earl of Kent, and his wife Elizabeth Talbot, and wrote "To my friend G.N. from Wrest". He praises the plain buildings and the generous welcome, and describes streams that "twice surround this island Mansion". The 2013 English Heritage survey calls it the first reference to a designed garden at Wrest, though it says the meaning is far from clear.

The classical north front came next. Anthony, 11th Earl, married Mary Lucas in 1663. Her father died in July 1671, leaving her a large estate, and the money paid for much of the new front, built "to disguise its irregularities". Sources date it differently: 1671 (Historic England listing), 1672 (Bedfordshire Archives), 1672–1676 (History of Parliament) and "the 1670s" (the 2013 survey). An undated agreement describes a building 212 feet long with a cupola. Amabel, the Earl's mother, kept the accounts: £3,227, over a quarter above the estimate. The architect is unknown.

Henry, Duke of Kent, considered rebuilding the whole house with plans from Giacomo Leoni, but nothing was built, so the old house survived another hundred years. Jemima added a dining room in 1760, and in 1791 both fronts were refaced with sash windows; John Woolfe redesigned the north front about 1790.

Thomas, 2nd Earl de Grey, inherited in 1833 and demolished the house, building the present one 200 metres north in 1834–1839. Demolition ran alongside the building: watercolours show parts standing in 1838, and Bedfordshire Archives gives 1834 to 1840. The survivors are the clock tower, moved to the new service yard, and a chimneypiece of about 1570 to 1600 now in the Orangery. It carries the arms of Edmund Grey, 1st Earl, and the motto "Foy est tout". The Earl's 1831 sketchbook records the old house just before it fell.

## The fountain and its eight statues
Kind: site
About: wrest-fountain
The round pond with its marble fountain stands about 200 metres south of the present house, where the path down the Long Water crosses the path from the Orangery. It is Grade II listed. A wide, shallow bowl of white Carrara marble sits on a large square base. Beneath it four seated young women each hold something to do with water: a fishing net with a basket of fish (north), a dolphin (south), a goose (east) and an oar (west). The pond wall and the eight pedestals round it are Ketton stone.

The eight marble statues stand on pedestals of the early 1700s, but the figures are early to mid nineteenth century. Their names have changed. Bedfordshire Archives names them Venus, Cato, Iris, the Tragic Muse, the Comic Muse, Flora, Pandora and Ceres. The Historic England listing, revised in 2012, reads them as Psyche and Venus (both after Canova), Ceres (a copy of an antique), two figures of Spring, Clytemnestra, Aeschines, and a figure it calls Tragic Comedy.

The date is disputed. Bedfordshire Archives says "probably built about 1840 for the 2nd Earl de Grey". The listing says the fountain was one of the pieces acquired by Anne Florence, Countess Cowper, after she inherited in 1859, and quotes a 1904 Country Life article that the statues "formerly stood at more distant points". The 2013 English Heritage survey says only that the circular fountain was installed "at some time", possibly by Lady Cowper. It was not the first fountain here. From 1686 Amabel and Anthony's formal garden had circular pools with fountains in the panels south of the house, and by about 1720 these had been filled in and replaced with statues.

The listing says the fountain "was fed by cisterns at the top of the house". That is gravity feed: water in a tank high in the house pushes down the pipe, and every 10 metres of height gives about 1 bar of pressure, so a jet of a few metres needs only a modest head. The present house, built in 1834–1839, stands about 200 metres away. The service yard also has a horse-driven pump house beside a well and a fire-engine house, both of 1834–1839; the listing does not say they filled the cisterns. In 2006 the water pipe to the fountain was replaced, and English Heritage's restoration plan was to run the fountain from an electric pump.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1113791; Bedfordshire Archives, Monuments and Ornaments at Wrest Park and Statues at Wrest Park; Historic England list entry 1403022; English Heritage Research Report 6-2013, Wrest Park Landscape Investigations.

## The sundial and the six statues
Kind: site
About: sundial, sundial-statues, henry-wynne
The sundial stands about 270 metres south of the house, in a D-shaped lawn south of the Broadwalk. It is a large brass dial plate on an octagonal marble pedestal carved with acanthus leaves and flower swags tied with ribbons. The pedestal is listed Grade II as late seventeenth century, and Bedfordshire Archives measures it at about three feet high.

The dial plate is dated 1682 and signed Henricus Wynne. Henry Wynne was a London instrument maker (the listing gives his death as 1709) and one of the best makers of "double horizontal" dials of the Restoration. The listing calls him "the architect Henry Wynne", which looks like a mistake. He made a similar dial for Windsor Castle in 1680, carrying the monogram of Charles II. The Wrest dial is about 777 millimetres across and is marked in one-minute steps from 4 a.m. to 8 p.m. It also has sixteen rings giving the latitude and longitude of places, the names and positions of twelve stars, and tables for the Moon's age.

Today's plate is a replica: the listing calls it fibreglass, the Sundial Society calls it an electrotype in bronze resin. The original was taken to Coles Park by John Murray, who bought Wrest in 1917; the Ministry bought it back after 1954. It then spent years at English Heritage's London office and returned in 2024, and is now kept at the Dairy. The original blade bore the Grey arms and the motto "Foy est tout" ("faith is all"). A 2024 register entry says the blade is missing, and Bedfordshire Archives found it missing in 2011, though a 2017 photograph shows it.

Where the dial first stood is not settled. English Heritage's 2013 survey says the Kip and Knyff view of about 1704 shows a sundial at the centre of the Great Court in front of the old house, and that by the 1720s a sundial stood at the middle of the south terrace. A square brick base found in the lawn here may be an original base. Two dials, bought in 1682 and 1690–1694, are mentioned.

Six marble statues stand round it on early-1700s Ketton stone pedestals. Clockwise from the north-east: Hebe (a copy of Thorvaldsen), a dancing bacchante with a bunch of grapes, a bacchante with a tambourine, a girl feeding a bird on her shoulder, a bacchante with a ewer, and Minerva with a Medusa breastplate (a copy of the Vatican statue). Bedfordshire Archives says about 1840 for Earl de Grey. The listing says Anne Florence, Countess Cowper, added them after 1859, and quotes William Treacher (1899) that they were copied from the Vatican and bought from the 1862 Exhibition.
Sources: Historic England list entries 1113797 and 1321796; British Sundial Society register S2125, S5169 and S6338; Bedfordshire Archives, Monuments and Ornaments at Wrest Park and Statues at Wrest Park; English Heritage Research Report 6-2013; Wikipedia, London dial.

## Sundial time and clock time
Kind: reference
About: sundial
A sundial reads local solar time, which differs from the clock for three reasons.

First, the blade (gnomon) must point at the celestial pole. It is tilted up from the horizontal by the latitude, which at Wrest is about 52 degrees north, so its shadow swings round at an even 15 degrees an hour as the Earth turns. A dial made for one latitude reads wrongly at another, which is why Wynne's dial lists the latitudes and longitudes of sixteen places.

Second, longitude. Clocks in Britain keep Greenwich time, and the Sun crosses the meridian four minutes later for every degree you go west. Wrest is about 0.41 degrees west, so local noon comes about 1 minute 40 seconds after 12.00 Greenwich time.

Third, the equation of time. The Earth's orbit is an ellipse and its axis is tilted, so the Sun's true position drifts against an even clock. Over the year the sundial runs up to about 16 minutes ahead of clock time in early November and about 14 minutes behind in mid-February. The two agree on about four days: around 15 April, 13 June, 1 September and 25 December. Even then the Wrest dial still runs 1 minute 40 seconds behind Greenwich time because of its longitude, so a perfect match with a watch is never expected. Once John Flamsteed's equation-of-time tables became available, makers such as Wynne experimented with putting them on dials. The Sundial Society register lists calendrical tables on the Wrest dial but does not say whether they include one.

Add British Summer Time, which runs from late March to late October. Then a clock is an hour ahead of Greenwich, and the dial reads about an hour behind it, plus or minus the equation of time and the minute and 40 seconds. A reading is also taken from the edge of the shadow, and a thick blade throws two edges; London dials have a gap at noon for that reason.
Sources: general knowledge (astronomy of the sundial and the equation of time); Wikipedia, London dial; British Sundial Society register S2125 and S5169.

## The Long Water
Kind: site
About: long-water, mercury-statue
The Long Water is the straight canal running south from the lawns to the Archer Pavilion, and it has been the axis of the garden for more than 300 years. English Heritage dates it to the early 1680s and credits Amabel Benn, her son Anthony, 11th Earl, and his wife Mary. The registered garden's history dates the Earl and Countess's phase of work 1671–1702. The date is uncertain. The 2013 English Heritage survey says only that it was probably dug before 1685, because the 1685–1701 garden accounts do not mention digging it. Later accounts show it cleaned, with two rows of hedges and iron gates added in 1701–1706.

It has changed since. The Duke of Kent added the Pavilion at its southern end in 1709–1711, and by 1719 the northern end, where you stand, had a spade-shaped head with two arms of water. Capability Brown softened the outer canals in 1758–1760 but left the Long Water alone. The north end was cut back in the later 1700s to extend it and later backfilled, as the 1882 map shows, so today's head is not exactly the Greys' original. OpenStreetMap shows the water about 330 metres long, though the whole axis, house to Pavilion, is about 800 metres.

It is formal for a reason. The straight canal was French taste, as at Versailles, and Charles II dug one at Hampton Court about 1670. After the Glorious Revolution of 1688 the Dutch style of William III followed, more modest. The future Duke toured the Netherlands, and his letters home mention tree-lined canals.

The water is fed by a stream that enters the garden's canals at the north-west corner, from Silsoe village, with conduits and cross-canals carrying it to the head of the Long Water. Archaeologists found brick land drains on its west bank flowing west to east, and the earliest water pipes, of terracotta and planked channels, are mentioned in 1703. The south end is held by a dam, with the Pavilion's causeway on it, and sluices control the levels. The banks are clay, which is puddled to stop leaks. The garden's original spring, behind the Bath House, no longer flows, so a circulating pump has been used since 1972. Calm water is a mirror, doubling the Pavilion.

On the east bank is a statue of Mercury, 2.5 metres high, with a winged Cupid at his feet. The listing calls it "Mercury and Cupid", probably late seventeenth century and perhaps by Caius Gabriel Cibber, based on Giambologna's Mercury of 1588, and moved here in the late nineteenth century. Bedfordshire Archives dates it mid-nineteenth century. The listing puts it 70 metres from the north end, but photographs place it nearer 200 metres down. Neptune (west), Juno (north-west) and Leda (north-east) stand along the canal.

## Amabel, Anthony and Mary: the makers of the Long Water
Kind: person
About: anthony-11th-earl, amabel-countess-of-kent, mary-baroness-lucas
Amabel Benn (about 1606–1698) was the daughter of Anthony Benn, Recorder of London, and the widow of Anthony Fane when, in 1644, she became the second wife of Henry Grey, 10th Earl of Kent. He died in 1651, leaving her a boy not yet six, Anthony, to bring up. Bedfordshire Archives calls her "the Good Countess". She stayed a dominant force at Wrest until she died in 1698: she bought land in the 1650s, leased the Cainhoe warren in 1656, and kept the accounts for the north front.

Anthony Grey (born 11 June 1645) succeeded as 11th Earl on 28 May 1651. He married Mary Lucas on 2 March 1663. Mary was the only surviving child of John, Baron Lucas of Shenfield, and was made Baroness Lucas of Crudwell in her own right in 1663. When her father died on 2 July 1671 she inherited a large estate in Wiltshire and Essex, and Henry, later Duke of Kent, was born that year. Bedfordshire Archives and the History of Parliament both link the wealth and the birth to the classical north front, which cost £3,227; the History of Parliament says about half came from the Lucas inheritance.

From 1686 Anthony and Amabel began a new walled formal garden south of the house, with fountains, two wildernesses and a terrace walk, costing over £3,000 until 1701. The Long Water, probably dug before 1685, is their best-known work. English Heritage credits the three of them together. The 2013 survey says most of the central garden shown on the Kip and Knyff views, the house, Great Court, walled garden and Long Canal, was their creation.

Anthony died on 19 August 1702 playing bowls at Tunbridge Wells, apparently from a stroke. Mary died on 1 November 1702, and both were buried at Flitton. Their son Henry inherited and became Duke of Kent in 1710. Anthony's marriage is dated 1662 in some sources because the year then began in March; it is 1663 by modern reckoning.
