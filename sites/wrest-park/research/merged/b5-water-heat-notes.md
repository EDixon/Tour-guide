## The Orangery
Kind: site
About: orangery
The Orangery stands on a terrace west of the French Parterre, on the site of an earlier greenhouse that Batty Langley designed for the Duke of Kent. Thomas Philip, 2nd Earl de Grey designed it himself, with James Clephan as clerk of works. He took the idea from a plate in Jacques-François Blondel's Maisons de Plaisance (also the source for his new house) and added classical ornament and a tall French dome at each end. Historic England lists it Grade II* and calls an orangery in this French style unique in England. Dates differ: the listing says about 1835, but a Historic England research report on the Bath House (2018) says the Orangery was built in 1838 or 1839, and the 2015 earthwork survey says about 1840. The Earl's new house went up in 1834–1839, and the Historic England landscape report says the Orangery followed soon after it.

It is yellow brick, with the front and sides in cement render. The main roof is glass on a shallow cast-iron frame. Cast iron is strong in compression, so slim glazing bars block less winter light than timber would (general reasoning, not stated in the listing). It faces east over the garden on grassed terraces, with broad central steps, smaller steps to the south and a ramped path round the north side. The rear is plain brick with five arched doors into a vaulted undercroft of thirteen bays and thirteen semicircular windows above.

Heat and water: the listing says the floor "contained heating grilles", replaced by ceramic tiles after 1948, and that a cast-iron pump near the north wall was "formerly used for pumping water up from the tanks in the undercroft". English Heritage's head gardener told Albion Archaeology in 2007 that rainwater from the roof was originally held in tanks in the undercroft, and that the hydraulic ram pump from the garden's water system, taken out in the 1980s, is now stored there. No source found says how the grilles were fed (boiler, hot water or warm air), so do not guess.

The orange trees were bought from King Louis-Philippe of France. In summer they stood in tubs on the terraced parterre below, and by about 1900 they were among the largest in England. The front doors were too low for grown trees, so the north end has a full-height double door of render over timber, disguised as wall.

Inside, a stone fireplace with the de Grey arms comes from the old house. The listing dates it about 1600; Historic England's 2013 report, following the Earl's own 1831 caption, says about 1570, with the arms of Edmund Grey, 1st Earl of Kent. Bedfordshire Archives says it was only installed in the Orangery in the twentieth century. On the steps are four early-1700s stone cherubs, probably by an unknown Flemish sculptor, holding a book, a puppy, a bird's nest and a torch. The Orangery was conserved and repainted in 2011.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1158549; Bedfordshire Archives, Buildings at Wrest Park; Historic England Research Report 6/2013 (landscape investigations); Historic England Research Report 74/2018 (Bath House); Historic England Research Report 84/2015; Albion Archaeology, Old Park Weir (2007); English Heritage, Revitalising Wrest Park.

## The Bath House and Cascade
Kind: site
About: bath-house, bath-house-cascade, bath-house-bath-filled, bath-house-restoration, bath-house-pipes-lead, recirculating-pump
The Bath House is a cold plunge bath for Jemima, Marchioness Grey, and her husband Philip Yorke, built about 1769–1771 by Edward Stevens. It is a single-storey figure-of-eight: a circular bath room to the south and an octagonal dressing room to the north. Contemporaries called it a Roman bath, a hermitage and a cold bath. In June 1771 Horace Walpole praised "a hermitage and cold bath" at Wrest in "bold good taste". It was really used: in 1784 the vicar asked for a key to the gardens to bathe for his health.

Construction: local ironstone, dressed inside and rough-hewn outside, with big round stones as lintels. Historic England says the rough ironstone shows "considerable engineering skill". The outer rusticated skin may have been applied after the shell was built, and early drawings show buttresses (now gone), perhaps to suggest a ruin propped up. The bath room has a stone dome with holes in it, three large ragged openings according to the listing, built to look ruined. The dressing room has a conical thatched roof, tall niches for clothes, and a floor of pebbles and deer bones in a central circle and eight radiating sections. It is uncertain how much is original, as the floor was redone in the 1960s.

The bath is a round tank in two stages with a stone path round the lower one. Narrow steps run down on either side of the doorway, rebuilt in the 1960s from slight remains. The lion's-head spout above is also from that restoration; the original inlet is unknown, though the listing calls it a lead lion's head.

Water: the bath was fed by pipes from a spring. They were wood, mended in 1783 and replaced in 1785, until 1801–1802, when boggy ground wrecking the wooden pipes led the agent to favour lead; lead pipes were laid in 1802. The Cascade, a mound with a rough arch, was built over the spring head. The listing says its chamber holds a lead-lined tank, but the 2015 survey found a concrete-lined tank, probably replacing an earlier one. The spring was flowing in 1952, then dried up as boreholes drew down the water table. Since about 1972 a pump on the Broad Water recirculates water to the Cascade. Earl de Grey thought the "chalybeate" (iron-rich) claim was wrong; later accounts disagree.

Later history: the Earl filled the bath in 1834, after hot and cold baths were fitted in the old house in 1827–1828. The ministry underpinned the building in 1954 and rebuilt it in the 1960s, re-thatching the dressing room and digging out the bath. English Heritage restored the grounds in 2019 and the Cascade and bridge in 2022.

Steps: the bath room's steps lead down into the bath; three steps rise to the dressing room; and the 2015 survey found a stepped path down the east side of the Cascade mound. Which of these the leaflet marks is unconfirmed.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1113790; Historic England Research Report 74/2018 (Kewley); Research Report 84/2015 (Alexander, Newsome and Chamberlin); Albion Archaeology 2007 (Old Park Weir) and 2016/177; English Heritage, Revitalising Wrest Park.

## The Capability Brown column (the Rustic Column)
Kind: site
About: brown-column, brown-river, capability-brown
The monument credited to Capability Brown is a tall stone column on a plinth, about 6 metres high, with two rough rusticated blocks breaking the shaft. Its inscription reads: "These gardens originally laid out by Henry Duke of Kent, were altered by Philip Earl of Hardwicke and Jemima Marchioness Grey, with the professional assistance of Lancelot Brown Esq. in the years 1758, 1759, 1760." Earl de Grey called it the Rustic Column. The listing (Grade II) says it was erected in 1760 and is attributed to Edward Stevens. Historic England's 2018 report reads the inscription as marking the end of Brown's works, so the column was probably put up later in the 1760s, perhaps in 1769–1771 when Stevens was active at Wrest; the 2013 report gives 1770–1772. (Stevens was born about 1744, so he was about sixteen in 1760.)

What tops it is disputed. Bedfordshire Archives says a pineapple, the Historic England listing says a finial ornamented with acanthus leaves, and the 2018 report calls it an urn-topped Tuscan column (the listing says Doric). A close look on the day would settle it.

Where it stands: Earl de Grey wrote in the 1830s that it was first put up "as an object to look at" from the Bath House and was moved in 1828 by Countess de Grey. The listing repeats 1828, but the 2015 survey puts the move at about 1830 and certainly by 1831. The 2015 excavations found its original site near the Great Yew Hedge, now marked by a plaque, visible from the Cascade's top. The listing suspects the rustication was added later to echo the rustic Bath House.

Brown at Wrest: Jemima (or Philip, who also employed Brown at Wimpole) hired him in 1758. English Heritage says he realised that to do more "might unravel the Mystery of the Gardens". The listing says he laid the waters together and made the straight canals meander. He returned briefly in 1778 and 1779. No Brown drawings for Wrest survive. Walpole attributed the Bath House to him, but Historic England thinks Stevens designed it.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1158972; Historic England Research Report 74/2018; Research Report 84/2015; Research Report 6/2013; Bedfordshire Archives, Monuments and Ornaments at Wrest Park; English Heritage, History of Wrest Park; Landscape Institute, Capability Brown at Wrest.

## The boundary canals ("the river")
Kind: site
About: boundary-canals, brown-river
The water round the west, south and east edges of the old garden, which visitors often call the river, is a chain of canals: Old Park Water on the west, the Serpentine to the south-west and the Broad Water on the south and east. It began as straight, formal canals dug for Henry Grey, Duke of Kent, as part of his Great Garden, and Albion Archaeology says most took their shape between 1702 and 1720. The canals were altered in the 1720s, and a serpentine canal was made in 1729. To the south, the garden ended at the Old Brook, still the boundary between the parishes of Silsoe and Gravenhurst.

In 1758–1760 Jemima, Marchioness Grey, and her husband Philip Yorke brought in Lancelot "Capability" Brown. Jemima valued her grandfather's garden, so his changes were limited. Jemima's letters describe the job as laying "the waters together around the garden", so the canals read as one stream winding among planting. Where levels differed more than digging could cure, a bank of earth was needed, which planting was to conceal. That is why the "river" curves while the Long Water stays straight.

A column about 6 metres tall records the work: see the section on the Capability Brown column for its inscription, finial and the dispute over its date and moves. The Bath House (about 1769–1771, by Stevens, for Jemima) stands on the west bank of the Bath House Water, an arm of the system fed by a spring, with the Cascade at its north end.

Everything holds water because of the clay: the banks are puddled clay, and the levels depend on weirs, sluices and culverts (see "How Wrest's water works"). Old Park Water is the highest of the chain, and the others are supplied from it.
Sources: Historic England register entry 1000113; list entries 1158972 and 1113790; Albion Archaeology, Old Park Weir (2007), quoting Bedfordshire Archives CRT 190/45; English Heritage, History of Wrest Park.

## How Wrest's water works: supply, levels and drainage
Kind: site
About: boundary-canals, brown-river, bath-house, orangery, recirculating-pump, old-park-weir-raised
Wrest Park's canals and lakes are man-made on gault clay, held in by clay banks, stone weirs and sluices. Albion Archaeology's 2007 report, built on a talk with English Heritage's head gardener, gives the plumbing. Apart from the circulating pump for the Bath House Cascade, the flow appears to be by gravity.

Supply and flow: a stream from the north-west (a farm ditch, which also carries run-off from the Silsoe bypass) feeds Old Park Water, the highest of the chain. The stream enters above the Bath House Pond (the Lily Pond), which spills into Old Park Water over a stepped sluice. Water passes Old Park Weir into the Serpentine, which falls 4–5 feet at its own weir, towards the Broad Water. The River Hit enters the south Broad Water through a stop-cock sluice. An underground drain with an adjustable pipe feeds Leg o' Mutton Lake from Old Park Water. Its exits carry excess water into the Long Canal, which passes it by pipe to Ladies Lake. Ladies Lake's outflow joins the north Broad Water near the Chinese Bridge. The south-east corner of the Long Canal has a wooden sluice gate, with an underground pipe running past the Archer Pavilion to a big brick culvert (5 feet square) that carries water out under the Broad Water to the River Hit.

Making the Long Canal: Albion's chronology says it was made about 1686–1688 by building a dam across a shallow valley (English Heritage says the early 1680s). Historic England's 2013 survey records an east-west dam and a causeway running from it to the Archer Pavilion.

Leaks and floods: the weirs are sandstone and ironstone blocks in mortar, and leaks are stopped by puddling the clay. About 1971 Old Park Weir was raised 6–10 inches in concrete to hold more water for the research institute's irrigation. By the 2000s this was blamed for flooding between the Orangery and Bath House; blocked clay pipes were dug out in 2003.

House supply: a hydraulic ram in a brick chamber in the woodland near the Bowling Green took water from Old Park Water and pushed it up to two 500-gallon lead tanks in the roof of the mansion, supplying house, stables and garden, with overflow feeding the marble and Atlas fountains. The sources do not date the ram. It was removed in the 1980s and is stored in the Orangery undercroft. A hydraulic ram uses the force of water suddenly stopping in a pipe to lift a part of it uphill, with no fuel (general knowledge).

Drying and refilling: the Bath House spring dried up (see the Bath House section), and a pump installed about 1972 circulates water from the north Broad Water. The canals were drained in the Second World War, and dredged in 1952, about 1965, 1973 and 1988–1989.
Sources: Albion Archaeology, Hydrology Restoration: Old Park Weir (2007/47) and Evaluation 2016/177; Historic England Research Report 84/2015; Research Report 74/2018; Historic England list entry 1158549; English Heritage, History of Wrest Park; general knowledge (hydraulic ram principle).

## The service yard: brewhouse, horse pump and fire engine
Kind: site
About: service-range
The service range at the east end of the new house was designed by Earl de Grey in 1834–1839, with James Clephan as clerk of works. Historic England lists the former brewhouse, link block, pump house and fire-engine house together as Grade II (2012). They sit beside the stable block because two of them needed horses.

The pump house was open-ended, aligned east to west, and driven by a horse; it adjoined a well in the courtyard. The listing does not say where the water went, and no equipment survives. A horse engine (or gin) is a beam or capstan turned by a horse walking in a circle, which drove a pump (general knowledge, not from the listing). The sources found do not say where this pumped water went, or how the pump house relates to the ram and the roof tanks.

The fire-engine house is a big double-height shed at right angles to the range, built for a horse-drawn fire engine. A fire engine of the 1830s was a hand- or horse-worked pump on a cart, and a country house kept one because the nearest brigade was far away (general knowledge).

The brewhouse brewed beer for the household and was linked to the main house by a beer pipe. By 1934 the building held water-softening equipment. Most of it is now offices and storage.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1403022; general knowledge (horse engines and fire engines); Albion Archaeology (2007), Old Park Weir, for the ram.

## The Archer Pavilion
Kind: site
About: pavilion, pavilion-paint, pavilion-roof-repair
The Archer Pavilion was designed by Thomas Archer and built in 1709–1711 for Henry Grey, then Marquess of Kent, who became Duke of Kent in 1710 while it was going up. It cost £1,809. Historic England lists it Grade I and calls it "a miniature masterpiece in the English Baroque style". It stands at the south end of the Long Water on the house's axis, closing the view and giving a view back.

Plan: borrowed from Borromini's Sant'Ivo in Rome, two interlocking triangles making a six-pointed star. Archer made three points semicircular and three rectangular. A central circular block carries a lead-covered dome with an Ionic lantern, an iron balustrade and a ball finial. The building is red and yellow brick on a base of channelled red brick, with tiny semi-domes where the curved wings meet the drum, and round "bull's-eye" windows. Brick walls in the audited area were about 50 centimetres thick. The exterior was originally rendered; it was later stripped back to the brick.

The Great Room is circular and open to the dome. Hauduroy painted it in 1712, probably in oil on plaster, and signed "Hauduroy pinxit 1712". It shows a coffered dome, Corinthian columns, urns, statues and Grey badges. The listing calls him Louis Hauduroy; English Heritage says Mark Anthony. He is one of two known surviving English schemes by him. The painting hides doors to spiral stairs: up to servants' bedrooms and down to a brick kitchen and a double privy. The two ground-floor closets served as bedrooms.

Roof: the heavy lead slowly slipped down the dome, letting rain into the roof void and damaging the lath-and-plaster ceiling beneath the painting. English Heritage repaired the dome in 2016–2017, with the lead due for replacement, and a bat roost in the dome needed a licence. A crack in the dome was treated in 1952. The 1990s audit found the lantern's timber and canvas in poor state.

Setting: it stands at the south end of an earth causeway running from a dam. A 1716 account mentions matting and "double deals for closets" for a "new building below the gardens", probably this. The "goose foot" of paths round it was restored in 2011.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1113807; Ancient Monuments Laboratory Report 28/97; Historic England Research Report 6/2013; FPCR Environment, Archer Pavilion at Wrest Park; English Heritage, Revitalising Wrest Park; English Heritage, History of Wrest Park.

## Thomas Archer, architect
Kind: person
About: thomas-archer
Thomas Archer (about 1668–1743) was the youngest son of a Warwickshire gentry family. He went to Trinity College, Oxford, in 1686, then spent four years abroad in the early 1690s, including Italy, where he saw the Roman Baroque of Bernini and Borromini at first hand. Historic England calls him "the only architect to bring a direct experience of European Baroque to England".

He was an amateur: from 1705 he held the court post of Groom Porter, which licensed gambling, and from 1715 he was comptroller of customs at Newcastle. Most of his commissions came from the aristocracy. He helped the 1st Duke of Devonshire at Chatsworth, giving the house its curved north front and probably building the domed cascade-house, and worked on Heythrop in Oxfordshire. Other works are St Philip's in Birmingham (1708–1715), St Paul's Deptford (1712–1730) and St John's, Smith Square, London (1713–1728).

At Wrest he also designed Cain Hill House (about 1712–1715, now demolished) for the Duke of Kent, but the Pavilion is the part that survives.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1113807 (education, travels, Chatsworth, Heythrop, Cain Hill House); Wikipedia, Thomas Archer (Groom Porter, comptroller, church dates).

## Edward Stevens, architect
Kind: person
About: edward-stevens
Edward Stevens (about 1744–1775) was an architect who died young and is little known. He was William Chambers's pupil from 1760 to 1766, when he set up on his own. His principal work seems to have been Doveridge Hall, Derbyshire, designed in 1769, built 1770–1777 and demolished in 1938. He went to study in Italy in 1774 and died there soon after.

At Wrest he is the documented architect of the Bath House: in September 1769 he wrote to Lord Hardwicke that he had nearly finished the front of the arch over the Spring Head, and Lord Hardwicke paid him £1,898 between August 1770 and July 1773. He also designed the Chinese Bridge of 1770, drew up a "Theatre or Colonnade" (not built), made alterations to the house, and is believed to have designed the column commemorating Brown. Historic England's 2018 report says the Bath House is one of several designs that owe something to Jemima and Philip's own taste and to their tutor Thomas Wright.
Sources: Historic England Research Report 74/2018 (Kewley); Historic England list entry 1158972.

## Lancelot "Capability" Brown
Kind: person
About: capability-brown, brown-river
Lancelot Brown (1716–1783) was the most famous English landscape designer of the 18th century. Born at Kirkharle in Northumberland, he became head gardener at Stowe in 1741, then worked all over England, reshaping more than 170 parks. His nickname came from telling clients their land had "capability" for improvement. His style swept away straight lines for rolling grass, clumps and belts of trees, and serpentine lakes made by damming streams, as at Blenheim. In 1764 he became the King's Master Gardener at Hampton Court.

At Wrest he was held back, which is why the formal garden survives. He worked there in 1758–1760, brought in by Jemima or her husband Philip, who also employed him at Wimpole. His brief was to curve and join the boundary canals, open views and plant. He visited briefly again in 1778 and 1779. No drawings survive, so no one knows exactly what is his. Some of his drainage schemes elsewhere did not work, and Historic England wonders if the drains at Wrest were the same. Historic England does not think he designed the Bath House.

Earth-moving in his day was done by hand: gangs of labourers with spades and wheelbarrows, with horses and carts for the soil.
Sources: Historic England Research Report 74/2018; Historic England list entry 1158972; Research Report 6/2013; general knowledge (Brown's career).
