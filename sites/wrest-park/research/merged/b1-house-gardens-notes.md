## The Conservatory
Kind: site
About: conservatory, italian-garden
The conservatory is a glass-roofed room at the west end of Wrest Park house, built with the house in 1834–1839 for Thomas Philip, 2nd Earl de Grey. Historic England's listing calls it an "elegant conservatory which has a glazed roof supported by ornate cast-iron trusses". It sits in one of the single-storey end pavilions of the house, and it closes the west enfilade, the line of state rooms that runs through the house. The room just before it is the Countess's sitting room, "more feminine" than the rest, with delicate plasterwork and putti hanging from the ceiling, and the conservatory opens straight off it.

From the conservatory a door leads out to the Italian Garden to the west. English Heritage says that visitors can look through the conservatory to the Italian Garden and the walled garden beyond, just as Henrietta, Countess de Grey did when the house was new in the 1830s. That view, from sitting room through glass to a formal garden, was part of the design.

How it works as a structure. Cast iron is strong when it is squeezed (compression) but brittle when it is stretched (tension). Builders of the 1830s therefore shaped cast-iron roof members as arches or triangulated trusses, so that most of the load goes into compression, and often used wrought iron for the stretched parts such as tie rods. The result is a roof of slim iron ribs with large panes of glass between them, far lighter than stone or timber. The listing says the trusses are cast iron and ornate; no source found names the iron founder or describes the glazing in detail.

Where it fits in history. Iron-and-glass building was new and fashionable: Joseph Paxton's Great Conservatory at Chatsworth was built in 1836–1840, and the Crystal Palace followed in 1851, about twelve to seventeen years after Wrest's conservatory. Glass was still taxed by weight until the duty was abolished in 1845, which kept large glass roofs expensive.

Heating. No source opened for this guide says how the conservatory was heated. Plant houses of this date were usually warmed by hot-water pipes or flues fed from a boiler outside, but that is general practice, not evidence about Wrest. The Earl wrote his own account of building the house, printed in the Bedfordshire Historical Record Society's Miscellanea (2024), which may answer it.

Recent history. English Heritage planned in 2008 to restore the conservatory and the Countess's sitting room, and says the conservatory was fully repaired and planted up as part of the 2011 restoration. Recent photographs show it planted with palms and other tender plants. The visitor entrance and lift are at this end of the house, and pushchairs stay outside.

## The Italian Garden
Kind: site
About: italian-garden, ceres-statue
The Italian Garden is a formal, square garden on the west side of the house, beside the conservatory. Historic England's listing of its structures dates it to about 1839, in the first years after the Earl built the new house, and says the statue of Ceres was probably introduced then. It is a Grade II listing; the garden itself lies within the Grade I registered park.

The layout. Gravel paths divide the garden into four quarters. Moulded stone curbs outline geometric beds around a circular central bed. There are three entrances: from the conservatory on the east side, and from the garden through gates on the west and south. The west gate is wrought iron, hung on two stone piers topped with vases; the south entrance is flanked by similar vases on plinths with carved panels. "Italian" here means formal and symmetrical. English Heritage describes the gardens' bedding as "fancy French curves and bold Italian geometry".

The statue. At the north end of the path, opposite the south entrance, stands Ceres, the Roman goddess of harvests and the earth's abundance. She holds flowers in one hand and rests the other on an overflowing cornucopia, on a moulded stone pedestal decorated with a scale pattern. Some photo captions call her Pomona, the goddess of fruit; the listing says Ceres. Bedfordshire Archives describes the same figure only as a mid-nineteenth-century marble woman with a large cornucopia of fruit and flowers.

What has been lost. The listing says four figure groups once stood in the quarters and that vases flanked the entrances. The groups have been removed and the vases replaced with plainer ones.

When was it laid out? Sources disagree. The listing says the garden was laid out or modified about 1839. A Heritage Fund news page says its layout "dates from 1882", while English Heritage's own conservation page says the beds were restored to their early-nineteenth-century design. I would give 1839 as the listing date and treat 1882 as a possible later alteration, unconfirmed.

Restoration. English Heritage restored the garden in 2011, after detailed research and archaeological survey. Broken stone edging was conserved and re-laid, and low-maintenance foliage planting was replaced by seasonal bedding such as tulips, wallflowers, hyacinths, dahlias and pelargoniums. Because the garden lies beside the conservatory and the walled garden, the view through the glass from the house is the best way to understand it.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1321790 (Italian Garden structures); Bedfordshire Archives, Statues at Wrest Park; Heritage Fund, "Grand garden revival at Grade I registered Wrest Park"; English Heritage, "Revitalising Wrest Park"; English Heritage, seasonal garden highlights page.

## The French Parterre
Kind: site
About: parterre, parterre-restored, mermaid-basin
The French Parterre is the formal garden of low box-edged beds below the terrace on the south front of the house. English Heritage says the upper gardens laid out in the 1830s between the new house and the earlier gardens were created in the French style, and Parks & Gardens UK describes a "formal 1830s parterre" on the south garden front. It was laid out for Thomas Philip, 2nd Earl de Grey to go with his French-style house (built 1834–1839).

A parterre is meant to be read as a pattern from above, so the best view is from the terrace or the house windows. The central gravel walk runs south from the house along the main axis toward the Long Water and the Archer Pavilion. Wooden Versailles planters, with Portuguese laurels alternating with pencil junipers, stand along it, and four lead statue groups flank the walk (see the section on the lead groups). English Heritage has also planted an avenue of 26 disease-resistant elms to replace the English elms lost to disease that once framed the parterre.

Restoration. Much of the intricate detail was lost in the early twentieth century, when the parterre was simplified to make it easier to maintain. English Heritage carried out a full-area archaeological excavation, which revealed the original beds and gravel layout, and restored the parterre in 2012 to reflect the Earl's design. Investigation took place in autumn 2011 (Heritage Fund).

The Mermaid Basin. On the west side of the parterre stands the Mermaid Basin, Grade II listed. It is Italian veined Carrara marble on a stone base, about 1.5 metres high. Four seated mermaids (or nereids) with double tails curl either side of a central pier decorated with rockwork, and each raises a hand to support a large fountain bowl, which is now missing; Bedfordshire Archives says the original broad basin has been replaced by a small one. Sources disagree on its date. Historic England thinks it is late nineteenth century, probably put up by Francis, 7th Earl Cowper (1834–1905), who inherited in 1880: the only evidence is a Country Life article of 9 July 1904 that mentions it, so it was acquired between 1880 and 1904. Bedfordshire Archives (2011) says "mid 19th century", probably by the 2nd Earl de Grey. Historic England's is the later, better-argued dating, but neither is certain.

French style at Wrest. The Earl's taste for the French style began before the house: his entrance lodges at Silsoe of about 1826 were his first Louis XV buildings. The parterre completes the idea: a French château with a French garden.
Sources: English Heritage, History of Wrest Park; English Heritage, "Revitalising Wrest Park"; Heritage Fund, "Grand garden revival at Grade I registered Wrest Park"; Parks & Gardens UK, Wrest Park; Historic England list entry 1158506 (Mermaid Basin, read via British Listed Buildings); Bedfordshire Archives, Monuments and Ornaments at Wrest Park; Bedfordshire Archives, Buildings at Wrest Park.

## The four lead statue groups on the parterre
Kind: site
About: parterre-statues, parterre-statues-restored, john-cheere
Four large lead statue groups flank the central walk of the French Parterre, two on each side. Historic England lists them together at Grade II* as "Four Statuary Groups in the South Parterre", dated about 1730 and attributed to John Cheere (1709–1787), the prolific London statue maker with a yard at Hyde Park Corner. Each group is about 3 metres high, in painted lead on splayed Portland stone bases that are probably nineteenth century.

The four groups. Venus and Adonis stands about 25 metres south-east of the house, with a seated Venus, Cupid releasing a dove (a symbol of departing love) and Adonis ready to leave with his hunting dog. Meleager and Atalanta stands about 25 metres south-west, with a standing Meleager holding a shepherd's crook and a seated Atalanta, a dog and a boar's head under the dog's paw. Aeneas and Anchises stands about 50 metres south-east: Aeneas strides out carrying his old father Anchises on his shoulder, with his son Ascanius beside him and a fallen woman at his feet, the escape from Troy in Virgil's Aeneid. The Abduction of Helen of Troy stands about 50 metres south-west: a striding soldier carries off a struggling woman, with a crouched man and a child, loosely derived from a work by the French sculptor Philippe Bertrand.

Naming muddle. Bedfordshire Archives calls the Meleager group "Diana and Endymion" and the Abduction of Helen "the Rape of the Sabine Women". Historic England's 2012 entry says the Meleager group was previously misidentified as Diana and Endymion. Some photo captions on Wikimedia Commons follow the older names, and a couple of them appear to be swapped between statues.

Where they came from. The listing says they are believed to come from Cheere's surviving stock, dispersed at the end of the eighteenth century, and passed through dealers or collectors before the Earl bought them. A view of 1839 shows them in their present positions. English Heritage's collection page says Venus and Adonis is "late 18th century" and was brought to Wrest about 1839, which differs from the listing's about 1730. English Heritage says many of Wrest's lead statues were melted down in 1809 by Amabel, Countess de Grey, so these groups are later arrivals.

Why white? Cheere worked in lead painted white to look like marble from a distance, because imported marble weathered badly in Britain.

Restoration. In April 2021 English Heritage announced the first phase of a wider project to restore the parterre statues. Conservators from Skillington Workshop removed the peeling paint with high-temperature steam, which does not damage the surface beneath, then repainted the figures to recreate the marble effect. Venus and Adonis and Aeneas and Anchises, both over 3 metres tall, were done first. Wrest has more than 70 statues and monuments across its 90 acres.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1113789 (Four Statuary Groups, South Parterre); English Heritage press release, 22 April 2021 (statue restoration); English Heritage, Wrest Park collection highlights; Bedfordshire Archives, Statues at Wrest Park.

## John Cheere, statue maker
Kind: person
About: john-cheere
John Cheere (1709–1787) was a London sculptor whose workshop at Hyde Park Corner made garden and interior statues in lead and plaster, mostly copies of classical and Renaissance works, for country houses across England. English Heritage describes him as a major manufacturer of lead statues in eighteenth-century London. His figures were usually painted white to imitate marble, though his sculpture yard also made coloured rustic pieces: English Heritage's collection includes a Cheere lead "Columbine" painted in bright, lifelike colours, with traces of paint left in the nostrils and mouth.

At Wrest, the four lead groups on the French Parterre are attributed to him by Historic England, which dates them about 1730 and says they probably came from his stock after it was dispersed at the end of the eighteenth century. The statue of William III north of the Archer Pavilion is also thought to be by Cheere. English Heritage's collection page says a Venus and Adonis may have been part of the stock auctioned after his death in 1787 and brought to Wrest about 1839. The Earl de Grey, who built the new house in 1834–1839, bought the groups for his parterre.

His yard worked like a garden-sculpture shop: an owner could pick a classical figure from stock, which made statues affordable (general knowledge, verify). Painted lead needs repainting from time to time: in 2021 conservators steamed off flaking paint from two of the Wrest groups and repainted them.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1113789; English Heritage, Wrest Park collection highlights; English Heritage press release, 22 April 2021; existing Wrest notes on the statue of William III (Historic England list entry 1158878); general knowledge for the catalogue-style trade (verify).

## The Dairy Sculpture Gallery
Kind: site
About: dairy, dairy-gallery
The ornamental dairy is a small building at the south side of the service range, to the east of the house, projecting at right angles from a garden wall. Historic England's listing of the house and service block (built 1834–1839 for the 2nd Earl de Grey) describes it as an "ornamental dairy, unfortunately much altered", with a verandah, stained glass windows and a cupola. The listing says its internal fittings have not survived. In 1982 an extension was built that incorporated the dairy by knocking through its north wall.

The dairy once supplied butter and cream to the de Grey family. Ornamental dairies were a fashion of country houses, places where the work of the dairy was done in a pretty building, cool in summer, and where the family could show visitors the process; the one at Wrest was designed to match the French-style house. (The fashion is general knowledge, not a Wrest-specific source.) English Heritage's conservation page says the ornamental dairy was restored and used as a sculpture gallery, listed under its 2012 work.

Today it is the Dairy Sculpture Gallery, where garden statues too fragile to stay outdoors are displayed. English Heritage names a Portland stone Neptune and a bronze sundial dated 1682 among the highlights. The listing for the sundial in the gardens also dates it to 1682, so the two may be related, but no source found says so.

Other sculpture at Wrest. English Heritage says the gardens and the dairy together hold a remarkable collection of eighteenth- and nineteenth-century statues. Highlights on its collection page include Atlas, a Cheere lead Columbine, a lead Diana of the 1720s (bought by English Heritage at auction in 2011 with Art Fund help, and put back in the garden at the head of Ladies Lake), a Hawking Party of 1839–1857 whose horse was carved in 1839 for the stable block entrance, and "A Nymph Going to the Bath" of 1837, bought by the Earl, who commissioned a companion piece from Richard James Wyatt. The pair flanked the entrance to the Ante-Library. The page does not say which of these is in the dairy.

The dairy stands close to the Petit Trianon playhouse and the old stable block, east of the house.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1311484 (house and service block, including the dairy); English Heritage, Wrest Park "uncovered treasures" page; English Heritage, "Revitalising Wrest Park"; English Heritage, Wrest Park collection highlights; Historic England list entry 1113797 (sundial); general knowledge for ornamental dairies (verify).

## Le Petit Trianon playhouse
Kind: site
About: petit-trianon
Le Petit Trianon, also called the Wendy House, is a small log playhouse near the house and the old stable block, built in 1856. Historic England listed it at Grade II in 2012 (list entry 1402866). It is a single-storey structure of split logs on a timber frame, standing on a low brick wall, with a tiled roof and a brick chimney. The gable ends have decorative split-log work and plain carved bargeboards. Inside are three rooms, which were described in 1885 as a kitchen, a scullery and a sitting room, with tongue-and-groove panelling, tiled floors, a brick-surrounded fireplace with a basket grate, and a panelled wall cupboard. Old records mention a sundial and a workshop, but the listing found no evidence of them. The roof and much of the split-log work were replaced in the late twentieth century after a tree fell on the building. Parks & Gardens UK records it as a three-roomed log cabin dated 1856.

Who was it for? The listing says it was built "for his children as a playhouse or summerhouse", following the fashion for rustic wooden buildings in gardens. The "he" is Thomas Philip, 2nd Earl de Grey (born 1781). In 1856 he was 74, and his two daughters were long grown up: his elder daughter, Anne Florence, was the mother of Francis Cowper, born in 1834. So the listing's "his children" is puzzling: grandchildren would fit better. No source found settles it, and the grandchildren idea is an inference, not a fact.

The name. It is named after the Petit Trianon at Versailles, the small château Louis XVI gave to Marie Antoinette, whose grounds held a make-believe rustic hamlet (see the Versailles section). No source found says why the Earl chose the name, or that the log house copies the hamlet: the name is the link. It fits his French taste: the house copies 18th-century French designs, and his Silsoe entrance lodges of about 1826 were his first Louis XV-style buildings.

Nearby, an "American Garden" was laid out in 1857, according to the listing.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1402866 (read via British Listed Buildings); Parks & Gardens UK, Wrest Park; Bedfordshire Archives, Buildings at Wrest Park; Wikipedia, Thomas de Grey, 2nd Earl de Grey (for his daughters; lead only); Historic England list entry 1158506 (for Francis Cowper's dates).

## Marie Antoinette and the Petit Trianon at Versailles
Kind: reference
About: marie-antoinette, maria-theresa, versailles-petit-trianon
The Petit Trianon is a small neoclassical château in the grounds of Versailles, built in 1761–1768 by the architect Ange-Jacques Gabriel for Louis XV. When Louis XVI came to the throne in 1774 he gave it to his queen, Marie Antoinette, who made it her private retreat, where she could escape the formality of court.

Marie Antoinette (born 2 November 1755, executed 16 October 1793) was an Austrian archduchess, a Habsburg: the youngest daughter of Empress Maria Theresa and of Francis I, the Holy Roman Emperor. Her marriage to the future Louis XVI in 1770 sealed the alliance between the Habsburgs and the Bourbons of France. This is the Habsburg link to Wrest: the playhouse borrows the name of her favourite house.

In the Petit Trianon park she had the Queen's Hamlet (Hameau de la Reine) built about 1783–1786 by Richard Mique, after designs influenced by the painter Hubert Robert: about ten rustic cottages and a farm around a lake, where she and her friends played at country life. The hamlet is the best-known example of the eighteenth-century fashion for make-believe rustic buildings. No Wrest source ties the log playhouse to it.

Why it matters at Wrest. The 2nd Earl de Grey built his house in 1834–1839 in the French style of the Louis XV era, copying a design from Blondel's Maisons de Plaisance (1737) and the Hôtel de Matignon in Paris. The name of his playhouse fits that taste, but no source documents the Earl's reasoning.
Sources: general knowledge (Château de Versailles, Petit Trianon and Hameau de la Reine; verify dates against the Château's own pages, which could not be opened); Historic England list entry 1311484 (house copies Blondel and the Hôtel de Matignon, per existing notes); Historic England list entry 1402866.
