## From horses to tractors in Britain
Kind: reference
About: ivel-agricultural-motor, fordson-model-f, ferguson-te20, dan-albone, henry-ford
For thousands of years British farms ran on muscle: oxen, then mostly horses. A 1951 University of Nottingham study put working horses on farms in England and Wales at nearly a million in 1910, falling to 549,000 by 1939, and a large share of every farm's land grew oats and hay to feed them. A tractor needs no feed when it is idle, which is a big part of its appeal.

Steam traction engines worked in Victorian fields, but they were heavy and mostly used for threshing and ploughing by contractors. The first successful light petrol tractor in Britain was the Ivel Agricultural Motor of 1902, built by Dan Albone, a bicycle maker in Biggleswade, Bedfordshire. It had three wheels, weighed far less than a steam engine, and could plough, haul and drive belt-powered machinery. Albone applied for his patent in February 1902, and about 500 were built before the company went into receivership in 1920. One is in the Science Museum collection. Albone himself died in 1906.

The big push came in the First World War. German submarines threatened imports, and the Ministry of Munitions ordered Fordson tractors from Henry Ford's firm in 1917, usually reported as 6,000 (some sources say 5,000 or 7,000), to plough up more grassland. The first was shipped in October 1917. The Fordson was small, light and cheap, and it made farmers see what tractors could do.

Change was still gradual. Horses remained common through the 1930s. After the Second World War the Ferguson TE20 and its three-point linkage helped make tractors the normal source of farm power. Local histories put the point at which tractors outnumbered horses between the late 1940s and the mid-1950s, and say the working farm horse had all but gone by about 1960. No single national series was found, so give the crossover as "around the early 1950s" rather than a year.

Sources: University of Nottingham (1951) agricultural economics study, ageconsearch.umn.edu record 267008 (horse numbers 1910 and 1939); Grasby parish history, Lincolnshire County Council (tractors overtaking horses); Science Museum Group, people record for Daniel Albone and the Ivel Agricultural Tractor object; Grace's Guide, Ivel; Biggleswade History Society, Dan Albone; The Henry Ford research guide, Fordson tractors manufactured for Britain during WWI; Farm Collector, early history of Fordson tractors. The crossover year is a judgement across local sources (flagged).

## Harry Ferguson and the three-point linkage
Kind: person
About: harry-ferguson, ferguson-te20, three-point-linkage, ferguson-ford-handshake, david-brown
Harry Ferguson was born on 4 November 1884 on a farm at Growell, near Dromore, County Down, and died on 25 October 1960. He started as a motor mechanic, built and flew his own aeroplane (31 December 1909, at Hillsborough Park), and then turned to tractors.

His great idea was the three-point linkage. Earlier, a plough was simply dragged behind a tractor on a chain. If it hit a rock or heavy ground, the tractor could rear up and tip over backwards. Ferguson's system joined the implement to the tractor by two lower arms and a single upper arm, so the two became one rigid unit. Hydraulics lifted and lowered the implement. The upper arm sensed the pull on the implement, and the hydraulics raised it a little in hard ground. This "draft control" also pushed some of the implement's weight onto the tractor's rear wheels, giving better grip and making rearing-up much less likely.

Ferguson's master patent was filed in Belfast on 12 February 1925, with a hydraulic version in 1926. The Ferguson Family Museum's chronology gives 1928 for the linkage patent, so sources differ. He built a tractor around the idea with David Brown of Huddersfield, made from 1936. He then showed his system to Henry Ford, and the two shook hands on a deal at Dearborn, usually dated October 1938 (the Ferguson Museum says 1939). Ford produced the Ford-Ferguson 9N in the United States from 1939. The partnership ended in 1947, after Henry Ford's grandson took over. Ferguson went on to build the TE20 at the Standard Motor Company's Banner Lane works in Coventry, from 1946, nicknamed the "Little Grey Fergie". The Ferguson Family Museum counts 517,651 TE20s, including variants, by 1956.

Ferguson sued Ford in the United States for using his ideas; the case began in 1944 and settled in April 1952 for just over $9 million. His company merged with Massey-Harris in August 1953. The three-point linkage, standardised in dimension categories, is still found on almost every tractor today, which is why one maker's plough can be attached to another maker's tractor.

Sources: Ferguson Family Museum, chronology (ferguson-museum.co.uk) and TE20 page; Grace's Guide, Ferguson TE20; Wikipedia, Harry Ferguson (dates, cross-checked against the museum). The linkage mechanics are general engineering knowledge. The handshake and patent dates are disputed between sources, as stated.

## Tractor safety: overturning, roll-over protective structures and safety cabs
Kind: reference
About: swedish-rops-law, rops-regulations
Tractors have a high centre of gravity, work on slopes and ditches, and tow heavy loads. That makes them prone to overturning, and early tractors had no protection at all. Overturning, either sideways on a slope or backwards when the front lifts, is a well-known cause of fatal tractor accidents.

There are two typical ways to overturn. A tractor can tip backwards if a towed load or a stuck implement pulls the front up. The three-point linkage and draft control reduced this. It can also roll sideways on a bank or when turning with a raised load. A driver without protection can then be crushed under the machine.

The answer is a roll-over protective structure, known as a ROPS: a strong frame, or a cab built on one, which holds its shape and leaves a survival space around the driver. It works together with a seat belt that keeps the driver inside it. The structure is tested by pushing, loading and, in some tests, dropping a weight onto it. Sweden led: its regulations required protective structures on new tractors from 1 July 1959, extended in 1965 to tractors driven by employees. A Swedish study found rollover deaths fell from 12 to 0.2 per 100,000 tractors between 1957–1964 and 1986–1990, as the share of tractors with a structure rose from 6 per cent to 93 per cent.

In Britain, makers developed safety frames and cabs in the 1960s, and the law followed with regulations in 1967. The Agriculture (Tractor Cabs) Regulations 1974 (SI 1974/2034) replaced them and came into operation on 5 December 1974. Workers driving tractors in agriculture must use an approved safety cab, except where that is impracticable (for example in orchards or hop-gardens). Tractors first sold before 1 September 1970 were exempt from some requirements until 1 September 1977. Cabs on tractors new from 1 June 1976 had to meet a 90 dBA noise limit, shown by a "Q" mark. Approved cabs carry a crown-in-triangle mark.

Sources: legislation.gov.uk, The Agriculture (Tractor Cabs) Regulations 1974 (SI 1974/2034), regulations 1 to 4 and explanatory note; ILO Encyclopaedia of Occupational Health and Safety, "Rollover"; Thorson and Springfeldt, Läkartidningen 1999 (1 July 1959); Springfeldt, Thorson and Lee (1998), Journal of Agricultural Safety and Health, as summarised in search results. No British overturn-death trend is cited because none was verified.

## Guidance by buried wire: how a tractor can follow a cable
Kind: reference
About: buried-wire-guidance
A buried-wire guidance system turns a basic fact of physics into a steering signal. When an alternating current flows along a wire, it creates a magnetic field that circles the wire and keeps changing direction. The field is strongest close to the wire and weakens rapidly with distance.

The vehicle carries two pick-up coils, one on each side of the front, or a pair of coils side by side. A changing magnetic field passing through a coil induces a small alternating voltage in it, which is the principle of electromagnetic induction. The nearer coil lies in a stronger field, so it produces a bigger voltage than the further one.

If the vehicle is centred over the wire, the two voltages are equal and their difference is zero: no steering is needed. If it drifts to the left, the left-hand coil gives the bigger signal. The difference between the two, called the error signal, tells the controller which way it has drifted and by how much. The controller turns that error into a steering command through a motor or hydraulic ram. The vehicle moves back towards the wire, the difference shrinks, and it settles into following the line. This is simple feedback control, like keeping a bicycle upright.

The current is usually set at a particular frequency, so that the vehicle's electronics can ignore other electrical noise. Extra tones can be sent along the same wire to give commands, such as "lift the plough" at the field end. The same idea is used by some factory and warehouse trucks that follow wires in the floor.

The drawback is cost and inflexibility: you have to bury and power a cable in every field. That is a main reason this approach faded once satellite positioning arrived. Future Farming notes that only a couple of such systems were sold, both to fruit growers.

Sources: the physics and control explanation is general textbook knowledge (electromagnetic induction, feedback control). Future Farming, "The invention and adoption of autosteer technology", for a Reading University tractor in 1958 that followed wires buried below ground, and for the few buried-wire systems sold to fruit growers.

## Machine vision for weeding and spraying
Kind: reference
About: excess-green-index, blue-river-acquisition
A camera can steer a farm machine, or decide where to treat, because crops and soil look different. A plant is green; soil is brown or grey.

The first job is to find the plants. A simple, widely used method is the excess green index, tested in 1995 by Woebbecke and colleagues. For each pixel, it takes the amounts of red, green and blue light and computes about twice the green minus the red and the blue. Plants score high and soil scores low, even as the light changes in brightness. A threshold then separates the two. Near-infrared cameras can also help, because leaves reflect strongly in that band.

The second job is finding rows. Seeds are drilled in straight lines, so a computer can fit a line through the plant pixels, for example using a Hough transform. The line is the centre of the row, and the difference between that line and where the machine is pointing becomes the steering error. Steerage hoes made this way can run blades within a couple of centimetres of the crop.

The third is telling weeds from crop. Plants in a row are expected, those between rows are weeds, which is the easy case. Within the row it is harder. Older systems measured colour, leaf shape and size, and position. Since the 2010s, deep learning has taken over: neural networks trained on many labelled photographs recognise species directly, even when leaves overlap.

The result can drive a mechanical hoe, a laser or a precision sprayer that switches individual nozzles on only over weeds. See & Spray, developed by Blue River Technology and bought by John Deere in 2017 for $305 million, sprays only the plants it classes as weeds. Blue River's claimed herbicide savings vary by source, from a tenfold cut to 90 per cent. The hard parts are changing light, shadow, dirt, wind and the huge variety of weeds.

Sources: Woebbecke, Meyer, Von Bargen and Mortensen (1995), "Color indices for weed identification under various soil, residue, and lighting conditions", Transactions of the ASAE 38(1), 259–269 (abstract read; the index 2g−r−b was among the best performers); Slaughter, Giles and Downey (2008), "Autonomous robotic weed control systems: A review", Computers and Electronics in Agriculture 61(1), 63–78; TechCrunch, 6 September 2017, and AgFunder News on Deere's purchase of Blue River. Row-finding by line fitting is general image-processing knowledge.

## Satellite guidance: GPS, RTK and auto-steer
Kind: reference
About: gps-full-operation, gps-selective-availability-off, autotrac-launch
The Global Positioning System (GPS) is a network of satellites run by the United States. Each satellite broadcasts its position and a precise time signal. A receiver measures how long signals from at least four satellites took to arrive and works out its own position. The system was declared fully operational in 1995. Until May 2000 the US deliberately degraded the civilian signal, called selective availability; gps.gov gives 1 May 2000 as the date it was switched off. Without that, an ordinary receiver is accurate to a few metres, which is not enough to drive between crop rows. GPS now sits alongside other systems (Russia's GLONASS, Europe's Galileo and China's BeiDou), so the general term is GNSS.

To get centimetre accuracy, farms use real-time kinematic positioning, or RTK. The receiver does not just use the coded signal. It also compares the phase of the carrier wave itself, which is far finer. The problem is that there are errors, such as delay through the atmosphere. A fixed base station at a known point, or a network of them, measures those errors and sends corrections to the tractor by radio or mobile data. The tractor's receiver then fixes its position to about 2 centimetres.

Auto-steer connects the receiver to the steering. The driver sets a straight or curved line (an "AB line") for the first pass, and the system steers each later pass parallel to it, leaving the driver to watch the implement and turn at the headland. Sources differ on who was first. NASA Spinoff says AutoFarm, a subsidiary of Novariant, released RTK AutoSteer in 1999 in California, Arizona and Australia. John Deere says its first GPS guidance system, AutoTrac, came in 2002 with about 30 centimetre accuracy, and RTK accuracy of about 2.5 centimetres followed in 2004.

Centimetre-level repeatability allows controlled traffic farming, where all machines use the same permanent wheel lanes year after year. Soil in the lanes is compacted and the soil in the rest of the field stays loose, which can improve crop growth and drainage.

Sources: GPS.gov, "Selective Availability"; MIT OpenCourseWare lecture notes on GPS history (1995 full operational capability; no day confirmed from an official page); NASA Spinoff, AutoFarm / Novariant; Farming News and Agriland on John Deere's 25 years of automated guidance; Future Farming, "The invention and adoption of autosteer technology". How RTK and controlled traffic work is general engineering knowledge; the compaction benefit is a widely stated claim, not quantified here.

## Autonomous farm robots today
Kind: reference
About: hands-free-hectare, deere-autonomous-8r
In 2017 a team at Harper Adams University in Shropshire, working with the company Precision Decisions, ran the Hands Free Hectare project. The aim was to grow a crop of spring barley without a person setting foot in the field. They used small converted tractors and a converted combine harvester, along with drones to survey the crop. The machines drove themselves using satellite positioning. The first crop was harvested in September 2017, billed as a world first, with a reported yield of 4.5 tonnes per hectare against a forecast of 5. It was not perfectly hands-off: the team kept the tractor away from the combine as a precaution rather than adapt it for unloading alongside. The project repeated the experiment with winter wheat in 2018.

In January 2022 John Deere unveiled a fully autonomous 8R tractor at CES. It pulls a tillage tool, uses six pairs of stereo cameras for 360-degree obstacle detection, and stops and alerts the farmer if unsure. The farmer sets it up through a phone app and can leave the field. Specialist firms also build small field robots, but this note does not describe individual products because none were checked.

Why bother? Small machines compact the soil less than a big tractor, they can work around the clock, and they remove the need for a driver in repetitive work. The difficulties are safety around people and animals, reliable navigation, cost, and the need for rules on driverless machines on public land. Most commercial robots still work in a limited setting, such as a single crop, a tunnel or a prepared field. Fully driverless general-purpose tractors remain uncommon.

Sources: UKRI / Innovate UK, "Hands-free crop harvested by robots in world-first trial"; FarmingUK, Hands Free Hectare articles; GOV.UK news story on the project; Electrek and Profi (4 January 2022) on John Deere's autonomous 8R at CES 2022; Lowenberg-DeBoer, Huang, Grigoriadis and Blackmore, "Economics of robots and automation in field crop production", Precision Agriculture (online 2019, volume dated 2020); Fountas et al. ## Why grain is dried and how dryers work
Kind: reference
Freshly cut grain is alive. If it is stored damp, the grain and the moulds and insects living on it respire, giving off heat and moisture, and the heap can warm up and rot. Some moulds also produce toxins. Drying removes water so that this cannot happen.

Moisture content is the share of a sample's weight that is water. AHDB says food-safety risk in cereals rises above 14.5 per cent moisture, storing below 13 per cent greatly limits moisture uptake and stops mites breeding, and grain at 18 per cent or more should be dried immediately. How long grain keeps safely depends on its temperature, so colder is safer. For long, stable storage, oilseed rape should be dried to 7.5 to 8 per cent and not below 6 per cent. Buyers also set their own delivery limits.

All dryers work the same way. Air is blown through the grain, takes up moisture and carries it away. Warmer air holds more water, so heating it makes drying faster. The dryer designs differ in how the grain meets the air:
- On-floor and bin drying: grain sits in a deep heap or bin and air is forced up through it, from a floor of perforated ducts. It is slow, cheap, and can use only slightly warmed air.
- Batch dryers: a fixed amount is dried, then emptied.
- Continuous-flow dryers: grain trickles down through hot-air columns, while fans blow air across. They are used for large throughputs.

Too much heat can damage grain quality and, for seed, germination, so drying temperatures are kept down for those uses. Grain is also cooled afterwards. Drying uses a lot of fuel, which is why a dry harvest is welcome.

Sources: AHDB, "Grain storage moisture targets for cereals and oilseed rape" (ahdb.org.uk knowledge library; all moisture figures). The explanation of respiration and the dryer types is general agricultural engineering knowledge; no temperature limits were verified, so none are given.

## Sprayers and spray drift
Kind: reference
A boom sprayer is a tractor-mounted or self-propelled machine carrying a wide horizontal pipe, often 24 to 36 metres long, with a nozzle every half metre. It pumps a mix of water and pesticide or fertiliser out through the nozzles onto the crop.

Drift is spray that is carried away from its target by the wind. It matters because pesticides can harm neighbouring crops, hedges, ponds, wildlife and people. The main factors are droplet size, boom height, wind and travel speed.

Droplet size matters most. Droplets under about 100 micrometres, a tenth of a millimetre, are widely treated as highly driftable, and Danish and other studies link the share of droplets below 100 micrometres to measured drift. Large droplets fall fast and land close to the nozzle, but may bounce off a waxy leaf. The way to reduce drift is to use a coarser spray from low-drift nozzles. The best known are air-induction (venturi) nozzles, which mix air into the liquid to make big, air-filled droplets. A North Dakota State University guide reports about a 90 per cent drift reduction compared with a standard flat-fan nozzle.

Boom height is the second factor. Spray from a boom that is high above the crop spends longer in the air, giving the wind more time to carry it off, so the boom should be as low as the nozzles allow and kept steady. Sprayer maker and label height recommendations vary by nozzle, so no single figure is given here.

Operators stop spraying in strong wind and leave unsprayed buffer strips beside watercourses. In Britain the HSE explains that a Local Environment Risk Assessment for Pesticides (LERAP) must be carried out and recorded if you want to reduce an aquatic buffer zone, and low-drift nozzles can earn a smaller buffer. Boom sprayers wider than 3 metres need a National Sprayer Testing Scheme test every three years (gov.wales guidance; rules may have changed since).

Sources: HSE, "Local environment risk assessment for pesticides (LERAP)" and "LERAP horizontal boom sprayers" (hse.gov.uk/pesticides); gov.wales, "Equipment inspection: pesticide users" (NSTS test intervals); NDSU Extension AE1246 (droplets under 100 micrometres; venturi nozzles); BCPC Weeds Conference 1999, Application Technology (low-drift star ratings); Danish EPA, "Dry deposition and spray drift of pesticides to nearby water bodies" (2003).
