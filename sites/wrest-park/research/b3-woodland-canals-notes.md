## Bowling Green House
Kind: site
About: bowling-green-house, bowling-urns, amphitheatre
Bowling Green House is a single-storey garden pavilion on the west side of the old garden, just south of the American Garden, beside the Duke of Kent's bowling green. Historic England (list entry 1158672, Grade II*) dates it to about 1735 and credits Batty Langley, working for Henry Grey, Duke of Kent. The dating is not settled. Parks & Gardens UK and the Heritage Fund say Langley remodelled an earlier building in 1735, and Parks & Gardens adds a latest date of 1734. Bedfordshire Archives says it "incorporates an earlier building". English Heritage's 2015 phased plan colours the main walls 1720–1721 and only the six front columns 1735 (read from the plan's colour key). The listing says the building "probably incorporates an early-eighteenth-century deer house"; no source found says more about it.

What you see: the east front, facing the green, is colour-washed render with a portico of six painted timber Tuscan columns. The west side is an arcade of arches on pale yellow brick piers (Flemish bond). There are small square wings north and south, and a hipped tiled roof. Inside are plaster panels and an ornate marble chimney-piece. The balustrade above the columns once carried six life-size statues; by 1831 they had been replaced by urns, and those were replaced again in the late nineteenth or early twentieth century by replica urns, probably by Barwell and Co. of the Eagle Foundry.

The two lead urns on stone pedestals, about 2 metres tall, stand about 5 metres south-east and north-east of the house. The listing dates them to about 1720 and says they are probably by John Van Nost the Younger, copies of carved urns by Edward Pearce and Cibber. One shows Venus in a sea chariot; the other shows a procession of nymphs, fauns and Bacchus. They appear in John Rocque's 1735 drawing and have stayed in place since. The listing's reasons for Grade II* include the rarity of early-eighteenth-century bowling pavilions.

Bowls and the family: Bedfordshire Archives says Anthony, 11th Earl of Kent, the Duke's father, died on a bowling green in Tonbridge. Wikipedia dates his death 19 August 1702 (single source); only Bedfordshire Archives mentions the bowling green.

The lake and the amphitheatre: the Leg o' Mutton Lake lies next to the house. A Geograph caption says the lake once had a semicircular extension on its south side, which gave it its name, and that the old shape can just be made out in the grass. That is one photographer's caption, so treat it as unconfirmed. The Historic England listing for the ha-ha in the Old Park (1402869) says that in 1729 work resumed on the garden, adding an amphitheatre north of the bowling green and a serpentine canal. No source found describes the amphitheatre's form. English Heritage's leaflet marks "Leg O'Mutton Lake and Amphitheatre" as one stop, number 15, next to Bowling Green House (14).

The listing calls the Duke "12th Duke of Kent"; he was the 1st Duke and 12th Earl.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1158672 (via British Listed Buildings); Historic England list entry 1402869 (ha-ha in the Old Park); Bedfordshire Archives, Buildings at Wrest Park; Bedfordshire Archives, Monuments and Ornaments at Wrest Park; Parks & Gardens UK, Wrest Park; Heritage Fund, "Grand garden revival at Grade I registered Wrest Park" (2011); English Heritage, Wrest Park phased plans (February 2015); English Heritage orientation leaflet (2024); Geograph photo 871840 (caption); Wikipedia, Anthony Grey, 11th Earl of Kent.

## The West Half House
Kind: site
About: west-half-house, great-garden
The West Half House is a small garden shelter in a clearing in the woodland west of the Long Water. Historic England (list entry 1158741, Grade II) dates it to about 1726 and says it was built for Henry Grey, Duke of Kent. Bedfordshire Archives also says about 1726, and calls the pair of Half Houses "essentially, glorified shelters".

It has a half-round (exedral) plan under a half-conical roof covered in lead. The south-facing front is white brick, with a central segmental arched opening between pilaster strips. The back is red brick and blind, pierced only by small round ventilation holes. One step leads in through the arch to a rendered interior with a semicircular wooden seat and a stone floor with decorative black tiles. The listing says it is little altered, though its wooden pediment was replaced in the twentieth century. No architect is recorded.

Where it fits: 1726 is when the avenues and "squares" beside the Long Water were largely complete (Historic England, list entry 1158972, on the Brown monument). English Heritage calls the whole thing "a rare example in England of a formal woodland garden in the French style", with woodland avenues decorated with statues and garden buildings. English Heritage's leaflet marks "Duchess's Square and West Half House" as one place (number 17) and "Duke's Square" (22) elsewhere in the wood. A garden blog describes the woodland as bosquets and geometric clearings, including the Duke's and Duchess's squares.

Purpose: shelters and seats. A garden blog describes the Half Houses as "concealed in the shrubbery and containing benches to rest on". The plan is an exedra, a half-round recess.

Just south is the statue of the lady reading, covered in its own section ("The statue of Jemima"). Further south is the Hutton Monument, a brick wall with an inset plaque about 1730 for the Duke's friend Thomas Hutton, stated by Bedfordshire Archives to be 175 yards south of this half house.

The East Half House is its twin, about 350 metres away across the Long Water (distance measured from OpenStreetMap positions). The two differ: the East has a semicircular arch under a triangular pediment, and niches that once held statues; the West has a segmental arch and pilaster strips.
Sources: Historic England list entry 1158741 (West Half House); list entry 1138342 (East Half House); list entry 1158972 (Monument to Capability Brown); Bedfordshire Archives, Buildings at Wrest Park; Bedfordshire Archives, Monuments and Ornaments at Wrest Park; English Heritage, History of Wrest Park; English Heritage orientation leaflet (2024); The Garden History blog, "Eternal Wrest" (2014).

## The statue of Jemima (the lady reading)
Kind: site
About: jemima-statue, john-cheere, jemima
South of the West Half House, in the clearing the leaflet calls Duchess's Square, sits a lead statue of a woman reading. Historic England (list entry 1113805, Grade II*) says she stands about 2.5 metres high on a Portland stone pedestal, and calls her "a rare example of a mid-eighteenth-century lead sculpture depicting a seated female figure". It dates the statue to about 1748 and says it is probably by John Cheere (1709–1787). Bedfordshire Archives gives the same pose: a woman in classical dress, seated, resting on an urn, with an open book in her left hand, standing 25 yards south of the West Half House.

Who is she? The sources disagree. Historic England says she is believed to be Jemima, Marchioness Grey, and links the statue to her influence on the garden. Bedfordshire Archives says she is thought to be Jemima, first wife of the Duke of Kent, who died in 1728. The Duke's first wife was Jemima Crew (died 2 July 1728), and his granddaughter was also Jemima, which makes the confusion easy. The statue is not documented as a portrait of either. If asked, give both. Do not assert one.

Jemima, Marchioness Grey (born 9 October 1722 at Copenhagen according to Bedfordshire Archives; The Peerage says 1723) was the Duke's granddaughter. She married Philip Yorke, the Lord Chancellor's son, on 22 May 1740, when she was 17 by the Bedfordshire Archives date. The Duke died on 5 June 1740, having outlived all his sons, and she inherited Wrest and the Marquess Grey title. Some summaries say she inherited on condition that she marry Philip; Bedfordshire Archives does not mention a condition, so treat that as unconfirmed. Philip became 2nd Earl of Hardwicke on 6 March 1764. A History Today piece describes the circle at Wrest: relatives and friends, days in the gardens and evenings trading poems.

John Cheere (1709–1787) was the younger brother of the sculptor Sir Henry Cheere and ran a business making statues, many of them in lead. Wikipedia credits him with the Medici lions at Stowe and, from 1751 to 1766, figures for the grottoes and gardens at Stourhead. Historic England's listing says he lived 1709–1789; Wikipedia says 1787. At Wrest he is thought to have made King William III by the Long Water and probably this statue. Bedfordshire Archives does not name a sculptor for the lady reading, and attributes the William III statue to John Nost.

Sources: Historic England list entry 1113805; Bedfordshire Archives, Statues at Wrest Park; Bedfordshire Archives, Jemima Marchioness Grey; Wikipedia, Henry Grey, 1st Duke of Kent; Wikipedia, John Cheere; The Peerage; History Today, "On the Home Front"; English Heritage orientation leaflet (2024).

## The East Half House
Kind: site
About: east-half-house, great-garden
The East Half House is the twin of the West Half House, in the woodland east of the Long Water, near the Pavilion end. Historic England (list entry 1138342, Grade II) describes it as "a garden house or alcove seat" for Henry Grey, Duke of Kent, dating from 1726, and says the architect is unknown. Bedfordshire Archives says about 1726 and groups it with the West Half House as "glorified shelters" in the wooded areas east and west of the central canal.

It is brick with a lead-covered half-conical roof. The plan is square at the front and apsidal (rounded) at the rear. The front is three bays wide, with a central semicircular archway under a triangular pediment, and flanking niches which once held statues. The interior is plain apart from a semicircular wooden seat. The pair were built with small differences, and Wikipedia says they sit in small clearings on either side of the Long Water.

Who designed things for the Duke: English Heritage says the Duke employed Hawksmoor, Archer, Langley and William Kent. Historic England's entry on the Brown monument also names Juvarra, Leoni and Gibbs among the architects from whom garden buildings were commissioned in the 1720s, and the Duke's garden buildings were not all built (general knowledge; unconfirmed). The Half Houses are not credited to any of them.

The Duke. Henry Grey (1671–1740) succeeded his father as 12th Earl of Kent in 1702 and was Lord Chamberlain from 1704 to 1710. He became Marquess of Kent in 1706, the year English Heritage gives for the start of his Great Garden, and Duke of Kent in 1710, in exchange for giving up the Chamberlain's post. He was made a Knight of the Garter in 1712. He outlived all his sons. English Heritage thinks the Pavilion's wall paintings celebrate the dukedom.

Walking: the Archer Pavilion is about 200 metres west of here (measured from OpenStreetMap positions), and the canal that curves round the garden's east side is close by. The 2011 restoration reinstated about three miles of woodland paths, which had been grassed over during the Second World War (Heritage Fund).

In 1726 the avenues and squares were largely complete. The other garden buildings the Duke left are the Pavilion (1709–1711), the Bowling Green House (about 1735), the Hutton Monument (about 1730) and the statue of William III (1730s).
Sources: Historic England list entry 1138342 (East Half House); list entry 1158972 (Monument to Capability Brown); Bedfordshire Archives, Buildings at Wrest Park; Wikipedia, Wrest Park; Wikipedia, Henry Grey, 1st Duke of Kent; English Heritage, History of Wrest Park; Heritage Fund (2011); Bedfordshire Archives, Monuments and Ornaments.

## Oldpark Water and the western canal
Kind: site
About: boundary-canals, serpentine-canal
Oldpark Water (also written Old Park Water) is the western stretch of the chain of canals that encircle the old garden, usually called "the river". It is part of the boundary canals: an Albion Archaeology report for English Heritage (2007) says "the encircling canals including Old Park Water and the Serpentine achieved their present form during the landscaping works carried out by Brown 1758–1760". It adds that Brown's designs were mostly "modifications in shape to earlier water bodies constructed from the period 1702–1720". English Heritage says the Duke's canals enclose the woodland garden on three sides.

It is presumably named for the Old Park, which lies to its west. Historic England (list entry 1402869) calls the Old Park the oldest parkland at Wrest, parts probably dating from the fourteenth century. An 18th-century sandstone ha-ha about 400 metres long (Grade II, listed 2012) runs across the Old Park from the garden boundary to a bridleway on the west.

Not all the curves are Brown's. The same listing says that in 1729 the Duke's garden works included a serpentine canal. Brown (1758–1760) joined the waters together and made the straight perimeter canals meander. Jemima valued her grandfather's garden, so his changes were limited.

How it works (Albion 2007, quoting earlier surveys): the canals are man-made and rely on weirs and retaining walls. The banks are natural clay, prone to erosion if levels fluctuate, and repairs still involve "puddling" the clay to stop leaks. Old Park Weir links Old Park Water with the Serpentine on the western edge of the park. The two waters were completed about 1760, and the difference in their levels needed a bank of earth "which planting and shrubs must conceal" (a Bedfordshire archives document, crt 190/45, quoted by Albion). A sluice was added between 1901 and 1924. The original spring in the Bath Ground no longer flows, so the present pumped circulation, adapted in 1972, keeps the water moving. A farm ditch at the north-west corner brings silt, so the head of Old Park Water silts up. A Historic England photograph records a dredger clearing Old Park Water, probably in 2009 (found by search only).

Views: the quiet tree-lined water makes a good still picture. The Bowling Green House stands on its east bank to the north, and the Serpentine carries on southward.

Sources disagree on when the garden began: Albion says "from the 1650s onwards"; Historic England's register gives the earlier formal landscape as 1671–1702, with the Long Water in the early 1680s.
Sources: Albion Archaeology, Old Park Weir, Wrest Park: Archaeological Evaluation (2007/109); Historic England list entry 1402869 (ha-ha in the Old Park); Historic England list entry 1158972; English Heritage, History of Wrest Park; Parks & Gardens UK, Wrest Park; Historic England register entry 1000113 (via Parks & Gardens UK).

## The Duke of Kent's Great Garden (1706–1740)
Kind: site
About: great-garden, boundary-canals
Between 1706 and the 1720s Henry Grey, Duke of Kent, laid out what English Heritage calls "a massive formal woodland garden, enclosed on three sides by canals". It is "a rare example in England of a formal woodland garden in the French style". It was built round his family's earlier work: English Heritage says the Long Water, made in the early 1680s, was the work of his grandmother Amabel Benn, his father Anthony and his mother Mary.

What he made: woodland flanking the Long Water, cut by straight avenues and open "squares", decorated with statues and garden buildings; formal canals enclosing it, with more at right angles to the Long Water; and, at the far end, Thomas Archer's Pavilion (1709–1711). The avenues and squares were largely complete by 1726, and the canals were altered in the 1720s. In 1729 more work added a serpentine canal and an amphitheatre north of the bowling green. John Rocque recorded the garden and its buildings in plans of 1735 and 1737.

The designers: English Heritage names Hawksmoor, Archer, Langley and William Kent. Historic England adds Juvarra, Leoni and Gibbs among the architects from whom garden buildings were commissioned in the 1720s; not all of the designs were built (unconfirmed). Surviving buildings: the Pavilion (1709–1711), the Half Houses (about 1726), the Hutton Monument (about 1730), the Bowling Green House (about 1735) and the statue of William III (1730s).

After him: Jemima, Marchioness Grey, inherited in 1740 and "hardly altered the core of the garden". Lancelot Brown worked on the periphery from about 1758 to 1760 to soften the perimeter canals. The register lists the Great Garden among Wrest's main phases (early eighteenth century and early nineteenth century, on a late-seventeenth-century base). Most park and avenue trees were felled between the 1920s and 1940s. The woodland paths were grassed over in the Second World War and about three miles were reinstated in the 2011 restoration.

Walking it: paths run each side of the Long Water to the Pavilion. The Half Houses give places to rest. The canals can be followed round the west (Oldpark Water) and east sides.

The register, as quoted by Parks & Gardens UK, says the garden's iconography relates to the Duke's Whiggish beliefs and his regard for William III. Albion Archaeology's date for the first formal garden here is "from the 1650s", earlier than the register's 1671.
Sources: English Heritage, History of Wrest Park; Parks & Gardens UK, Wrest Park (quoting Historic England's register entry 1000113); Historic England list entry 1158972; Historic England list entry 1402869; Heritage Fund (2011).

## The Duke of Kent and the War of the Spanish Succession
Kind: person
About: duke-of-kent, spanish-succession, pavilion
Henry Grey (1671–1740) lived through the War of the Spanish Succession (March 1701 to September 1714) as a member of the royal household. He succeeded as Earl of Kent in 1702, was Lord Chamberlain and a Privy Councillor from 1704 to 1710, became Marquess of Kent in 1706 and Duke of Kent in 1710. Wikipedia says he got the dukedom for giving up the Chamberlain's post. It calls him "the politically expedient candidate", seen as lacking talent and ambition. He was made a Knight of the Garter in 1712 and was Lord Lieutenant of Bedfordshire from 1711 to 1740.

The war: when Charles II of Spain, the last Spanish Habsburg, died childless in November 1700, Philip of Anjou, grandson of Louis XIV, became Philip V. In September 1701 the Habsburg Emperor Leopold I, the Dutch Republic and Britain renewed the Grand Alliance. The Habsburg claimant was Leopold's son, Archduke Charles. In 1711 Charles became Holy Roman Emperor, which led the British government to seek peace. The Treaty of Utrecht followed in 1713 and the Treaties of Rastatt and Baden in 1714. Britain kept Gibraltar and Menorca.

Genuine links, and their limits. First, timing: the Pavilion was begun in 1709 and finished in 1711, and the dukedom came in 1710, during the war, in the year before Charles became Emperor. English Heritage thinks the Pavilion's wall paintings celebrate the dukedom. Second, politics: the register says the garden's iconography reflects the Duke's Whiggish beliefs and regard for William III, whose 1689 Grand Alliance against France was renewed in 1701. I found no source showing the Duke fought, held a command, or built anything in direct response to a battle. His posts were in the royal household. Any story linking the Pavilion to a particular victory is unsupported.

The Duke's family: he married Jemima Crew in 1694 (she died on 2 July 1728), and Lady Sophia Bentinck on 24 March 1729. Of his sons, Henry died in 1717, Anthony, Earl of Harold, in 1723 and George in 1733; he died on 5 June 1740. Shortly before he died he became Marquess Grey, with the title passing to his granddaughter Jemima.
Sources: Wikipedia, Henry Grey, 1st Duke of Kent; Wikipedia, War of the Spanish Succession; English Heritage, History of Wrest Park; Historic England register entry 1000113 (via Parks & Gardens UK); Bedfordshire Archives, Buildings at Wrest Park.

## Batty Langley
Kind: person
About: batty-langley, bowling-green-house
Batty Langley (baptised 14 September 1696, died 3 March 1751) was a garden designer and writer of pattern books. He was born at Twickenham, the son of a gardener, Daniel Langley, and was named after David Batty, one of his father's patrons. His one confirmed commission at Wrest is Bowling Green House, one of his few known commissions overall (Wikipedia); Bedfordshire Archives also credits him with a building on the site of the later Orangery.

Pattern books were illustrated guides that let builders and craftsmen copy correct designs and proportions without an architect. Langley's included Practical Geometry (1726), New Principles of Gardening (1728), A Sure Guide to Builders (1729), Ancient Masonry (1733–1736), The Builder's Complete Assistant (1738) and The Builder's Jewel (1741). Their geometric rules and details spread across Georgian Britain and America: George Washington drew on them for Mount Vernon.

He is best known for trying to "improve" Gothic architecture by giving it classical rules and proportions (Ancient Architecture, Restored, and Improved, 1742, reissued in 1747 as Gothic Architecture, improved by Rules and Proportions). Horace Walpole, whose Strawberry Hill was a major Gothic Revival work, criticised this harshly. Langley named four sons Hiram, Euclid, Vitruvius and Archimedes, wrote a column for the Grub Street Journal (1734–1735) as "Hiram", is thought to have been a Freemason, and was imprisoned for debt in Newgate.

At Wrest he worked for the Duke of Kent in the 1730s. Parks & Gardens UK and the Heritage Fund say he remodelled the Bowling Green House in 1735 (Parks & Gardens gives a latest date of 1734); the Historic England listing says about 1735. A garden blog says he and Thomas Wright worked on the grounds in a more informal style between the Duke's early work and Rocque's 1737 survey; this is not confirmed elsewhere.

The Duke's other designers: English Heritage names Hawksmoor, Archer, Langley and William Kent.
Sources: Wikipedia, Batty Langley; Historic England list entry 1158672; Bedfordshire Archives, Buildings at Wrest Park; Parks & Gardens UK, Wrest Park; Heritage Fund (2011); The Garden History blog (2014); English Heritage, History of Wrest Park.

## John Cheere
Kind: person
About: john-cheere, jemima-statue, william-statue
John Cheere (1709–1787) was a London sculptor who made garden statues; the Wrest ones credited to him are lead. He was the younger brother of the sculptor Sir Henry Cheere, and began in John Nost's yard, later working with his brother when Henry set up on his own. Wikipedia credits him with the Medici lions at Stowe, figures for the grottoes and gardens at Stourhead (1751–1766) and four for the Pantheon there (1766). His dates are given as 1709–1789 in the Historic England listing for the statue at Wrest; the dates file uses Wikipedia's 1709–1787.

At Wrest the lady reading (about 1748) is "probably" by him, according to Historic England (list entry 1113805), which calls it a rare mid-eighteenth-century lead figure of a seated woman, about 2.5 metres high on a Portland stone pedestal, and the lead statue of King William III by the Long Water is "thought to be" by him (list entry 1158878, 1730s). Bedfordshire Archives gives neither to Cheere: it credits the William III statue to John Nost or his son and names no sculptor for the lady reading. Wikipedia also pictures a statue of Harlequin by him at Wrest Park (not found in any Wrest listing).

The lady reading is the only lead statue besides the Main Walk groups that Bedfordshire Archives describes: it calls those four groups "painted lead and thought to be about 1730", on bases that look nineteenth-century. Lead was a practical garden material because a figure could be cast from a mould, which is general background rather than a Wrest-specific fact.

English Heritage's leaflet says there are over 40 statues dotted around the grounds. When asked who made a Wrest statue, say "probably" or "thought to be" unless the source names the maker outright.
Sources: Wikipedia, John Cheere; Historic England list entries 1113805 and 1158878; Bedfordshire Archives, Statues at Wrest Park; English Heritage orientation leaflet (2024); general knowledge for lead casting.
