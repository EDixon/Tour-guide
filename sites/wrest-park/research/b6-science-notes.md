## The science years (east lawn)
Kind: site
About: niae, sun-insurance, ministry-purchase, niae-closure
From the east lawn you look north-east towards the modern buildings that make up the former research campus. For nearly sixty years, from 1948 until 2006, this side of Wrest Park was home to an agricultural engineering research institute: first the National Institute of Agricultural Engineering (NIAE), renamed Silsoe Research Institute in 1991. English Heritage's history says the estate "housed agricultural engineering research institutes from 1948 until 2006". The Historic England register entry adds that industrial farm buildings and offices were added to the east and north-east of the house in the later twentieth century, which is the part of the estate you are looking at. The old stable block (built 1834–1839 for the 2nd Earl de Grey) lies in front of them and is listed as Grade II; a search summary of its listing says it was used as offices.

Three uses of Wrest follow one another after the de Greys sold in 1917. In the Second World War the Sun Insurance Office moved its head office here from London. After the war the state bought the house and gardens. The institute then took the buildings on a lease. The institute closed in 2006 and English Heritage took over the house and began restoring the gardens.

The institute is best known for four things. It tested farm machinery for the government and manufacturers. It developed a driverless tractor steered by a buried wire. It built one of the first camera-guided field robots, demonstrated in 1996, whose guidance later went into commercial hoes. And it did applied physics: drying crops, spraying, and measuring wind on buildings. Each has its own note.

What is left of the institute? Geograph photographs taken in 2018 show buildings in this part of the park captioned "Industrial buildings in Wrest Park" and "Enterprise House", and a Geograph description says the site now has rentable office space. A search summary also says some research still goes on at Wrest Park behind the scenes, with former institute staff still working there (IAgrE). Former staff keep in touch through the Wrest Park Group, and the Institution of Agricultural Engineers has held celebration days at Wrest Park (25 November 2018 and 14 May 2022). Dates and sources disagree in small ways: see the notes on the institute's arrival (1947 or 1948), the Ministry purchase (1946 or 1949) and the closure (2006 or 2007).

Sources: English Heritage, History of Wrest Park; Parks & Gardens UK, Wrest Park (drawing on the Historic England register entry 1000113); Silsoe Research Institute history (silsoeresearch.org.uk); Museum of English Rural Life, Silsoe Research Institute archive catalogue (SR SRI); IAgrE event pages (ehniae2018, SRI2020); Geograph photographs 5819679 and 5819686; Historic England listing 1311475 (stable block, via search summary).

## The institute's history, 1924–2006
Kind: reference
About: niae, niae-origins, niae-closure
The institute began in Oxford. In 1924, as the Institute of Agricultural Engineering at the University of Oxford, it ran farm machinery trials and research into subsoiling, haymaking and crop drying. The Ministry of Agriculture set up an Agricultural Machinery Testing Committee the same year and asked it for testing. It was renamed the Institute for Research in Agricultural Engineering in 1932 and moved in 1934 from St Giles to larger premises in Parks Road.

The Second World War made it central to food production. From 1939 its staff advised the Ministry on tractor numbers, power needs, servicing, training and mechanising crops. In 1942 it became a branch of the Ministry and moved to temporary quarters at Askham Bryan, 4 miles west of York. It was renamed the National Institute of Agricultural Engineering, and twelve staff who made the move formed its core. In the early 1940s testing and training took priority over research, with training aimed at machinery instructors and members of the War Agricultural Executive Committees. The MERL archive keeps a staff magazine, "Furrow Press", from October 1944.

By 1947 the staff numbered 150 and a new home was found at Wrest Park. The institute's own history says the move took place between June 1947 and July 1948; English Heritage gives 1948 as the start of its use, and one timeline gives 1947. The simplest reading is that staff began to arrive in 1947 and the move was complete in 1948.

In 1949 the institute was transferred to the Agricultural Research Council, which meant more long-term research and less routine testing, though tractor testing continued. It became the AFRC (Agriculture and Food Research Council) Institute of Engineering Research in 1986, and was renamed Silsoe Research Institute in 1991 as a more manageable name. In 1994 the new Biotechnology and Biological Sciences Research Council (BBSRC) took over and the institute became one of its eight grant-supported institutes. Work widened to physical, engineering and mathematical methods applied to farming and biological processes.

In 2004 the BBSRC decided to stop supporting it because its own research priorities had changed. The institute ceased operations at the end of March 2006, after 80 years. English Heritage's own history also gives 2006. Wikipedia's article on Silsoe says "until 2007", and Cranfield University's separate Silsoe College (founded as the National College of Agricultural Engineering, at Silsoe from 1963) finished moving to Cranfield by December 2007, which may be the source of that second date. This note takes 2006 for the institute.

The MERL archive of the institute covers 1918–2005 and runs to 49 linear metres.

Sources: Silsoe Research Institute history (silsoeresearch.org.uk/sri-history); Silsoe Research Institute home page (closure); Museum of English Rural Life, SR SRI archive catalogue; English Heritage, History of Wrest Park; Wikipedia, Silsoe; Wikipedia, National College of Agricultural Engineering.

## Sun Insurance at Wrest in the Second World War
Kind: site
About: sun-insurance
The Sun Insurance Office bought Wrest Park in 1939 as a wartime head office, away from the bombing risk in London. Several sources agree on 1939. Geograph descriptions say it was "bought in 1939 by the Sun Insurance Company as its WWII HQ", and Bedfordshire Archives holds a Sun Insurance Office list, dated 1939, that assigns individual departments to Wrest Park and to Mickleham Downs in Surrey in case of evacuation. So the plan was made before war broke out, and both Wrest and a Surrey site were considered. The timeline's 1940 date therefore fits the move-in rather than the purchase: archive photographs dated about 1939–1940 show Sun staff already living in the house, with air-raid shelters being built between the living quarters.

On numbers, Wikipedia (citing the Imperial War Museum's film record, "The Sun at Wrest in Beds") says the company moved from Threadneedle Street in London, built 12 huts and converted the stables to house 300 employees. A search summary of the Imperial War Museum record adds that most staff lived on site from Monday to Friday and went home at weekends; that detail has not been read in the record itself. The Museum's page could not be opened, so the huts and 300 figures rest on Wikipedia's reading and should be treated as secondary.

One Sun employee, L. A. Worker, later gave Bedfordshire Archives his papers and photographs of the gardens and park "before it was ploughed up", dated 1940–1949. Parks & Gardens UK, drawing on the Historic England entry, says almost all park and avenue trees were felled between the 1920s and the 1940s. Sun Insurance later became Sun Alliance, now part of RSA.

When did Sun leave? No source read gives the exact year. The sources agree that after the war the estate passed to the state, and Geograph gives 1946 for the Ministry's purchase. The dates file therefore runs the Sun years 1939 to 1946, which is unconfirmed at the end.

Why Wrest? The sources do not say. The obvious reasons are a country house away from London's bombing, with parkland for huts and stables to convert, but that is inference, not something the records state.

Sources: Wikipedia, Wrest Park (citing Imperial War Museums, "The Sun at Wrest in Beds", object 1060036428; page not readable); Bedfordshire Archives catalogue Z503/8, Z50/104/35, Z50/104/37, Z503/20/1-23; Geograph shared description of Wrest House and Park (snippet 7382); Parks & Gardens UK, Wrest Park.

## Why the state bought Wrest Park (1946–1954)
Kind: reference
About: ministry-purchase, niae
After the Second World War a government ministry bought the house, gardens and part of the park. Historic England's register entry (as summarised by Parks & Gardens UK) calls it the Ministry of Public Building and Works. That ministry did not exist until 1962, so the entry is using the later name for the department that was the Ministry of Works in the 1940s (general knowledge, to be verified). The register entry (written while English Heritage managed the site) says English Heritage "lease the site" to Silsoe Research Institute, the institute that grew out of the NIAE.

The date of the purchase is disputed. Geograph descriptions, which echo the Historic England listing, say 1946. A search summary attributed 1949 to Country Life, but that article could not be opened and Wikipedia's Wrest Park text gives no year, so 1949 is unconfirmed. A search summary of a 1960 Hansard written answer mentions work on the garden buildings that began in September 1949. That may explain a 1949 date for the Ministry's involvement, but the answer itself could not be opened. The dates file uses 1946 and flags it.

Why was the state interested? The government wanted a home for the NIAE, whose staff had reached 150 in 1947 and had been in temporary quarters near York since 1942. The wartime Ministry of Agriculture had already directed the institute, and Wrest had an empty house, stables, outbuildings and room for test fields. The sources do not give the Ministry's motives in its own words, so this is inference.

The state also opened the garden. A written Commons answer of 1 November 1960 says the gardens had been open to the public on summer weekends since 1954, and that work on the garden buildings was unfinished, with completion hoped for in 1961. A search summary of a February 1960 answer says the house and gardens were leased to the British Society for Research in Agricultural Engineering and occupied by NIAE; this was not read directly.

Sources: Parks & Gardens UK, Wrest Park (Historic England register 1000113); Geograph photo descriptions 3153875 and snippet 7382; Hansard, written answer "Wrest Park, Bedfordshire", 1 November 1960 (api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard); Silsoe Research Institute history; general knowledge on the Ministry of Works.

## The driverless tractor and the buried wire
Kind: site
About: driverless-tractor, niae
The family's top interests meet here. The idea was to steer a tractor with no driver by following a signal in the ground, and the story starts at Reading University, not Silsoe.

According to Farmers Weekly's "Machinery milestones", the Reading project began in the mid-1950s on an International Harvester B-250 tractor. Steering followed "an electric current carried in a wire buried in the ground below cultivation depth". Development was at the university's Sonning farm, and the tractor was first shown about 1958 to "an audience of highly sceptical agricultural students". Sensors on the front followed the wire and steered the tractor. A front-mounted emergency stop was triggered by touching an obstruction. Programmed signals sent along the wire could raise or lower the implement linkage at the headland turn, operate the power take-off, or stop the tractor.

How does that work? An alternating current in a long wire produces a magnetic field around it. Pick-up coils on the front of the tractor measure how strong the field is on each side, and the steering moves to keep the two readings balanced, so the wire acts as an invisible rail. That is general physics (magnetic induction), not something the article spells out. The brief calls it a "leader cable"; Farmers Weekly says "guide wire".

Reading then handed the project to the NIAE. Silsoe carried on with the buried-wire system on a Fordson Major tractor that had already been used in a hydrostatic transmission project, and demonstrated the emergency stop with a straw bale. Farmers Weekly gives no dates for the Silsoe phase, so the earlier tour text ("1950s: Silsoe took over") should not be taken as a firm date. The date used in the dates file is that of the Reading start, about 1955.

It was not alone. Auto-Track, a company in Hampshire, developed a buried-wire tractor and showed a prototype at the 1969 Royal Show.

Sources: Farmers Weekly, "Machinery milestones: driverless tractors"; general knowledge (magnetic induction, flagged verify).

## Machine vision and the robot hoe
Kind: site
About: robot-hoe, guided-hoe-sale, niae
By the early 1990s the institute had moved from buried wires to cameras. Farmers Weekly says it began a new driverless project then, and demonstrated it in 1996. The vehicle was a small tool-carrier tractor with a 10 horsepower engine. Instead of a wire, steering came from computer analysis of images from a CCTV camera. It was driven by hand to and from the field. In the field it ran up and down crop rows and made 180-degree turns at the headland on its own. It could pick out weeds between the rows and spot-treat them with herbicide, and put fertiliser close to the plants. Its guidance was accurate enough to run hoe blades within 2 centimetres of the crop plants.

A Farmers Weekly report of 28 February 1997 says the experimental spray vehicle had first been demonstrated in summer 1996 and that the Ministry of Agriculture would fund a down-the-row hoe from 1 April 1997. In plot tests on brassica crops, targeted treatment cut herbicide use by 90 per cent compared with blanket spraying. Nick Tillett was the project co-ordinator and expected the first commercial product to be a steerage hoe, a tractor attachment with a driver, with the guidance added. The fully automatic vehicle would come later.

That is what happened. Farmers Weekly (12 May 2000) reports the camera system fitted to a standard steerage hoe: a mass-produced black-and-white video camera, scanned 25 times a second by a Pentium-based computer, finds the crop rows ahead, and the steering keeps the blades between them. In winter wheat trials it cut herbicide use by 80 per cent without reducing yields at about 6 kilometres per hour, a speed limited by the hoe blades rather than the guidance. Professor John Marchant of the institute said a hoe developed with Garford Engineering and Robydome Electronics would be on sale in 2001. A search summary of an IAgrE profile says the Garford Robocrop hoe was first sold in 2001, and that Nick Tillett and Tony Hague set up Tillett and Hague Technology after the closure was announced in 2005, with over 600 machines sold using their computer-vision technology. Farmers Weekly's 'milestones' article adds that the hoped-for commercial driverless row-crop tractor never appeared.

The 1996 demonstration is the one dated in the tour timeline. The research papers by Hague, Marchant and Tillett were not located, so figures here come from press reports.

Sources: Farmers Weekly, "Machinery milestones: driverless tractors"; Farmers Weekly, "MAFF to finance robot weeder development" (28 February 1997); Farmers Weekly, "Auto-guidance hoe cuts down pesticides use" (12 May 2000); Farmers Weekly, "Robot hoe to reduce pesticide use" (11 September 2000); IAgrE profile of Dr Nick Tillett (via search summary, unverified); Tillett and Hague Technology listing.

## Tractor testing and safety cabs
Kind: site
About: tractor-testing, safety-cab-tests, tractor-cab-regulations, niae
Testing was the institute's oldest job. The Ministry of Agriculture set up the Agricultural Machinery Testing Committee in 1924, the year the institute began, and testing was done at first solely for the Ministry. Manufacturers soon wanted independent results to help them develop new machines. The institute's history says tractor testing "always continued in some capacity", from the World Agricultural Tractor Trials of 1930 through the Royal Agricultural Society trials of the 1930s to later NIAE, British Standards Institution and OECD tests. When the institute moved to the Agricultural Research Council in 1949, research grew and testing shrank, but did not stop.

The Museum of English Rural Life holds the institute's archive, with printed test reports on hundreds of machines: grass and grain dryers, potato planters and harvesters, slurry pumps, silage equipment and tractors. The latest tractor report seen in the catalogue is a 1991 Massey-Ferguson 399.

Safety cabs are a good example. The catalogue includes NIAE reports on the Bonser Safety Weather Cab Mk II on a David Brown 990 (1963), the Sta-Dri Mark III cab on a Fordson Super Major (1964), the Duncan Safety Frame and Cab (1964) and a County 1164 tractor fitted with the County Safety Cab (1971). New Zealand safety-frame reports of 1965–1967 say their strength tests used apparatus similar to the NIAE's at Silsoe, and a Spanish official bulletin of 1980 approved a cab on the strength of an OECD test carried out by NIAE (both via search summaries, so verify).

Why did it matter? A tractor can overturn, and a rigid cab or frame keeps the driver from being crushed. The Agriculture (Tractor Cabs) Regulations 1974 require safety cabs, which include safety frames, on tractors driven by agricultural workers, with certificates of approval given by the Minister, and a mark on each approved cab. A "Q" mark means the noise inside is 90 dBA or less. A farming-industry article reports about 45 deaths a year from overturning tractors before 1974, falling sharply after (search summary, verify). Whether NIAE's tests were the basis of the statutory approvals is not confirmed in the sources read: the catalogue shows only that it tested cabs and frames.

Sources: Silsoe Research Institute history; Museum of English Rural Life, SR SRI catalogue (items ET2/1/502, 533, 545, 766, 885); The Agriculture (Tractor Cabs) Regulations 1974, SI 1974/2034, explanatory note (legislation.gov.uk); AGRIS records of Lincoln University (NZ) frame reports; Spanish BOE bulletin of 17 January 1980; FarmingUK, "Wise-up on new tractor law say HSE".

## Crop drying, spray drift and the Silsoe cube
Kind: site
About: crop-drying, spray-drift, silsoe-cube, niae
Three strands of the institute's physics and engineering are worth knowing.

Crop drying goes back to Oxford. The 1924 institute was already researching it, alongside subsoiling and haymaking. Hay and grain stored damp will heat and rot, so warm air is blown through them. The MERL catalogue lists a booklet "Crop Drier Engineering Development" (undated, 1940–1949), "The Testing of Grass Driers" (1950) and test reports on many grain, grass and pyrethrum dryers from the 1950s onwards. The institute also tested dryers made by firms such as Gascoigne, Blanch-Bettison and Penney and Porter.

Spray drift is the sideways carry of fine droplets from a boom sprayer onto neighbouring land. Paul Miller of Silsoe Research Institute presented a 1999 paper on field-boundary drift to the British Crop Protection Council. Its finding, as summarised in the search result, was that drift can be related to the share of the spray volume in small droplets, and that for all nozzle types the height of the boom is critical. A mechanistic "Silsoe Drift Model" was developed by Miller and Hadfield, and a later ground-boom model by Clare Butler-Ellis and Miller. The ways to cut drift that Miller lists are low-drift nozzles, such as air-induction nozzles, and keeping the boom low and steady. Miller later led research at Wrest Park and spoke at the IAgrE's 2022 celebration.

The Silsoe cube is the most famous piece of physics. Researchers at the institute, chiefly Richards and Hoxey (as authors of the papers), built a cube 6 metres on each side in open country on the Silsoe site and measured the wind pressure on its faces and the airflow around it. A cube is simple, symmetrical and can be turned to face the wind at different angles. A 2002 paper by Hoxey, Richards and Short compared full-scale measurements with wind-tunnel results, and found that suction on the roof and side walls with the wind square-on was stronger in the real wind than in most tunnel tests. The cube is now a standard check for wind-tunnel and computer-fluid-dynamics work. A search summary says Birmingham University's Silsoe-Birmingham venture still offers a full-scale test site at Wrest Park. The year it was built and its exact position on the estate were not found, so do not tell visitors where to look.
