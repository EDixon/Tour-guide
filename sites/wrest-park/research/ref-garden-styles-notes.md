# Garden styles and garden buildings (reference pack)

## The French formal garden (Le Nôtre and Versailles)
Kind: reference
About: andre-le-notre, versailles-gardens, french-formal-garden
The French formal garden reached its height under Louis XIV. Its great designer was André Le Nôtre (1613–1700), a gardener's son who became Louis XIV's royal gardener. At Vaux-le-Vicomte (1656–1661) and then at Versailles, from 1661, he treated the garden as an extension of the building: a strong central axis ran out from the house, with cross-axes, long avenues, terraces and water laid out by geometry and seen best from above.

The parts have names. A parterre is a flat, level, patterned garden beside the house, meant to be viewed from the windows or a terrace. A parterre de broderie ("embroidery parterre") uses curling box hedges, like the scrolls of embroidery, on a background of coloured gravel, sand or crushed brick. Beyond the parterres came the bosquets (formal groves cut with paths), then the wider park. Water was treated the same way: still, straight-sided canals and basins that mirrored the sky. The Grand Canal at Versailles, dug in the late 1660s and early 1670s, is cross-shaped and roughly 1.5 kilometres long. Fountains were fed by a huge and not always adequate machine-powered water supply.

The idea behind it was control. The garden showed that the king, like the gardener, could impose order on nature. Le Nôtre never came to England, but his pupils and books did: Dezallier d'Argenville's treatise on gardening (1709) was translated into English in 1712, and English designers such as George London and Henry Wise adapted the French pattern, often on a smaller scale and with more emphasis on walks and hedges.
Sources: Encyclopaedia Britannica, "André Le Nôtre" and "Garden and landscape design"; Château de Versailles, history of the gardens; Oxford Dictionary of National Biography entries on London and Wise; Historic England, Introductions to Heritage Assets: Designed Landscapes; Jellicoe and others, The Oxford Companion to Gardens (1986).

## The Dutch formal garden and William III
Kind: reference
About: dutch-formal-garden, het-loo, hampton-court-gardens, william-iii
The Dutch style is not the same as the French. Where French gardens were vast, with long views over a whole landscape, Dutch gardens were small, walled or hedged, and full of detail. They were laid out in a rectangle, often with a straight canal along one side, and packed with clipped yews and box (topiary), pleached hedges, small compartments, summerhouses, statues, tulips and other bulbs, and orange trees in tubs. Water was normally a canal rather than a cascade, because the Netherlands is flat and waterlogged: a canal drained the garden as well as decorating it.

The best-known royal example is Het Loo, near Apeldoorn, built from 1684 for William of Orange as Stadtholder. After William and Mary were invited to take the English throne in 1688, they brought Dutch taste with them. At Hampton Court from 1689 the designer Daniel Marot and the royal gardeners George London and Henry Wise remodelled the great gardens, while Charles II's earlier canal (the Long Water) and the fan of avenues known as a patte d'oie (see below) stayed. Kensington Palace gardens were also reshaped.

Dutch fashion reached the gentry as well: Westbury Court in Gloucestershire (laid out 1696–1705, now National Trust) is a rare surviving small Dutch water garden. Straight canals, clipped evergreens and ranks of potted citrus became fashionable in England in the 1690s and early 1700s, and remained so until the move to a more "natural" look in the 1730s.
Sources: Encyclopaedia Britannica, "William III"; Historic Royal Palaces, Hampton Court gardens history; National Trust, Westbury Court Garden; Palace Het Loo Museum, garden history; Oxford DNB, "London, George" and "Wise, Henry".

## Wildernesses, rides and garden buildings as eye-catchers
Kind: reference
About: formal-woodland-garden
In the early 1700s, English owners with space and money made formal gardens out of woodland. A "wilderness" meant not wild country but a planted grove cut with geometric paths, often in the shape of a maze, star or set of diagonal walks, usually behind the flower garden. A patte d'oie ("goose foot") is a spread of three or more straight avenues radiating from one point, like the toes of a goose. It was borrowed from France and used to organise a wood, so that walkers could see along each ride to something at the end.

That something was an eye-catcher: a temple, pavilion, statue, obelisk, urn or gate placed so that a ride framed it. This was a main use of garden buildings. The building did two jobs: it drew the eye and the visitor along the path, and once reached it gave a seat, shade or shelter and a new view back. A fine example of the formal woodland garden with garden buildings is the early layout at Stowe in Buckinghamshire, under Charles Bridgeman from about 1714.

Many such gardens were altered or swept away after about 1740 by the fashion for the landscape style, but the lines of rides and the buildings at their ends often survived, and today they are among the clues that an estate had a formal garden.
Sources: Historic England, Introductions to Heritage Assets: Registered Parks and Gardens and Garden Buildings; Garden History Society / The Gardens Trust, "Formal gardens"; Oxford Companion to Gardens (general reference); National Trust, Stowe.

## The English landscape garden
Kind: reference
About: english-landscape-garden, charles-bridgeman, william-kent, stowe-gardens, ha-ha
From the 1720s a new idea developed in England: that a garden should look like an idealised natural landscape, in the manner of the paintings of Claude and Poussin. The poet Alexander Pope and the Earl of Shaftesbury's followers argued for "nature", and designers began to soften the straight lines.

Charles Bridgeman (about 1690–1738), a royal gardener to George II's court from 1728, was the bridge between the two styles. At Stowe he kept a formal outline but introduced a ha-ha, and he helped create the Serpentine in Hyde Park and Kensington Gardens in 1730–1731, a lake with curving shores. A ha-ha is a ditch with a vertical retaining wall on the garden side and a sloping bank on the other, so that the view runs out into the park without a visible fence, but cattle and deer cannot cross. The name is said to come from the surprised "ha!" of a walker who stumbles on one.

William Kent (about 1685–1748), a painter and architect turned garden designer, took the next step. At Rousham (Oxfordshire) and the Elysian Fields at Stowe he arranged trees, water and classical temples as a series of "pictures" to be found on a circuit walk. Horace Walpole wrote that Kent "leaped the fence, and saw that all nature was a garden". Serpentine streams, clumps of trees and open grass followed. Lancelot "Capability" Brown (see his own section) later turned this into a whole-landscape style.
Sources: Oxford DNB, "Bridgeman, Charles" and "Kent, William"; Historic England, Introductions to Heritage Assets: Designed Landscapes; National Trust, Stowe and Rousham histories; Horace Walpole, On Modern Gardening (1771, published 1780); Encyclopaedia Britannica, "Landscape gardening".

## Lancelot "Capability" Brown (1716–1783)
Kind: person
About: capability-brown
Lancelot Brown was born in 1716 at Kirkharle in Northumberland, and began as a gardener's boy. In about 1739 he went south, and from 1741 worked at Stowe in Buckinghamshire under William Kent, becoming head gardener there. He set up on his own in 1751 and over the next three decades designed or advised on perhaps 170 estates, among them Blenheim, Warwick Castle, Petworth, Croome and Chatsworth. From 1764 he was Master Gardener at Hampton Court, which gave him a royal salary and a house there. He died in 1783.

The nickname is said to come from his telling clients that their land had "capabilities". His approach was to remove walls, parterres and avenues close to the house, and to bring smooth lawn right up to the windows. He then shaped the ground with gentle swells, and planted belts of trees round the edge and clumps of trees on the rises. He also dammed streams to make large lakes with winding edges which seemed to be natural rivers; a good deal of his effort went into the engineering of hidden dams, drains and sluices. The ha-ha kept livestock off the lawn. In 2016 the 300th anniversary of his birth was marked by a national celebration, led by the Landscape Institute and others.

Brown's clients paid well, and critics, including William Chambers and the Picturesque writers, said he made all parks look the same.
Sources: Oxford DNB, "Brown, Lancelot [Capability]"; Landscape Institute, Capability Brown Festival 2016 pages; National Trust, "Capability Brown"; Historic England, Capability Brown landscapes; Encyclopaedia Britannica.

## Humphry Repton and the Picturesque
Kind: person
About: humphry-repton, picturesque
Humphry Repton (1752–1818) set up as a landscape gardener in 1788, in effect as Brown's successor. He was a good artist, and he is famous for his "Red Books": hand-written reports bound in red morocco, with watercolours whose flaps lifted to show "before" and "after" views of an estate. Clients could see exactly what they were buying. He published Sketches and Hints on Landscape Gardening in 1795.

Repton stood between Brown's smooth parkland and the wilder Picturesque taste. Writers such as Uvedale Price and Richard Payne Knight argued that Brown's lawns were bland and that landscape should be rugged, varied and irregular, like a painting by Salvator Rosa. Repton took a middle way. He kept Brown's lakes and clumps, but he put back a terrace or flower garden beside the house, so that the house had a setting, and he suggested cottages, rustic bridges and winding walks. His reintroduction of terraces and formal flower beds near the house helped to prepare the way for the revived formal gardens of the nineteenth century.
Sources: Oxford DNB, "Repton, Humphry"; Encyclopaedia Britannica, "Humphry Repton"; Historic England, Introductions to Heritage Assets: Designed Landscapes; The Gardens Trust, Repton bicentenary pages (2018).

## The Victorian return to formal gardens
Kind: reference
About: victorian-formal-garden, nesfield, charles-barry, bedding-schemes
By the 1830s many owners felt that Brown's bare lawns left a country house floating without a setting. Gardeners and writers (J. C. Loudon, and later Reginald Blomfield, whose 1892 book The Formal Garden in England argued for architecture in the garden) pressed for terraces and parterres to come back near the house, with the park beyond.

Two men led this. Sir Charles Barry (1795–1860), architect of the Houses of Parliament, laid out Italianate terraced gardens at places such as Trentham, Shrubland Park and Cliveden. William Andrews Nesfield (1793–1881), a former soldier and watercolourist, was the most sought-after garden designer of his day. He revived the parterre de broderie, using coloured gravel, crushed brick, glass and box in French patterns, at Castle Howard, Witley Court and elsewhere, and he worked at Kew. Italianate gardens had terraces, balustrades, fountains and urns. French revival gardens used scrolling box.

New plants made the colour. Greenhouse-raised tender plants like pelargoniums ("geraniums"), calceolarias, verbenas and lobelias could be bedded out in hundreds by early summer, and glass was now cheap. This gave "bedding schemes" and later "carpet bedding". Gardeners were also helped by the lawn mower (patented 1830) and by cheaper heating. The style was criticised later, by William Robinson and Gertrude Jekyll, who preferred looser planting.
Sources: Oxford DNB, "Barry, Sir Charles" and "Nesfield, William Andrews"; Historic England, Introductions to Heritage Assets: Designed Landscapes, and Parks and Gardens registration notes; The Gardens Trust, "Victorian gardens"; RHS, history of bedding; Encyclopaedia Britannica.

## Orangeries: how citrus was grown and heated
Kind: reference
About: orangery, orangeries-and-heating, glass-tax-repeal
Oranges and lemons do not survive English winters, but they were wanted as status symbols from the 1600s. The solution was to grow them in tubs and carry them outdoors for the summer, then shelter them in a building from about October to May. That building was the orangery: a tall, south-facing room with big windows on one side and a solid roof and walls on the others. Citrus need frost-free conditions rather than heat, so early orangeries had little heating: charcoal or coke stoves or braziers, or small furnaces with flues (brick or tile channels) running through the walls or under the floor. Orangeries with a heated floor (a hypocaust) used the same principle as Roman buildings. Shutters covered the glass on freezing nights.

A greenhouse or "stove" was different: a hot, glass building for tropical plants such as pineapples. Early ones used tan-bark pits (the rotting bark gave off heat). Hot-water pipes, heated by a boiler and circulating by gravity, came into use in the 1820s and 1830s, and were more even and safer than flues. The weight of glass was another limit. Excise duty on glass, in place from 1746, made big panes expensive; it was repealed in 1845. After that, and with the new sheet glass of the 1830s, glasshouses became larger and lighter. Joseph Paxton's Great Conservatory at Chatsworth (1836–1840) and his Crystal Palace (1851) were the best-known results.
Sources: RHS, history of greenhouses and orangeries; Historic England, Introductions to Heritage Assets: Garden Buildings; National Trust, collections and "Orangeries"; Oxford DNB, "Paxton, Sir Joseph"; Encyclopaedia Britannica, "Orangery" and "Greenhouse".

## Joseph Paxton, Chatsworth and the Crystal Palace
Kind: person
About: joseph-paxton, glass-tax-repeal, chatsworth-great-conservatory, crystal-palace
Joseph Paxton (1803–1865) was a farmer's son from Bedfordshire who became head gardener to the 6th Duke of Devonshire at Chatsworth in 1826, when he was 23. He built the Great Conservatory there in 1836–1840: a curved glasshouse about 300 feet (90 metres) long, heated by hot-water pipes from boilers beneath. In 1849 he got the giant Amazon water lily (Victoria amazonica) to flower in a special house at Chatsworth. He claimed that the leaf's ribbed underside inspired the structure of his next design.

When Prince Albert's Great Exhibition of 1851 needed a building in Hyde Park within months, the official designs failed. Paxton sketched his own on blotting paper, and the Crystal Palace was built of cast-iron columns, wooden sashes and more than 290,000 identical glass panes, made in standard sizes, in a few months. It was a greenhouse on a vast scale. It was later moved to Sydenham in south London, where it stood until it burned down in 1936. Paxton was knighted in 1851 and later became an MP. The repeal of the glass duty in 1845 had made his kind of building affordable.
Sources: Oxford DNB, "Paxton, Sir Joseph"; Chatsworth House Trust, history of the Great Conservatory; Victoria and Albert Museum, Great Exhibition 1851 pages; Encyclopaedia Britannica, "Crystal Palace".

## Garden follies and buildings: temples, bath houses, hermitages and ruins
Kind: reference
About: garden-buildings, cold-bath, hermitage
Historic England describes garden buildings as structures that give pleasure, shelter or a focus in a designed landscape. Many are called follies, though most had a use.

A temple or summerhouse, often in Greek or Roman style, was a place to sit and admire a view, and a banqueting house was a small room, often away from the house, for the sweet course and wine. The Gothic taste (see Batty Langley) produced Gothic towers and arches. A bath house or cold plunge bath was a small building with a stone-lined pool, filled with spring water. Cold bathing was believed healthy after Sir John Floyer's book on the subject (1697), and cold plunges were seen as invigorating and good for rheumatism and "nerves". Owners built them near a spring, usually unheated and with a small room to undress.

A hermitage was a rustic hut or cell, sometimes with a paid or imaginary hermit, to promote reflection: the owner of Painshill (Surrey) is said to have advertised for one. A sham ruin was a fake medieval castle, abbey or Roman arch, built to give age and romance to a view, a trend that peaked in the mid 1700s. Grottoes, shell houses and rustic root-houses are others. Such buildings were meant to surprise, with a route planned to reach each at the best moment.
Sources: Historic England, Introductions to Heritage Assets: Garden Buildings (and Follies); Oxford Companion to Gardens (general reference); National Trust, "Follies"; Oxford DNB, "Floyer, Sir John".

## Chinoiserie in English gardens
Kind: reference
About: chinoiserie, william-chambers, kew-pagoda, chinese-designs-book, willow-pattern
Chinoiserie means European design inspired by (and often misunderstanding) China. It was fashionable across Europe in the mid 1700s, as trade brought in porcelain, lacquer and wallpaper. In English gardens it meant brightly painted pavilions, lattice bridges and pagodas with bells and dragons at the roof corners, usually set among water.

William Chambers (1723–1796) had visited Canton as a young man working for the Swedish East India Company, and published Designs of Chinese Buildings in 1757. He then designed buildings at Kew for Princess Augusta, including the ten-storey Pagoda of 1762, around 50 metres high; its eighty dragons were once gilded. Chambers later claimed in his Dissertation on Oriental Gardening (1772) that Chinese gardens were a guide to irregular design, though he admitted he had to be a little fanciful. Thomas Chippendale also popularised the taste, with "Chinese Chippendale" furniture in the 1750s and 1760s. Amateur pattern books, such as those by Edwards and Darly, spread the fashion.

The "Willow Pattern" is a later piece of English chinoiserie. Blue-and-white china printed with a bridge, pagoda, willow and two birds was devised by English potters in the 1790s, and the story of the lovers was added afterwards. It was not a Chinese pattern, though it was inspired by Chinese blue-and-white porcelain.
Sources: Oxford DNB, "Chambers, Sir William"; Historic Royal Palaces, Kew Pagoda; V&A, "Chinoiserie"; Encyclopaedia Britannica, "Chinoiserie"; Spode / Victoria and Albert Museum, willow pattern history.

## Lead garden statuary
Kind: reference
About: lead-garden-statuary, john-van-nost, andrew-carpenter, john-cheere
Before the 1700s, garden statues were mostly marble and imported from Italy. In England from about 1690 many were cast in lead, which was cheaper, tough enough to stand outside, and easy to make from a mould. A sculptor made a model, a mould was taken, and the lead was cast in sections, then soldered together. The same model could be cast many times.

The leading yard was run by John Nost (or Van Nost) the elder, a Flemish sculptor who died in 1710. The yard at Hyde Park Corner passed to John Van Nost the younger (died 1729) and then to Andrew Carpenter (about 1677–1737), who had worked for the Nosts. John Cheere (1709–1787) bought Carpenter's stock and yard about 1739 and sold a wide range of figures: classical gods, shepherds and shepherdesses, seasons, Roman emperors and even animals, plus busts, garden vases and urns, sometimes in plaster. Customers could choose from a catalogue of stock, giving the effect of a showroom.

Lead statues were often painted, commonly to imitate stone, and some were bronzed or gilded. Many old garden figures have lost their paint, which is why people sometimes see them as plain grey.
Sources: Oxford DNB, "Nost, John" and "Cheere, Sir Henry" and "Cheere, John"; Historic England, garden-sculpture listings; V&A, "Garden statuary"; Gunnis, Dictionary of British Sculptors (general reference).

## Trompe-l'œil and painted rooms
Kind: reference
About: trompe-l-oeil
Trompe-l'œil is French for "deceive the eye": a painting so realistic that it looks like a real object, or a flat surface made to look three-dimensional. The idea is ancient. Roman villas at Pompeii had painted walls with fake windows, columns and garden views. It returned in Renaissance Italy, and in the 1600s and 1700s was used in English houses: a painted niche with a statue, a door that does not open, a ceiling that seems to open on to the sky, or a landscape painted over a wall, used to suggest more space or grandeur than was there.

Foreign painters such as Antonio Verrio and Louis Laguerre did big painted staircases and ceilings in the 1680s and 1700s. Grisaille, painting in shades of grey to look like stone carving, was a cheaper way to give a room carved ornament. In gardens, "dummy boards" (cut-out painted wooden figures of people or animals) and painted walls were used from the 1600s to fool the eye at a distance, and a painted perspective could make a short garden alley seem to continue.

Painters used shadows, careful perspective and a fixed viewing point; trompe-l'œil usually works best from one spot, and falls apart once you move.
Sources: Encyclopaedia Britannica, "Trompe l'oeil"; V&A, "Trompe l'oeil"; Oxford DNB, "Verrio, Antonio" and "Laguerre, Louis"; National Trust, "Painted decoration".
