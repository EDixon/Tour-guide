## From horses to tractors in Britain
Kind: reference
About: ivel-agricultural-motor, fordson-model-f, ferguson-te20, dan-albone, henry-ford
For thousands of years British farms ran on muscle: oxen, then mostly horses. Horse numbers on farms peaked in the early twentieth century at somewhere around a million working animals, and a large share of every farm's land grew oats and hay to feed them. A tractor needs no feed when it is idle, which is a big part of its appeal.

Steam traction engines worked in Victorian fields, but they were heavy and mostly used for threshing and ploughing by contractors. The first successful light petrol tractor in Britain was the Ivel Agricultural Motor of 1902, built by Dan Albone, a bicycle maker in Biggleswade, Bedfordshire. It had three wheels, weighed far less than a steam engine, and could plough, haul and drive belt-powered machinery. Several hundred were built, and one is in the Science Museum collection.

The big push came in the First World War. German submarines threatened imports, and the government ordered thousands of Fordson Model F tractors from Henry Ford's firm in 1917 to plough up more grassland. The Fordson was small, light and cheap, and it made farmers see what tractors could do.

Change was still gradual. Horses remained common through the 1930s, when tractors began to be fitted with rubber tyres and power take-off shafts. The real turning point came after the Second World War with the Ferguson TE20 and its three-point linkage, and with wartime labour shortages and government grants. By the mid-1950s tractors outnumbered working horses on British farms, and by the 1960s the farm horse was a rarity. The exact crossover year depends on which agricultural census figures you use.

Sources: Museum of English Rural Life (Reading), tractor and horse collections and online histories; Science Museum Group collection, Ivel Agricultural Motor; Encyclopaedia Britannica, "tractor" and "Fordson"; general knowledge for the approximate horse and tractor numbers (check against Ministry of Agriculture census returns before quoting figures).

## Harry Ferguson and the three-point linkage
Kind: person
About: harry-ferguson, ferguson-te20, three-point-linkage, ferguson-ford-handshake, david-brown
Harry Ferguson was born in 1884 on a farm at Growell, County Down, in what is now Northern Ireland, and died in 1960. He started as a motor mechanic, built and flew his own aeroplane in 1909, and then turned to tractors.

His great idea was the three-point linkage. Earlier, a plough was simply dragged behind a tractor on a chain. If it hit a rock or heavy ground, the tractor could rear up and tip over backwards. Ferguson's system joined the implement to the tractor by two lower arms and a single upper arm, so the two became one rigid unit. Hydraulics lifted and lowered the implement. The upper arm sensed the pull on the implement, and the hydraulics raised it a little in hard ground. This "draft control" also pushed some of the implement's weight onto the tractor's rear wheels, giving better grip and making rearing-up much less likely.

Ferguson built a tractor around the idea with the engineer David Brown: the Ferguson-Brown Type A, from 1936. In 1938 he showed his system to Henry Ford, and the two reached a famous "handshake agreement" in 1939. Ford produced the Ford-Ferguson 9N in the United States. The partnership ended in 1946, and Ferguson went on to build the Ferguson TE20 in Coventry, nicknamed the "Little Grey Fergie". About half a million were made between 1946 and 1956.

Ferguson later sued Ford for using his ideas, and settled in 1952. His company merged with Massey-Harris in 1953. The three-point linkage, standardised in dimension categories, is still found on almost every tractor today, which is why one maker's plough can be attached to another maker's tractor.

Sources: Encyclopaedia Britannica, "Harry Ferguson"; Museum of English Rural Life, Ferguson collection and tractor histories; Science Museum Group, Ferguson-Brown and TE20 entries; general knowledge for the linkage mechanics.

## Tractor safety: overturning, roll-over protective structures and safety cabs
Kind: reference
About: rops-regulations, ferguson-te20
Tractors have a high centre of gravity, work on slopes and ditches, and tow heavy loads. That makes them prone to overturning, and early tractors had no protection at all. Overturning accidents, either sideways on a slope or backwards when the front lifts, have long been one of the most common ways farm workers were killed.

There are two typical ways to overturn. A tractor can tip backwards if a towed load or a stuck implement pulls the front up. The three-point linkage and draft control reduced this. It can also roll sideways on a bank or when turning with a raised load. A driver without protection can then be crushed under the machine.

The answer is a roll-over protective structure, known as a ROPS: a strong frame, or a cab built on one, which holds its shape and leaves a survival space around the driver. It works together with a seat belt that keeps the driver inside it. The structure is tested by pushing, loading and, in some tests, dropping a weight onto it. Sweden was an early leader and made protective structures a requirement in 1959. In Britain, makers developed safety frames and cabs in the 1960s, and the law followed. The Agriculture (Tractor Cabs) Regulations 1974 (SI 1974/2034) required approved safety cabs on tractors driven by agricultural workers. They also had a noise limit of 90 dBA for cabs marked "Q". Later UK rules and international test standards, including those of the OECD, kept strengthening and harmonising the requirements.

The effect was large. Deaths from overturning fell sharply after the cab rules, though agriculture remains one of Britain's more dangerous industries, according to the Health and Safety Executive.

Sources: legislation.gov.uk, The Agriculture (Tractor Cabs) Regulations 1974 (SI 1974/2034); Health and Safety Executive, agriculture and tractor safety pages; OECD tractor testing codes; general knowledge for the Swedish 1959 date (check before quoting; the exact commencement date of the British rules was not verified).

## Guidance by buried wire: how a tractor can follow a cable
Kind: reference
About: buried-wire-guidance
A buried-wire guidance system turns a basic fact of physics into a steering signal. When an alternating current flows along a wire, it creates a magnetic field that circles the wire and keeps changing direction. The field is strongest close to the wire and weakens rapidly with distance.

The vehicle carries two pick-up coils, one on each side of the front, or a pair of coils side by side. A changing magnetic field passing through a coil induces a small alternating voltage in it, which is the principle of electromagnetic induction. The nearer coil lies in a stronger field, so it produces a bigger voltage than the further one.

If the vehicle is centred over the wire, the two voltages are equal and their difference is zero: no steering is needed. If it drifts to the left, the left-hand coil gives the bigger signal. The difference between the two, called the error signal, tells the controller which way it has drifted and by how much. The controller turns that error into a steering command through a motor or hydraulic ram. The vehicle moves back towards the wire, the difference shrinks, and it settles into following the line. This is simple feedback control, like keeping a bicycle upright.

The current is usually set at a particular frequency, so that the vehicle's electronics can ignore other electrical noise. Extra tones can be sent along the same wire to give commands, such as "lift the plough" at the field end. The same method has been used in factory and warehouse trucks, which follow wires buried in the floor.

The drawback is cost and inflexibility: you have to bury and power a cable in every field. That is a main reason this approach faded once satellite positioning arrived.

Sources: general knowledge of electromagnetic induction and feedback control (standard physics textbooks); Encyclopaedia Britannica, "automated guided vehicle"; for the agricultural example see the Farmers Weekly "Machinery milestones" article cited in site notes.

## Machine vision for weeding and spraying
Kind: reference
About: machine-vision-weeding, excess-green-index
A camera can steer a farm machine, or decide where to treat, because crops and soil look different. A plant is green; soil is brown or grey.

The first job is to find the plants. A simple, widely used method is the excess green index, introduced in 1995 by Woebbecke and colleagues. For each pixel, it takes the amounts of red, green and blue light and computes about twice the green minus the red and the blue. Plants score high and soil scores low, even as the light changes in brightness. A threshold then separates the two. Near-infrared cameras do the same job even better, because leaves reflect strongly in that band.

The second job is finding rows. Seeds are drilled in straight lines, so a computer can fit a line through the plant pixels, for example using a Hough transform. The line is the centre of the row, and the difference between that line and where the machine is pointing becomes the steering error. Steerage hoes made this way can run blades within a couple of centimetres of the crop.

The third is telling weeds from crop. Plants in a row are expected, those between rows are weeds, which is the easy case. Within the row it is harder. Older systems measured colour, leaf shape and size, and position. Since the 2010s, deep learning has taken over: neural networks trained on many labelled photographs recognise species directly, even when leaves overlap.

The result can drive a mechanical hoe, a laser or a precision sprayer that switches individual nozzles on only over weeds. Systems such as See & Spray, developed by Blue River Technology and bought by John Deere in 2017, report large cuts in herbicide use. The hard parts are changing light, shadow, dirt, wind and the huge variety of weeds.

Sources: Woebbecke, Meyer, Von Bargen and Mortensen (1995), Transactions of the ASAE; Slaughter, Giles and Downey (2008), "Autonomous robotic weed control systems: a review", Computers and Electronics in Agriculture; Wang, Zhang and Wei (2019), review of weed detection, Computers and Electronics in Agriculture; John Deere / Blue River press releases (2017).

## Satellite guidance: GPS, RTK and auto-steer
Kind: reference
About: gps-full-operation, rtk-auto-steer
The Global Positioning System (GPS) is a network of satellites run by the United States. Each satellite broadcasts its position and a precise time signal. A receiver measures how long signals from at least four satellites took to arrive and works out its own position. The system was declared fully operational in 1995. Until May 2000 the US deliberately blurred the civilian signal, called selective availability. Without that, an ordinary receiver is accurate to a few metres, which is not enough to drive between crop rows. GPS now sits alongside other systems (Russia's GLONASS, Europe's Galileo and China's BeiDou), so the general term is GNSS.

To get centimetre accuracy, farms use real-time kinematic positioning, or RTK. The receiver does not just use the coded signal. It also compares the phase of the carrier wave itself, which is far finer. The problem is that there are errors, such as delay through the atmosphere. A fixed base station at a known point, or a network of them, measures those errors and sends corrections to the tractor by radio or mobile data. The tractor's receiver then fixes its position to about 2 centimetres.

Auto-steer connects the receiver to the steering. The driver sets a straight or curved line (an "AB line") for the first pass, and the system steers each later pass parallel to it, leaving the driver to watch the implement and turn at the headland. Commercial systems arrived in the late 1990s and became common in the 2000s.

Centimetre-level repeatability allows controlled traffic farming, where all machines use the same permanent wheel lanes year after year. Soil in the lanes is compacted and the soil in the rest of the field stays loose, which can improve crop growth and drainage.

Sources: Encyclopaedia Britannica, "Global Positioning System"; GPS.gov (US government), history and selective availability; Ordnance Survey and AHDB pages on precision farming and RTK; AHDB and Soil Association material on controlled traffic farming (to confirm benefit claims before quoting figures).

## Autonomous farm robots today
Kind: reference
About: hands-free-hectare, farm-robots-today
In 2017 a team at Harper Adams University in Shropshire, working with the company Precision Decisions, ran the Hands Free Hectare project. The aim was to grow a crop of spring barley without a person setting foot in the field. They used small converted tractors and a converted combine harvester, along with drones to survey the crop. All the machines drove themselves using satellite positioning and the crop was harvested in 2017, which the team presented as a first. The project followed up with winter wheat and later "Hands Free Farm" work.

Other autonomous farm robots have followed. Small Robot Company in England developed lightweight field robots to monitor and treat individual plants. Saga Robotics (Norway) built Thorvald, a modular robot used in polytunnels, and Naïo Technologies (France) makes weeding robots. Major manufacturers also showed driverless tractors, such as John Deere's autonomous 8R shown in 2022.

Why bother? Small machines compact the soil less than a big tractor, they can work around the clock, and they remove the need for a driver in repetitive work. The difficulties are safety around people and animals, reliable navigation, cost, and the need for rules on driverless machines on public land. Most commercial robots still work in a limited setting, such as a single crop, a tunnel or a prepared field. Fully driverless general-purpose tractors remain uncommon.

Sources: Harper Adams University, Hands Free Hectare project pages and press releases; Lowenberg-DeBoer et al. (2020), "Economics of robots and automation in field crop production", Precision Agriculture; Fountas et al. (2020), "Agricultural robotics for field operations", Sensors; John Deere press release, CES 2022; company web pages (Small Robot Company, Saga Robotics, Naïo).

## Why grain is dried and how dryers work
Kind: reference
About: grain-drying-principles
Freshly cut grain is alive. If it is stored damp, the grain and the moulds and insects living on it respire, giving off heat and moisture, and the heap can warm up and rot. Some moulds also produce toxins. Drying removes water so that this cannot happen.

Moisture content is the share of a sample's weight that is water. In Britain, cereals are often combined at 16 to 20 per cent in a damp season. For storage, the AHDB advises roughly 15 per cent or below for a few months, and about 14 per cent or less for storage into the spring. Oilseed rape needs to be much drier, about 9 per cent, because its oil-rich seed is more prone to going off. Buyers also set limits for delivery.

All dryers work the same way. Air is blown through the grain, takes up moisture and carries it away. Warmer air holds more water, so heating it makes drying faster. The dryer designs differ in how the grain meets the air:
- On-floor and bin drying: grain sits in a deep heap or bin and air is forced up through it, from a floor of perforated ducts. It is slow, cheap, and can use only slightly warmed air.
- Batch dryers: a fixed amount is dried, then emptied.
- Continuous-flow dryers: grain trickles down through hot-air columns, while fans blow air across. They are used for large throughputs.

Too much heat damages the grain. Milling wheat, malting barley and seed corn all have lower temperature limits so that baking quality and germination are protected. Grain is also cooled afterwards. Drying uses a lot of fuel, which is why a dry harvest is welcome.

Sources: AHDB, Grain storage guide and Grain drying pages; Museum of English Rural Life, agricultural engineering collections; Encyclopaedia Britannica, "grain storage"; general knowledge for how the dryer types work (check the AHDB figures before quoting).

## Sprayers and spray drift
Kind: reference
About: sprayer-drift-principles
A boom sprayer is a tractor-mounted or self-propelled machine carrying a wide horizontal pipe, often 24 to 36 metres long, with a nozzle every half metre. It pumps a mix of water and pesticide or fertiliser out through the nozzles onto the crop.

Drift is spray that is carried away from its target by the wind. It matters because pesticides can harm neighbouring crops, hedges, ponds, wildlife and people. The main factors are droplet size, boom height, wind and travel speed.

Droplet size matters most. A fine mist of droplets under about 100 micrometres, a tenth of a millimetre, is light enough to be blown a long way and also evaporates quickly. Large droplets fall fast and land close to the nozzle, but may bounce off a waxy leaf. Nozzle makers classify sprays from "very fine" to "extremely coarse". The way to reduce drift is to use a coarser spray from low-drift nozzles. The best known are air-induction (venturi) nozzles, which mix air into the liquid to make big, air-filled droplets that burst on contact.

Boom height is the second factor. Spray from a boom that is high above the crop spends longer in the air, giving the wind more time to carry it off. So keep the boom low (commonly around 50 centimetres above the crop) and steady, which means using boom suspension to stop it swinging.

Operators also stop spraying if the wind is too strong, or too calm and warm (when air can rise), and leave unsprayed buffer strips beside watercourses. In Britain, a risk assessment scheme called LERAP sets buffer zones, and sprayers must pass regular inspection under the National Sprayer Testing Scheme. Modern machines use individual nozzle control and pulse-width modulation to change the flow without changing droplet size.

Sources: Health and Safety Executive and Voluntary Initiative pages on sprayers; Chemicals Regulation Division / HSE, LERAP guidance; National Sprayer Testing Scheme (NSTS) pages; Nuyttens et al. (2007), "Spray drift as affected by meteorological conditions", Biosystems Engineering; general knowledge for the approximate thresholds.
